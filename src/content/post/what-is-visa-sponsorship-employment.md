Visa sponsorship for employment means a U.S. employer files the immigration paperwork that lets you work for it legally. "We sponsor visas" can mean very different commitments, though: a one-time temporary work visa, a full green card process, or both. Understanding what sponsorship involves helps you ask the right questions and spot offers that are not what they seem.

## What does it mean to sponsor someone for a job?

In immigration terms, the employer is the **petitioner** and you are the **beneficiary**. The employer files a petition with USCIS, and sometimes first with the Department of Labor, showing that the job and you meet the requirements of a specific visa category. You cannot sponsor yourself for most employment categories; the exceptions, such as EB-1A and the national interest waiver, are self-petitions based on your own qualifications.

## Two types of sponsorship

### Temporary work visas

The most common is the H-1B for specialty occupations. Others include L-1 for intracompany transfers, O-1 for extraordinary ability, TN for Canadians and Mexicans, and E-3 for Australians. These give you permission to work for a set period, usually renewable, but do not make you a permanent resident.

### Green card sponsorship

Employment-based green cards usually follow three steps:

1. **PERM labor certification**, in which the employer tests the U.S. labor market and the Department of Labor certifies that no qualified U.S. worker is available at the required wage.
2. **Form I-140**, the immigrant petition.
3. **Adjustment of status or consular processing**, when your priority date is current.

Many employers sponsor a temporary visa first and start the green card later. Some never start it. Ask.

## How does sponsorship work in practice?

The employer hires an immigration attorney, gathers information about the job and the company, and collects your documents: degrees, transcripts, experience letters, passport, and immigration history. The attorney prepares filings and the employer signs them as the petitioner. Approval allows you to work for that employer in that role.

## Who pays?

Rules on who pays matter and are often misunderstood:

- **H-1B**: The employer must pay certain fees, including the ACWIA training fee and the fraud fee, and cannot deduct petition-related costs from your pay in a way that brings it below the required wage.
- **PERM**: Department of Labor rules require the employer to pay the costs of the labor certification, including attorney fees and recruitment. You cannot reimburse them.
- **I-140 and adjustment of status**: Rules are more flexible. Some employers pay everything; some ask employees to cover the green card application for themselves and family.

An employer asking you to pay for your own H-1B or PERM, directly or through deductions, is a red flag.

## Questions to ask before accepting a sponsored job

- Which visa will you file for me, and when?
- Are you subject to the H-1B lottery, or cap-exempt?
- Will you sponsor a green card? In which category, and when will you start?
- Who pays legal and government fees for me and my family?
- If I leave within a certain period, will you ask me to repay anything?
- Is the role full-time and at a fixed location, or at client sites?

## What sponsorship does not guarantee

Sponsorship is a commitment to file, not a guarantee of approval. A petition can be denied, the H-1B lottery can pass you over, and green card waits can be long for some nationalities. Plan for timelines in years, not months, if you are aiming for a green card.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can any company sponsor a visa?
Any U.S. employer can file, but it must meet the requirements of the category, such as paying the required wage for an H-1B or proving the ability to pay for a green card.

### Can I change jobs after being sponsored?
For temporary visas, a new employer usually files a new petition. For green cards, changing jobs before certain stages can restart the process.

### Does sponsorship mean the employer controls my status?
To a large extent, yes, for employer-based categories. Leaving the job can affect your status, so plan any move with counsel.

### Can a family member sponsor me for a job?
A relative's company can sponsor you, but the job must be real and the relationship must be disclosed, especially in PERM cases where family ties draw scrutiny.

### Is sponsorship different for remote jobs?
The work location matters for the H-1B and PERM. Remote roles need the correct location listed and the right wage for that area.

### What if an employer withdraws sponsorship?
For an H-1B, withdrawal can end your status after the grace period. For a green card, withdrawal of an approved I-140 after 180 days generally does not cancel your priority date.

### Do recruiters charge for sponsorship?
Legitimate employers do not ask workers to pay for H-1B or PERM costs. Treat fees requested from you with caution.
