The B-1 business visitor visa lets you come to the U.S. for short business activities: meetings, negotiations, conferences, and consultations with business associates. It does not let you work. That distinction sounds clear, but it is where most B-1 problems start, because many ordinary business trips drift into hands-on work that the visa does not allow.

## What a B-1 visitor can do

Typical permitted activities include:

- Meeting with clients, partners, suppliers, or colleagues
- Negotiating and signing contracts
- Attending conferences, trade shows, or training seminars
- Consulting with U.S. business associates
- Researching a market or scouting locations for a future business
- Settling an estate or handling a legal matter
- Installing, servicing, or repairing equipment purchased from a foreign company, when the sales contract requires it

You are generally paid by your employer abroad. A U.S. company can usually reimburse incidental travel expenses, but it should not put you on its payroll.

## What a B-1 visitor cannot do

The B-1 does not allow productive work in the U.S. labor market. Examples that cross the line:

- Filling a role at a U.S. company, even temporarily
- Writing code, doing design work, or delivering a project for a U.S. client while here
- Managing day-to-day operations of a U.S. office
- Performing services for which you receive U.S.-source pay
- Running a trade show booth that sells products directly to the public, in many cases

The question officers ask is whether you are doing work a U.S. worker would otherwise be hired to do. If yes, you likely need a work visa such as an H-1B, L-1, E, or O-1.

## Gray areas worth a second look

**Training.** Receiving training is usually fine. Delivering training to U.S. employees can be acceptable in narrow situations but often is not.

**Remote work while visiting.** Answering emails for your foreign employer on a short trip is usually tolerated. Spending weeks here doing your full foreign job remotely raises questions.

**B-1 in lieu of H-1B.** A narrow policy lets certain professionals who would qualify for an H-1B perform short, specific projects while paid abroad. It is applied cautiously and should be planned with counsel.

**Board meetings.** Foreign directors can usually attend U.S. board meetings and be compensated for that attendance.

## The visa interview and entry

Most first-time applicants apply on Form DS-160 and attend an interview at a U.S. consulate in their country of nationality or residence, which is now the general rule. Be ready to explain:

- The purpose and length of your trip
- Who pays you and where
- Your ties to your home country, such as employment, family, and property
- Why you will return

Bring an invitation letter from the U.S. company describing the specific activities, and a letter from your employer confirming your role and salary. Consistency matters. Officers notice when the letter says "meetings" but your answers describe a work project.

Citizens of Visa Waiver Program countries can often travel on ESTA for the same business purposes, subject to the same limits on work.

## What happens if you get it wrong

CBP officers can refuse entry, cancel the visa, and in serious cases find misrepresentation, which can bar future visas. A history of B-1 trips that look like disguised work can also hurt later applications for work visas or green cards.


## Frequently asked questions

### How long can I stay on a B-1?

CBP decides at entry, usually admitting business visitors for the time needed for the trip, often up to six months. Check your I-94 for the exact date. Extensions are possible on Form I-539 but should have a real business reason.

### Can I open a U.S. company on a B-1?

You can meet lawyers and bankers, sign leases, and set up a company. You cannot run its daily operations or work for it. If you plan to manage the business, look at the E-2, L-1, or another work visa.

### Can I be paid an honorarium?

Certain academic visitors can receive an honorarium for a short usual academic activity, subject to limits on how many institutions and how many days. Most business visitors should not receive U.S.-source pay.
