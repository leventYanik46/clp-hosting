You can sue the city for a pedestrian or bike crash, but only if you give written notice first, and the notice is due long before the lawsuit: 90 days in New York, six months in Washington, DC, and six months for Virginia cities and towns. Miss the notice and the claim against the government is usually gone, even though the claim against the driver survives. That is the single most important fact about these cases, and it is why CLP's [pedestrian and bicycle crash practice](/practice-area/key-services/pedestrian-and-bicycle-accidents) asks on the first call whether a road condition, signal, or public vehicle played any part in what happened.

## When the government shares responsibility

A driver is the obvious defendant, but the condition of the road often explains why the driver did not see you or could not stop. Public bodies can be liable for conditions they created or failed to fix, including:

- A crosswalk signal that gave a walk sign while turning traffic had a green arrow.
- A marked crosswalk with no lighting, faded paint, or a sight line blocked by overgrown vegetation the city was told about.
- A bike lane that ends abruptly into a merge, or a pothole or steel plate in the lane that threw the rider.
- A construction zone with no pedestrian detour or barriers.
- A city-owned vehicle: a bus, a garbage truck, a police cruiser, or a contractor's truck on a city job.

The last category is the simplest, because a public employee driving negligently is treated much like any other driver once the notice rules are met. The road-condition cases are harder, because governments are immune for some decisions and not others.

## Sovereign immunity: when you can sue the city and when you cannot

Every jurisdiction protects a government's discretionary planning decisions. Choosing not to put a signal at an intersection, or setting the speed limit, is generally a policy judgment courts will not second-guess. Failing to maintain what the government built is different. A signal that malfunctioned, a sidewalk that was left broken after complaints, or a crosswalk whose paint was never renewed is a maintenance failure, and maintenance claims are the ones that succeed.

Virginia layers more immunity on top. Counties in Virginia are broadly immune from negligence claims, while cities and towns can be sued for proprietary functions, and street maintenance is treated as one. Claims against the Commonwealth itself, including VDOT roads, run through the Virginia Tort Claims Act, which has its own notice requirement and a cap on damages. New York allows suits against municipalities but gives them qualified immunity for highway planning decisions made after a reasonable study, and New York City's prior written notice law bars most pothole and sidewalk defect claims unless the city had written notice of that specific defect before the crash. DC has a discretionary function exception that works much like Virginia's planning immunity.

## The notice deadlines that come before any lawsuit

These deadlines are jurisdictional in most cases. Courts cannot extend them for a good reason.

In DC, a claim against the District for unliquidated damages requires written notice to the Mayor within six months of the injury, stating the time, place, cause, and circumstances. A police report prepared in the regular course of duty within that period can satisfy the requirement, but relying on it is a gamble, and a separate letter is the safe course.

In Virginia, a negligence claim against a city or town requires written notice within six months, delivered to the city attorney, the mayor, or the chief executive. The Virginia Tort Claims Act requires notice to the Attorney General within one year for claims against the Commonwealth.

In New York, a notice of claim must be served within 90 days, the municipality may demand a sworn examination under General Municipal Law section 50-h, and the lawsuit itself must be filed within one year and 90 days of the crash rather than the usual three years.

Federal property has its own rules. Rock Creek Parkway in DC and the George Washington Memorial Parkway in Virginia are National Park Service roads, so a crash caused by their condition is a claim against the United States under the Federal Tort Claims Act, which requires an administrative claim on Standard Form 95 within two years before any suit.

## Evidence that wins a claim against a public body

A government defendant will argue it had no notice of the hazard. The response is the city's own records: 311 complaints, maintenance logs, work orders, signal timing plans, and prior crash reports at the same location, all obtainable through public records requests. Photograph the scene with measurements before the city repairs it, which often happens within days of a serious crash. A traffic engineering expert can compare the intersection to the design standards the city adopted. Our post on [who is at fault when a car hits a pedestrian](/who-is-at-fault-car-hits-pedestrian) covers the driver side of the same evidence.

## The driver's claim and the city's claim run together

In most cases you pursue both. The driver's insurer will blame the road, the city will blame the driver, and the plaintiff's job is to prove each contributed. Virginia's contributory negligence rule still applies to the pedestrian in both claims, and DC's special rule for pedestrians and cyclists applies only to the collision with the vehicle, not to the claim about the sidewalk.
