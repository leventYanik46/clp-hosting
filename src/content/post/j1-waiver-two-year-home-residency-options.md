The J-1 two-year home residency requirement stops some exchange visitors from changing to certain U.S. statuses, such as H-1B or a green card, until they have spent two years in their home country. Whether you are subject to it is written in your DS-2019 and visa, and the answer shapes your whole immigration plan. A J-1 waiver can lift the requirement, but only on specific grounds.

## Who is subject to the requirement

The requirement, often called 212(e), applies to J-1 and J-2 exchange visitors who meet any of these conditions:

- **Government funding.** The exchange program was financed in whole or in part by the U.S. government or the government of your home country.
- **Skills list.** Your field of study or work appears on the Exchange Visitor Skills List for your country, meaning your home country has said it needs people with those skills.
- **Graduate medical education.** You came to the United States for graduate medical education or training.

Check the notation on your visa, DS-2019 and I-94. If you are unsure, you can request an advisory opinion from the Department of State.

## What it blocks

Until you complete two years of physical presence in your home country or receive a waiver, you cannot change status to H-1B, L, or K, and you cannot adjust status to a green card. You can still work in J-1 or in some other statuses without those limits, depending on circumstances.

## The main waiver grounds

1. **No Objection Statement.** Your home government issues a statement that it has no objection to your not returning. Not all countries allow it, and for some it is not available to people whose training was government funded.
2. **Interested Government Agency (IGA).** A U.S. federal agency asks for your waiver because your work serves its interests, usually for research.
3. **Exceptional hardship.** Requiring you to return would cause exceptional hardship to a U.S. citizen or permanent resident spouse or child. This is a high bar, since ordinary difficulty does not count.
4. **Persecution.** You would face persecution in your home country based on race, religion, or political opinion.
5. **State Department of Health or Conrad 30.** Physicians who agree to work in a medically underserved area for a set period, typically three years, may receive a waiver request from a state health department. The number of requests each state can make is limited.

## How the process works

The application normally begins with a request to the Department of State's Waiver Review Division, which reviews the case and issues a recommendation. If the recommendation is favorable, USCIS makes the final decision through a separate filing. Timelines vary and the first stage can take months, so early planning matters.

## Why physicians need extra planning

Conrad 30 positions are in demand, with deadlines that vary by state. A job offer in an eligible area is only one piece. Physicians should talk to an attorney before they accept a position, since contract terms, employer obligations and what happens if the job ends can affect the waiver.

## When to hire a J-1 waiver attorney

A lawyer can confirm whether you are actually subject to the rule, choose the strongest ground, prepare the hardship or persecution evidence, and work with the employer on state-level programs. If you are considering an H-1B or a green card and your DS-2019 shows the requirement, speak to counsel early.
