A durable power of attorney and a health care directive answer two different questions. The first is who can pay your mortgage, manage your accounts, and sign your tax return if you cannot. The second is who can consent to surgery, choose a facility, or decline a ventilator on your behalf. People often assume one document covers both. It does not, and the gap only shows up when a hospital or a bank refuses to talk to the person you assumed was in charge. This post explains what each document does, how the forms differ in the firm's three jurisdictions, and what a court will do if you have neither.

## What a durable power of attorney does

A power of attorney names an agent (sometimes called an attorney-in-fact) to act for you in financial and legal matters. "Durable" means the authority continues after you lose capacity, which is the whole point of signing one for incapacity planning. The document can grant broad authority over banking, real estate, investments, insurance, taxes, business interests, and litigation, or it can be limited to one transaction.

Two drafting choices matter most. The first is whether the power takes effect immediately or "springs" into effect on a doctor's certification of incapacity. Springing powers feel safer, but they force your agent to prove incapacity to every bank, which delays the help you needed. We usually recommend an immediate power held by someone you trust. The second is which specific powers to include. Gifting, changing beneficiary designations, creating or funding trusts, and dealing with retirement accounts generally must be spelled out or the agent cannot do them. A form that is silent on these can block the very planning the family needs later, which is why we draft them as part of a complete [estate plan](/practice-area/estate-planning) rather than as a standalone form.

## What a health care directive does

The health care side names a different kind of agent and records your treatment wishes. The agent decides when you cannot: consenting to or refusing treatment, choosing providers, accessing medical records, and arranging care. The instructions portion, often called a living will, states what you want about life-prolonging treatment, artificial nutrition, and comfort care when recovery is not expected. Providers follow the agent within the limits the instructions set.

The forms carry different names by state. Virginia combines both functions in an advance medical directive under its Health Care Decisions Act. New York separates them: a health care proxy appoints the agent, and a living will records instructions. The District of Columbia uses a durable power of attorney for health care. Each requires witnesses, and each limits who may witness, typically excluding the agent and often excluding treating providers. A document signed in one state is generally honored in another, but hospitals are more comfortable with their own state's form, so a client who splits time between Herndon and New York should consider signing both.

## Power of attorney vs health care directive, side by side

- Subject matter: the financial power covers property and money; the health care directive covers medical treatment and personal care decisions.
- Agent: the two roles can go to the same person, but they do not have to. The best money manager in the family is not always the best person to sit with a physician.
- Effective date: financial powers can be immediate or springing; health care authority almost always begins only when a physician determines you cannot decide for yourself.
- Duration: both end at death. The executor named in your will takes over the estate, and the health care agent's authority ends, with limited exceptions for matters such as organ donation and disposition of remains where the form addresses them.
- Oversight: neither document involves a court, which is their advantage and their risk. Choose agents carefully and name an alternate in each.

## What happens if you sign neither

Without a durable power of attorney, no one has legal authority over your finances when you become incapacitated, not even a spouse for accounts in your sole name. The family's only route is a court petition. In Virginia that means asking the circuit court to appoint a conservator for your estate and a guardian for your person; in New York it is a guardianship proceeding under Article 81 of the Mental Hygiene Law; in DC it is a petition to the Superior Court's probate division. The process requires a filing, notice, often a court-appointed evaluator or counsel for you, a hearing, and ongoing reporting. It is public, slower, and more expensive than the documents it replaces. Our [conservatorship](/practice-area/key-services/conservatorship) and [legal guardianship](/practice-area/key-services/legal-guardianship) pages describe the proceedings when they cannot be avoided.

Without a health care directive, most states let close family make medical decisions under a default priority list, but the list may not match your wishes, siblings may disagree, and a hospital facing a dispute can refuse to act until a court resolves it. Unmarried partners typically sit far down the list or are not on it at all.

## Choosing and preparing your agents

Pick people who are organized, reachable, and willing to act against pressure from relatives. Tell them they are named, give them copies, and tell them where the originals are. Keep the documents fresh; institutions grow suspicious of a power of attorney signed fifteen years ago, and some will demand an updated one or an affidavit from the agent. Add a HIPAA authorization so the health care agent and other named people can get information before the directive technically takes effect. Then review both documents against the rest of the plan, using our [estate planning documents checklist](/estate-planning-documents-checklist), whenever the people you named change.
