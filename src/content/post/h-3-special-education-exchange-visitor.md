The H-3 special education exchange visitor program is a separate branch of the H-3 category for people coming to the United States to receive practical training and experience in the education of children with physical, mental, or emotional disabilities. It is limited to 18 months, it is capped at 50 approvals per fiscal year nationwide, and it has its own eligibility rules for both the facility and the participant. Because the cap is small and the fiscal year starts October 1, timing the Form I-129 filing matters as much as the content.

## What the petitioning facility must show

The petition is filed by a facility, not by an individual. The regulation requires a facility that has professionally trained staff and a structured program for providing education to children with disabilities, and for providing training and hands-on experience to participants in the exchange program. Schools for the deaf or blind, special education departments of public school districts, residential treatment programs, and university-affiliated clinics are typical petitioners.

The petition must include a description of the training program, the facility's professional staff, and the participant's role, including how much of the participant's time will be spent in direct instruction and how much in observation and supervised practice. The facility must also show that the participant's involvement in custodial care of the children will be incidental to the training. A program in which the visitor is mainly a classroom aide or a residential supervisor will fail on that point. Our [H-3 visa](/practice-area/key-services/h-3-visa) page explains how we document the facility's program in a way that separates instruction from care.

## Who qualifies as a participant

The participant must meet one of three profiles:

- Nearing completion of a bachelor's or higher degree in special education.
- Already holding a bachelor's or higher degree in special education.
- Having extensive prior experience in teaching children with physical, mental, or emotional disabilities.

Evidence for the first two is a transcript and a letter from the registrar or dean confirming expected completion, or the degree itself with a credential evaluation if it was earned abroad. The third profile needs employment letters describing the settings, the populations served, the years involved, and the duties. A general teaching background without a special education focus does not satisfy the experience route.

## The 18-month limit and the cap

An approved petition may cover up to 18 months. No extension beyond that is available, and after 18 months in this status the participant must spend six months outside the United States before being readmitted in H or L status or changing to it. Because the annual cap is 50, petitions filed late in the fiscal year may be approved with a start date in the next fiscal year or held until numbers are available. Facilities that run the program every year should file early in the fiscal year, and because the count is reached in some years, confirm availability with USCIS or counsel before relying on a start date.

## Pay, dependents, and status rules

Participants may be paid a stipend or salary, and the petition must identify the source. Spouses and children may hold H-4 status; they may study but may not work. The participant may change to a different status from inside the United States if eligible and if the change is filed before the H-3 period ends, though the category is not dual intent and does not lead directly to permanent residence. The practical next steps after any H-3 program are set out in our post on [what happens after H-3 training ends](/after-h-3-training-ends-options).

## How this differs from the standard H-3 trainee

Three differences matter. The standard trainee petition must prove the training is unavailable in the home country and will support a career abroad; the special education branch does not carry those two requirements in the same form, though the program must still be training rather than employment. The standard trainee may stay two years; the special education participant, 18 months. And the standard category has no numerical cap, while this one has 50.

What the two share is the rule against productive employment. The facility must not use the participant to fill a position that U.S. teachers or aides would otherwise hold. Petitions that look like staffing, especially in districts with shortages, draw Requests for Evidence. Facilities that need a working teacher rather than a trainee should look at the H-1B, which requires a bachelor's degree in a specialty and, for public schools, is often filed cap-exempt or through a qualifying affiliation; the existing overview of [which employers are H-1B cap-exempt](/h-1b-cap-exempt-employers) explains that point. The [J-1 visa](/practice-area/key-services/j-1-visa) teacher category is another route for working educators, through a designated sponsor.

## Preparing the petition

A complete filing includes the Form I-129 with the H supplement, the facility's program description and staff list, the participant's degree or experience evidence, a statement that custodial care is incidental, the remuneration details, and the current USCIS filing fee. Keep the program description concrete: the classrooms or units the participant will rotate through, the supervising teachers and their credentials, the weekly schedule, and the evaluation method. A training plan written to the standard described in our post on [how to write an H-3 training plan](/h-3-training-plan-requirements) will satisfy this branch as well.
