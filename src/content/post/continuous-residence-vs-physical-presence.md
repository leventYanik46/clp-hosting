Form N-400 asks two different questions about your time in the United States, and applicants fail them for different reasons. Continuous residence asks whether you kept your home here for the whole statutory period without a single trip long enough to break it. Physical presence asks how many days you were actually inside the country. You can pass one and fail the other: a resident who took one eleven-month trip may have plenty of days but a broken residence, while a consultant who flies out every other week may have an unbroken residence and too few days. This post explains how USCIS counts continuous residence and physical presence, which trips reset the clock, and how to plan travel before you file.

## Continuous residence and physical presence: two tests, two counts

Under the five-year rule you must have lived in the United States as a permanent resident continuously for five years before filing, and you must have been physically present for at least 30 months of those five years. Under the three-year rule for spouses of U.S. citizens, the numbers are three years and 18 months. Both rules add a third requirement: three months of residence in the state or USCIS district where you file. Continuous residence must also continue from the filing date until you take the oath, so a long trip after filing can undo an application that was fine when submitted.

Physical presence is pure arithmetic. USCIS adds up every day you were outside the country during the statutory period, using the trips you list on the form and the entry records CBP holds, and checks that the total is under half. Continuous residence is about whether any single absence was long enough to show you moved your home abroad. ## The six-month presumption

A single absence of more than six months but less than one year creates a presumption that you broke continuous residence. The presumption can be rebutted, and the officer will ask for evidence that your life stayed here: a job you kept or returned to, a home you kept paying for, immediate family who stayed, U.S. tax returns filed as a resident for that year, and proof that you did not take employment abroad. The weaker your ties, the more a nine-month trip looks like a move. Two trips of five months each do not trigger the presumption, but they may still cost you under the physical presence count and will be questioned at the interview.

## The one-year break and the restart date

An absence of one year or more breaks continuous residence automatically. There is no rebuttal. The practical consequence is a new waiting period: you become eligible again four years and one day after the date you returned, or two years and one day under the three-year rule. Holding a re-entry permit during the trip does not change this result. The permit protects your green card against an abandonment finding at the border, which is a separate question explained in our post on [getting a re-entry permit before a long trip](/re-entry-permit-before-long-trip), but it does nothing for the naturalization clock. Residents who confuse the two often file an N-400 that is denied on arithmetic alone, and our post on [what happens after an N-400 denial](/n-400-naturalization-denied-next-steps) explains why a hearing rarely fixes that.

## Form N-470 for qualifying work abroad

Congress created one exception. A permanent resident who will work abroad for the U.S. government, an American research institution recognized by the Attorney General, an American firm or its subsidiary engaged in developing U.S. foreign trade, a public international organization of which the United States is a member, or a religious denomination as a minister or missionary, may file Form N-470 to preserve continuous residence. You must have spent at least one uninterrupted year physically in the United States as a permanent resident before the qualifying work begins, and the application should be filed before you have been abroad for a year. An approved N-470 preserves continuous residence but, except for certain religious workers, it does not count the time abroad as physical presence. Someone with an N-470 who spends four of five years overseas still fails the 30-month test.

## Planning travel before you file

Start with a dated list of every trip during the statutory period, including short ones, and total the days. If you are under the limit but close, delay filing until the oldest long trips fall outside the five-year window; the window moves forward every day, so a resident who was abroad a great deal in year one can simply wait until year one drops off. Remember the 90-day early filing rule works in the other direction: you may file up to 90 days before the five-year anniversary, but the residence and presence requirements are measured through the filing date, so early filing shortens the period you have to show. Keep trips short between filing and the oath, disclose every absence at the interview, and file tax returns as a resident throughout.
