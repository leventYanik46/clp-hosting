A deadlock between 50/50 business owners is the most common governance failure in closely held companies, and the most avoidable. Two partners split ownership equally because it felt fair at the start, nobody wrote a tie-breaker into the operating agreement or shareholders' agreement, and now they disagree about a hire, a distribution, or whether to sell. Neither can outvote the other. The company cannot act, and the state default rules usually offer only one blunt remedy: dissolution. The way out depends on what the governing documents say, so the first step in any 50/50 deadlock is to read them. ## What deadlock does to the company

The immediate damage is operational. Decisions that require owner or board approval stop: the lease renewal does not get signed, the line of credit is not renewed, the key employee's raise is not approved and she leaves. Vendors and customers notice. The company's value, which is what both owners are ultimately fighting over, declines while they fight.

The legal damage follows. One owner starts acting unilaterally, arguing the business must go on; the other claims breach of the operating agreement or of fiduciary duties. Distributions stop or become a weapon. Each side hires counsel. At that point the dispute has moved from governance into litigation, where CLP's [commercial litigation and dispute resolution](/practice-area/key-services/commercial-litigation-dispute-resolution) team takes over, and the costs rise quickly.

## Deadlock provisions that should already be in the documents

A well-drafted operating agreement or shareholders' agreement for a 50/50 company contains an escalation sequence. The typical tools, roughly from least to most drastic:

- A cooling-off period and mandatory negotiation between the owners, with a deadline.
- Mandatory mediation with a neutral mediator, at the company's expense.
- A tie-breaking vote. Some companies appoint an independent third director or manager whose only job is to break ties, or give the casting vote to a trusted advisor on specific categories of decisions.
- A defined deadlock event that triggers a buy-sell mechanism. The most common is the shotgun clause (sometimes called a Texas shootout): one owner names a price per unit, and the other must either buy at that price or sell at that price. Variations include a sealed-bid auction between the owners and a put/call at an appraised value.
- As a last resort, an agreed procedure for selling the whole company to a third party or dissolving it in an orderly way.

Each mechanism has a cost. Shotgun clauses are fair only when both owners have similar access to capital; an owner who cannot fund a purchase will always be the seller. Tie-breaker directors work only if both owners trust the person and the person is willing to serve. Mediation works when the dispute is about business judgment and fails when it is about trust. Choosing the right sequence for a particular pair of owners is the drafting task, and our post on [when to update your bylaws or operating agreement](/when-to-update-bylaws-operating-agreement) explains why a 50/50 split with no mechanism should trigger an update immediately.

## What to do when the documents are silent

If there is no deadlock clause, the owners can still agree to one. A standalone deadlock agreement, negotiated with counsel while both sides are still speaking, can adopt any of the tools above for the current dispute. This is the cheapest path and the one most often skipped, because by the time owners seek advice they have stopped negotiating directly.

The next option is a negotiated buyout. One owner buys the other at a price set by appraisal, by formula, or by negotiation, with payment terms that reflect what the buyer can actually finance. A buyout agreement needs the same documentation as any purchase of an ownership interest: a purchase agreement, releases, a transition plan, and amendments to the governing documents. Our post on [the steps to sell a business](/steps-to-sell-a-business-loi-to-closing) describes the equivalent process with a third-party buyer, and selling the whole company to an outsider through a [structured sale process](/practice-area/key-services/mergers-acquisitions-business-transactions) is sometimes the cleanest answer when neither owner can or will buy the other.

## Judicial dissolution and other court remedies

When nothing else works, state law allows a court to intervene. Virginia, DC, and New York each have corporation statutes that permit a shareholder to petition for judicial dissolution when the directors or shareholders are deadlocked and the deadlock threatens irreparable injury to the company or the business can no longer be conducted to the shareholders' advantage. The LLC statutes have parallel provisions, generally allowing dissolution when it is no longer reasonably practicable to carry on the business in accordance with the operating agreement. In New York, a holder of fifty percent of the voting shares has a specific statutory route to petition for dissolution on deadlock grounds.

Courts have tools short of dissolution. Depending on the state and the entity, a court may appoint a custodian or receiver to run the company while the dispute is resolved, order a buyout of one owner by the other or by the company, or supervise a sale. In practice, the filing of a dissolution petition often prompts the settlement the owners could not reach on their own, because both now face the prospect of a court-ordered liquidation at distressed values.

Litigation is slow and public, and the standards differ across jurisdictions, so the remedy a court will actually grant depends on where the company is formed and the facts of the deadlock. Our post on [the legal aspects of running a business](/understanding-corporate-law-a-detailed-guide-legal-aspects-of-business) gives the framework; the application is a case-by-case question.

## Preventing the next one

Every deadlock resolution should end with amended governing documents. If the owners stay together, add the escalation sequence they wish they had. If one buys the other out, the survivor should fix the governance for the next partner or investor. And any founder forming a new company with one other person should treat the deadlock clause as a condition of signing, because the question is never whether two owners will disagree but what happens when they do.
