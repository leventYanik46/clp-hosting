The category code printed on your green card tells you, and any officer who looks at it, how you became a permanent resident. It is a short combination of letters and numbers, such as IR6, CR6, or E26. Most people never think about it, but the code can matter when you sponsor relatives, apply for citizenship, or deal with conditional residence.

## Where to find the code

On current green cards, the category appears on the front, labeled "Category." It is separate from your USCIS number (A-number) and your card number.

## How the codes are built

Most codes follow a pattern: letters for the type of case, and a number that often shows whether you got the green card abroad or inside the U.S.

- A low number, such as 1 through 5, usually means you entered as an immigrant through consular processing
- A higher number, such as 6 through 9, or a 0, usually means you adjusted status inside the U.S.

## Common family-based codes

- **IR1 / IR6:** Spouse of a U.S. citizen, married two years or more at the time of approval. IR1 abroad, IR6 adjusted in the U.S.
- **CR1 / CR6:** Spouse of a U.S. citizen, married less than two years. The "C" means conditional. You must file Form I-751 to remove conditions before the card expires.
- **IR2 / IR7:** Child of a U.S. citizen.
- **IR5 / IR0:** Parent of an adult U.S. citizen. IR0 means the parent adjusted status in the U.S.
- **F11 / F16, F21 / F26, F31 / F36, F41 / F46:** Family preference categories (F1 through F4), with the second digit showing consular or adjustment processing.
- **F2A codes:** Spouses and children of green card holders.

## Common employment-based codes

- **E11 / E16:** EB-1 extraordinary ability.
- **E21 / E26:** EB-2, including advanced degree professionals. E26 means the EB-2 green card was approved through adjustment in the U.S.
- **E27 and E28:** Typically used for the spouse and child of an EB-2 principal adjusting status.
- **E31 / E36:** EB-3 skilled workers.
- **EB-5 codes:** Investor categories, including conditional codes that require removing conditions later.

## Other categories

Refugee, asylee, diversity visa, and special immigrant categories each have their own codes. If your code is unfamiliar, USCIS and State Department resources list the classification symbols, or an attorney can confirm what it means.

## Why the category code matters

**Conditional residence.** A "C" code, such as CR6, means the card is valid for two years and you must remove conditions. Missing the I-751 deadline can end your status.

**Citizenship timing.** Spouses of U.S. citizens may be able to naturalize after three years instead of five. Your code helps show the basis for that.

**Sponsoring relatives.** Your green card category does not usually limit who you can sponsor, but being a permanent resident versus a citizen does.

**Errors.** If the code on your card is wrong, for example IR6 instead of CR6 or the reverse, it can cause problems later. Request a correction from USCIS on Form I-90 when the error was USCIS's.


## Frequently asked questions

### Does my category code ever change?

It stays the same on renewal cards unless USCIS corrects an error. Removing conditions changes the card type and code to the permanent equivalent.

### Do employers need to know my category?

No. For Form I-9, a valid green card is enough. Employers should not ask about the category or the basis for residence.

### Is a diversity visa code different?

Yes. Diversity visa green cards carry their own codes, typically beginning with DV, with the number showing consular or adjustment processing.

### What does a CR6 code mean for travel?

A conditional resident can travel like any permanent resident. If the card expires while you are abroad, bring the I-751 receipt notice, which extends status for a set period.
