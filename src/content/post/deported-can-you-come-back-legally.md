Many people ask: if you get deported, can you come back legally? The answer is often yes, but only after waiting a certain period or getting special permission. The length of the bar depends on how you were removed and your history.

## Reentry bars after removal

**Five years:**
- Removed through expedited removal or at a port of entry
- Removed after arriving and being placed in proceedings

**Ten years:**
- Removed after a removal order from an immigration judge
- Departed while an order of removal was outstanding

**Twenty years:**
- Removed a second time

**Permanent:**
- Removed after a conviction for an aggravated felony

## The unlawful presence bars

Separate from removal bars, unlawful presence bars apply:

- **Three-year bar:** more than 180 days but less than one year of unlawful presence, followed by departure
- **Ten-year bar:** one year or more of unlawful presence, followed by departure

## The permanent bar for reentry

The most serious bar applies to people who:

- Were removed and then reentered or tried to reenter without inspection, or
- Accumulated more than one year of unlawful presence, left, and then reentered or tried to reenter without inspection

This bar is permanent. After 10 years outside the U.S., the person can ask for permission to reapply, which is difficult to obtain.

## Form I-212: permission to reapply

Form I-212 asks for permission to reapply for admission before the bar ends. USCIS considers:

- Reasons for removal
- Time since removal
- Family ties in the U.S.
- Evidence of rehabilitation
- Hardship to family
- Moral character

## Waivers for other grounds

Even with an approved I-212, other inadmissibility grounds may require a waiver, such as Form I-601 for unlawful presence or criminal grounds.

## Paths to return

- **Family petitions:** a U.S. citizen spouse or parent can file an I-130
- **Employment petitions:** an employer can sponsor you
- **Visitor or other visas:** if eligible and the bar has passed or is waived

## Risks of reentering without permission

Reentering without permission after removal is a federal crime. It can lead to prosecution, imprisonment, and the permanent bar. Prior removal orders can be reinstated without a new hearing.

## Practical example

A man was ordered removed by an immigration judge in 2018 and deported. He later marries a U.S. citizen, who files an I-130 for him. He faces the 10-year bar from the removal order, and he may also face an unlawful presence bar from the time he spent in the U.S. before removal. To immigrate before 2028, he would need an approved Form I-212 and possibly a Form I-601 waiver for unlawful presence. If he had tried to reenter without permission after removal, the permanent bar would apply, and his options would be far narrower.

## Get your records first

Before filing anything, request your immigration records through FOIA so you know exactly what type of removal or departure is on file. Many people don't know whether they received expedited removal, a removal order, or voluntary departure, and the answer determines the bars that apply.

## Voluntary departure vs removal

People who left under voluntary departure, and complied with its terms, are generally not subject to removal bars, although unlawful presence bars may still apply. If voluntary departure was granted and the person didn't leave in time, they can face penalties and a 10-year bar to several forms of relief. Knowing which type of departure you had is essential.

## Returning on a visitor visa after removal

Even after a bar ends, a prior removal is a factor in any visa application. Consular officers will ask about your history, and you'll need to show you'll comply with visa terms. Expect more scrutiny, and bring documentation of your ties abroad.
