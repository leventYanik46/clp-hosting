The EB-3 skilled worker, professional, and other worker subcategories share almost everything: a permanent, full-time job offer from a U.S. employer, PERM labor certification in most cases, an I-140 petition, and a wait for a visa number under the Visa Bulletin. What separates them is one thing that surprises many applicants. The category is set by what the job requires, as written in the labor certification, not by the highest credential on your resume. A worker with a master's degree applying for a job that needs two years of experience is a skilled worker case. Here is how each subcategory works and why the choice matters for the wait.

## Skilled workers: at least two years of training or experience

A skilled worker fills a position whose minimum requirements are at least two years of training or experience. Relevant post-secondary education can count toward the training. The job must be permanent, not temporary or seasonal. Common examples include electricians, welders, chefs, technicians, and many healthcare support roles.

The worker must meet the job's requirements by the priority date, which is the date the PERM application was filed with the Department of Labor. Experience gained after that date does not count. Experience gained with the sponsoring employer usually cannot count either, unless the earlier job was substantially different from the one being offered. That rule catches many employees who were trained in-house.

## Professionals: a bachelor's degree the job requires

A professional holds a U.S. bachelor's degree or a foreign equivalent, and the job requires that degree. The degree must be a normal requirement for the occupation, not just something the employer prefers. Accountants, engineers, teachers, and IT staff often fall here when the employer does not need the higher EB-2 requirements.

In the professional subcategory, experience cannot substitute for the degree. A three-year foreign bachelor's degree or a combination of shorter credentials may not be treated as equivalent to a U.S. four-year degree, so foreign education often needs an expert evaluation before the PERM is filed. If the worker's education does not fit neatly, the skilled worker subcategory may be the safer path, provided the job's requirements are drafted to allow it.

Registered nurses and physical therapists usually file in this subcategory under Schedule A, a Department of Labor list of shortage occupations. Schedule A cases skip the PERM recruitment process, though the employer still completes Form ETA-9089 and files it with the I-140 at USCIS.

## Other workers: jobs requiring less than two years

The other worker subcategory covers permanent jobs that require less than two years of training or experience. Examples include some food processing, housekeeping, warehouse, and caregiving positions. The employer still has to complete PERM and prove no qualified U.S. workers are available.

The key difference is the wait. By statute, other workers share a separate, smaller annual limit, and the Visa Bulletin lists them in their own column. Their priority dates often move more slowly than those for skilled workers and professionals, so check the current Visa Bulletin before planning around this subcategory.

## Why the job description decides the category

The PERM application must state the job's actual minimum requirements. The employer cannot tailor them to fit one worker, and requirements that go beyond what is normal for the occupation need a documented business necessity. Those stated requirements then fix the category the I-140 can be filed in. Our walkthrough of the [PERM labor certification process](/perm-labor-certification-process) covers how the job description is set.

That has two practical effects. First, the employer and the worker should agree on the category before recruitment starts, because changing the requirements later means a new PERM. Second, a job written at the skilled worker level supports an EB-3 skilled worker petition even if the worker has a doctorate; the extra education does not move the case into EB-2.

## When a worker could fit more than one category

Skilled workers and professionals share a single column in the Visa Bulletin, so the choice between those two rarely affects the wait. It does affect the evidence: the professional route needs a qualifying degree, the skilled worker route needs two years of documented experience or training.

The bigger choice is often between EB-3 and EB-2. A job that requires a master's degree, or a bachelor's plus five years of progressive experience, can support EB-2, and EB-2 dates sometimes move faster.
