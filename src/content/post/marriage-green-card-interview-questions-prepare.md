A marriage green card interview is not a quiz you can pass by memorizing facts. USCIS wants to decide whether your marriage is genuine, and an officer reads that from how a couple talks about shared life, not from perfect answers. Honest, consistent answers and good documents matter more than rehearsed ones.

## What happens at the interview

You and your spouse attend together at a USCIS field office. The officer places you under oath, reviews your forms and documents, and asks questions. Many interviews are short and end with a decision or with a request for more evidence. Some officers question spouses separately, particularly when something in the file raises concerns. Do not read that as a sign of failure. It is a standard tool.

## Types of questions to expect

Officers usually start with identity and form questions, such as your name, address and whether the information in your application is still correct. Then they move to the relationship. Common topics include:

- **How you met,** and how the relationship developed
- **The proposal and the wedding,** who attended, where it took place
- **Daily routine,** such as who wakes first, who cooks, who handles bills
- **The home,** layout, furniture, which side of the bed each person uses
- **Family and friends,** who you have met and when
- **Work and finances,** where each spouse works, how bills and accounts are shared
- **Future plans,** children, travel, and where you hope to live

Questions can seem odd. They are meant to find out whether two people are really living as a couple. A natural answer, even if it differs slightly from your spouse's, is fine. Small differences in detail are normal. Large contradictions on basic facts are what draw follow-up.

## What to bring

- Passports and any government-issued IDs for both spouses
- The interview notice
- Originals of civil documents, including your marriage certificate, birth certificates, and divorce decrees from prior marriages
- Your green card application copy and any receipt notices
- Updated evidence of your life together: joint lease or mortgage, bank statements, insurance, bills, tax returns, photos, travel records, correspondence
- Evidence that has changed since filing, such as a new address or a child's birth certificate
- Translations for any document not in English, with a certification
- The sponsor's updated financial documents

Bring more than you think you need, but organize it so you can find items quickly.

## How to prepare honestly

1. **Review your own application.** Re-read the forms you signed. Answers at the interview should match them.
2. **Talk through your shared history.** This is not coaching each other. It is refreshing your memory of dates and details you may have forgotten.
3. **Tell the truth.** If you do not remember something, say so. Guessing leads to contradictions.
4. **Answer only the question asked.** Short, direct answers are fine.
5. **Bring an interpreter if you need one,** unless USCIS tells you otherwise, and check the rules first.

## What to avoid

- Memorizing a script
- Arguing with the officer
- Bringing documents you cannot explain
- Concealing past issues, since the officer may already know about them

## Should an attorney attend

An attorney can accompany you, and it is often a good idea when your case has complications, such as an age gap, a short courtship, prior immigration problems or a language barrier. A lawyer can also prepare you and review evidence beforehand. If your interview is coming up, a consultation can help you walk in organized and calm.
