Consular processing is how a relative who lives outside the United States becomes a permanent resident: the immigrant visa is issued at a U.S. embassy or consulate, and the green card arrives in the mail after the person enters. Compared with adjustment of status inside the country, it involves more agencies and more handoffs, and most of the delay families experience comes from a document that one of those agencies did not accept. This walkthrough follows a family case from petition approval to the port of entry. CLP's [family reunification and consular processing](/practice-area/key-services/family-reunification) team manages this stage for spouses, children, parents and siblings abroad.

## Step one: USCIS approves the petition and transfers it to the NVC

Consular processing for an immigrant visa begins when USCIS approves Form I-130 and sends the file to the National Visa Center, a Department of State office that holds every approved petition until a visa is available. The NVC assigns a case number and an invoice identification number; you will use both to log in to the Consular Electronic Application Center (CEAC) for the rest of the process. For immediate relatives (the spouse, unmarried child under 21, or parent of a U.S. citizen) the NVC starts processing right away. For every preference category the file waits until the Visa Bulletin's Dates for Filing chart reaches the priority date; our explainer on [priority dates versus filing dates](/priority-date-vs-filing-date) covers how to read that chart.

## Step two: fees, the DS-260 and the agent of record

Once the case is active, the NVC sends a welcome notice. If no attorney is on record, the petitioner or applicant files the Choice of Address and Agent (Form DS-261) so the NVC knows whom to write to. Then two fees are paid online: the immigrant visa application fee for each applicant, and the affidavit of support fee. Check the current State Department fee schedule; the amounts change.

After the fees post, each intending immigrant completes Form DS-260, the online immigrant visa application. It asks for every address since age 16, education and work history, prior U.S. travel, previous visa refusals, and the security and background questions. Answer precisely and consistently with the I-130 and with any earlier visa application; a mismatch is the most common reason an interview ends in a request for more information. Print the confirmation page for the interview.

## Step three: civil documents and police certificates

Each applicant uploads scans of civil documents to CEAC: birth certificate, marriage certificate, divorce or death certificates for any prior marriage, military records if any, and the passport biographic page. Applicants 16 and older also need police certificates: from their country of nationality and current residence if they lived there for six months or more after turning 16, from any other country where they lived for a year or more after turning 16, and from any country where they were arrested. The State Department's Reciprocity Schedule says, for each country, which documents exist, what they look like, and which are unavailable; follow it exactly. Every foreign-language document needs a certified English translation.

The NVC reviews the uploads and either marks the case documentarily complete or sends a checklist of rejected items. Blurry scans, cropped edges and missing translations account for most rejections, and each round trip costs weeks.

## Step four: Form I-864 and the domicile question

The petitioner submits Form I-864, the affidavit of support, with the most recent federal tax return or IRS transcript, W-2s or 1099s, and proof of current income at or above 125 percent of the Federal Poverty Guidelines for a household that includes the immigrants. If the petitioner's income falls short, a joint sponsor files a separate I-864, and a household member can add income on Form I-864A.

The I-864 also requires the sponsor to be domiciled in the United States. A petitioner who has been living abroad with the family must show either that the United States remained their domicile or that they will reestablish it no later than the immigrant's arrival: a job offer, a lease or home purchase, school enrollment for children, transferred accounts. The NVC cannot find the case complete without this, and consulates refuse visas over it. For how derivatives who come later handle the I-864, see our post on [how following to join works](/following-to-join-spouse-children).

## Step five: the medical exam, the interview and entry

When the case is documentarily complete and, for preference cases, the Final Action Date has been reached, the NVC schedules the interview and sends an appointment letter. Before the interview each applicant sees a panel physician approved by the embassy for the medical examination and vaccinations; the results go to the consulate, often in a sealed envelope the applicant must not open. Applicants bring original civil documents, the passport, photographs, and the DS-260 confirmation.

The consular officer decides at the interview. If a document is missing or a security check is pending, the officer issues a refusal under section 221(g) and tells the applicant what is needed; the case stays open until it is cured. If the officer finds a ground of inadmissibility, such as prior unlawful presence or a misrepresentation, the applicant may need Form I-601 and a qualifying relative's hardship before a visa can issue. Our post on [what to do when a visa application is denied](/my-visa-application-was-denied-what-should-i-do) covers these outcomes, and when a case stops moving for months with no explanation, an [immigration mandamus](/practice-area/key-services/immigration-mandamus) lawsuit can compel a decision.

An approved applicant receives the immigrant visa in the passport, typically valid for up to six months and tied to the medical exam date. Before traveling, pay the USCIS Immigrant Fee online so the green card can be produced. At the port of entry the officer admits the applicant as a permanent resident; the card follows by mail. A spouse married for less than two years at admission receives a two-year conditional card, explained on our [marriage-based green card](/practice-area/key-services/marriage-based-green-card) page.
