Hospitals facing nursing shortages often ask about the H-1B visa for nurses. The answer surprises many employers: most staff registered nurse positions don't qualify for an H-1B, because the job usually doesn't require a bachelor's degree. Some nursing roles do qualify, and for many foreign-trained nurses, an employer-sponsored green card is the more reliable route.

## Why most RN jobs don't qualify for an H-1B

The H-1B requires a specialty occupation, meaning a job that normally requires at least a bachelor's degree in a specific field. Registered nurses in the U.S. can be licensed with an associate degree or a diploma, so the general RN position doesn't meet that standard on its own.

USCIS guidance recognizes this. A petition for a general staff nurse role will typically be denied unless the employer can show that the specific position requires a bachelor's degree in nursing.

## Nursing roles that can qualify

Some nursing positions do require a bachelor's degree or higher and can support an H-1B petition:

- **Advanced practice registered nurses (APRNs),** such as nurse practitioners, clinical nurse specialists, certified nurse midwives, and nurse anesthetists, whose roles require graduate education
- **Nurse managers and administrators** in positions that require a BSN or higher
- **Specialty nursing roles** where the employer can document that a bachelor's degree is required, for example in certain critical care, oncology, or research nursing positions
- **Positions at hospitals with Magnet status** or similar designations, where the employer requires a BSN for all nurses in that role

The employer must show its actual hiring practice. If the hospital routinely hires associate-degree nurses for the same job, claiming a BSN requirement for one foreign nurse will not hold up.

## Licensing requirements

Every nurse must be licensed in the state where they will work. For H-1B petitions, the nurse generally needs a full, unrestricted license, or proof that the nurse is eligible for licensure and will obtain it promptly. Foreign-educated nurses usually need to pass the NCLEX-RN exam and obtain a credentials review.

For visa issuance, many foreign-educated nurses also need a certificate from an approved organization, such as a VisaScreen certificate, confirming their education, licensure, and English proficiency.

## Why a green card is often the better route

Registered nurses are listed on Schedule A, Group I, a Department of Labor list of occupations with a recognized shortage of U.S. workers. That designation means:

- **No PERM recruitment.** The employer can skip the labor market test and file the I-140 directly with USCIS, attaching an uncertified labor certification form and proof the nurse meets the licensing requirements.
- **EB-2 or EB-3.** Most nurses qualify for EB-3. Nurses with advanced degrees may qualify for EB-2.
- **Direct path to permanent residence,** without the H-1B lottery or the specialty occupation question.

The limitation is the visa backlog. EB-3 waits for nurses can be long, especially for nationals of some countries. Visa availability for nurses has fluctuated, so check the Visa Bulletin.

## Other options

- **TN visas for Canadian and Mexican nurses.** Registered nurses are on the USMCA list of professions, so Canadian and Mexican nurses can work on TN status without the bachelor's degree question.
- **H-1C** expired years ago and is no longer available.

## Planning a nursing recruitment program

For hospitals and healthcare employers, the right approach depends on the role and timeline:

1. For **staff RN positions,** start an EB-3 Schedule A green card process and plan for the wait.
2. For **APRN and management roles,** consider an H-1B, especially at cap-exempt nonprofit hospitals affiliated with universities.
3. For **Canadian and Mexican nurses,** consider TN status as a bridge.

## Timing a Schedule A case

Because Schedule A cases skip recruitment, an employer can move from offer to I-140 filing relatively quickly, once the prevailing wage is issued and the notice of filing has been posted at the worksite for the required period. The nurse's priority date is the date the I-140 is filed. From there, the timeline depends almost entirely on the Visa Bulletin. Nurses abroad usually consular process, while those already in the U.S. in another status may adjust status if a visa number is available and their status allows it.

## Questions hospitals should answer before recruiting abroad

- Will the role require a BSN, and do we hire only BSN nurses for it today?
- Which state license will the nurse need, and how long does licensure take for foreign-educated applicants?
- Who pays for credential reviews and the NCLEX, and how is that documented?
- Are we prepared to wait for visa availability, and how will we fill the position in the meantime?

Clear answers to these questions keep recruitment programs honest and keep petitions consistent with the hospital's actual practice.
