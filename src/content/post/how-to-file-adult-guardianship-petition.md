To file for guardianship of an adult, you need four things before the court will hear you: a written evaluation of the person's capacity, a petition that names the powers you want and explains why nothing less will work, proof that the person and their close relatives were notified, and a hearing at which you prove incapacity by clear and convincing evidence. The order of those steps and the court that handles them differ between Washington, DC, Virginia, and New York, but the structure is the same everywhere. Here is the process in the order it actually happens, with the places where petitions stall.

## Step 1: Get the capacity evidence first

The evaluation is the foundation of the case, so start there. Virginia requires an evaluation report from a licensed physician, psychologist, or other qualified professional who has examined the respondent, filed with the court before the hearing. New York relies on a court evaluator appointed after filing, but a petition with no medical support behind it is unlikely to be granted. DC appoints an examiner and a visitor who report to the court.

Ask the treating physician or a neuropsychologist for a written assessment that describes functional limitations in concrete terms: what the person cannot do, how often, and with what consequences. "Moderate dementia" tells the judge less than "cannot identify her medications, has left the stove on twice this month, and does not recognize that she has stopped paying rent." If the person refuses to be examined, say so in the petition and ask the court to order an evaluation. Our post on [when an adult needs a court-appointed guardian](/when-adult-needs-court-appointed-guardian) walks through the legal standard the evaluation has to meet.

## Step 2: Draft a petition that answers the court's questions

A guardianship petition is not a form you fill in and sign. The judge, the guardian ad litem, and the respondent's counsel will all read it as the roadmap for the case. It should cover:

- Who the respondent is, where they live, and who their spouse, adult children, parents, and siblings are, with addresses, because all of them are entitled to notice.
- The specific decisions the person can no longer make and the evidence for that.
- Which powers you are asking for, and whether you want a full or a limited guardianship.
- Any request for authority over finances. In Virginia and DC that is a separate [conservatorship appointment](/practice-area/key-services/conservatorship) that can be requested in the same petition; in New York it is the property side of an Article 81 guardianship.
- What alternatives exist and why they are not enough: an existing power of attorney, an advance directive, a representative payee.
- Why you are a suitable guardian, and any conflicts of interest, such as being a creditor or a beneficiary under the person's will.

A petition that is vague on the powers requested or silent on alternatives is the most common reason a case comes back from the court with questions. ## Step 3: File for guardianship in the right court and serve notice

Virginia petitions go to the circuit court for the city or county where the respondent lives or is located. In DC, the petition is filed in the Probate Division of the Superior Court. In New York, Article 81 petitions are filed in Supreme Court, and the case begins with an order to show cause that sets a hearing date on a short timetable.

The respondent must be served personally with the petition and a notice that explains their rights in plain language, including the right to be represented by counsel and to attend the hearing. Relatives on the statutory list get notice by mail. Skipping a sibling, or serving the respondent by leaving papers with a nursing home receptionist, is a defect that can force the whole hearing to be rescheduled. Keep proof of every service.

## Step 4: Cooperate with the guardian ad litem or court evaluator

After filing, the court appoints someone independent to look at the case. Virginia appoints a guardian ad litem who must visit the respondent, explain the proceeding, and file a written report with recommendations. New York appoints a court evaluator with a similar role and may also appoint counsel for the person. DC appoints counsel for the subject if they have none, plus a visitor and an examiner.

This person will interview you, the respondent, and often other family members, and will ask to see the evaluation and the financial picture. Treat the visit as the most important meeting in the case. Be candid about family disagreements; the evaluator will hear about them anyway, and a petitioner who seems to be hiding conflict loses credibility.

## Step 5: The hearing

At the hearing you present the evaluation, your own testimony, and any other witnesses. The respondent has the right to attend, to testify, and to contest the petition. In Virginia the respondent can demand a jury. The judge must find incapacity by clear and convincing evidence and must consider whether a limited guardianship or a less restrictive alternative would protect the person.

Uncontested cases with a solid evaluation and complete notice are usually brief. Contested cases, where the respondent objects or siblings disagree about who should serve, can involve discovery, competing medical opinions, and multiple hearing dates. The court's calendar and the availability of the evaluator drive the timeline far more than anything the petitioner does after filing. If the situation cannot wait, there is a separate track for [emergency and temporary guardianship](/emergency-temporary-guardianship-how-fast).

## Step 6: Qualify and start reporting

An order granting the petition is not the end. The guardian must qualify: take an oath, and, where finances are involved, post a bond before the clerk issues the certificate or letters that banks and hospitals will accept. New York requires new guardians to complete a training course. Then the reporting cycle begins. Virginia guardians file an annual report on the person's condition with the local department of social services. DC and New York guardians report to the court on the schedule the order sets, and New York requires an initial report within the first few months. Conservators and property guardians face a stricter regime, described in [what a conservator's annual accounting must show](/conservator-annual-accounting-requirements).
