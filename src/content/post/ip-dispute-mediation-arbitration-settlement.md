Yes, and most of them are. The overwhelming majority of trademark, copyright, and trade secret disputes end with a signed agreement rather than a verdict, and many end without a complaint ever being filed. The real question is which route gets you the most of what you want at the least cost. To resolve an IP dispute, the options run from a phone call between principals to a binding arbitration award, each trading off speed, confidentiality, enforceability, and finality differently. CLP's [IP dispute resolution practice](/practice-area/key-services/ip-litigation-and-dispute-resolution) starts every matter by mapping the client's objective against those routes before any filing.

## Direct negotiation and the agreements that end disputes

The simplest route is a negotiated agreement, usually following a demand letter or a response to one. Three forms recur.

A coexistence agreement resolves a trademark conflict by having each party agree to limits on its own use: different goods, territories, or trade dress, or a promise not to expand into the other's field. It lets both marks continue and is common where neither party can be sure of winning a likelihood-of-confusion fight.

A license converts an infringer into a paying customer. The accused party keeps operating, and the owner gets royalties and quality control. It is the usual outcome where the infringer has built a business the owner would rather tax than destroy.

A settlement agreement with a release closes the dispute on agreed terms: cessation of use by a date or after a phase-out, destruction of inventory, a payment, non-disparagement, and a release of claims. It should be specific about what conduct is permitted going forward, since vague settlements breed second disputes.

Our post on [what to do when you receive a cease and desist letter](/received-cease-and-desist-letter-next-steps) describes the negotiation from the recipient's side.

## Mediation

Mediation is a structured negotiation with a neutral who has no power to decide. The mediator meets each side, usually separately, tests its assessment of the case, and carries proposals back and forth. It is confidential, relatively fast, and inexpensive compared with litigation. Its value in IP disputes is that a mediator experienced in the field can tell each party, privately, how its position looks to someone neutral, which is often the first time either side has heard it.

Mediation can happen before a lawsuit, through private mediators or WIPO's mediation service, or during one, where many federal districts require it before trial. It fails when one party is not actually willing to settle, or when the dispute turns on a legal question that only a ruling can resolve, such as whether a registration is valid.

## Arbitration

Arbitration is a private trial before one or three arbitrators whose decision is binding and enforceable in court. Parties get there because a contract between them (a license, distribution, or employment agreement) contains an arbitration clause, or because they agree to arbitrate after the dispute arises.

The advantages are real: confidentiality, arbitrators with IP expertise, more control over schedule and procedure, limited discovery, and finality, since review of an award is narrow. The disadvantages follow from the same features. Limited discovery hurts a party that needs documents from the other side to prove its case. Narrow review means a wrong award usually stands. An arbitrator cannot bind third parties, cannot cancel a trademark registration (though the award can order a party to surrender one), and an arbitral injunction must be confirmed by a court before it can be enforced. It suits a licensor and licensee who want to keep working together; it is unavailable against a stranger counterfeiting your product.

## Administrative proceedings for specific disputes

Some disputes have a dedicated forum that is faster and cheaper than court but limited in what it can award.

The Trademark Trial and Appeal Board at the USPTO hears oppositions to pending applications and petitions to cancel registrations. It decides only whether a mark may be registered; it cannot award damages or enjoin use in the marketplace. It is the right venue when the real issue is the registration, and its findings on likelihood of confusion can carry weight in a later court case between the same parties. Our [trademark enforcement and TTAB proceedings](/practice-area/key-services/trademark-enforcement-and-ttab-proceedings) page covers these proceedings.

The Uniform Domain-Name Dispute-Resolution Policy, administered by providers approved by ICANN, resolves disputes over domain names registered in bad faith. The only remedies are transfer or cancellation, and the complainant must show the domain is confusingly similar to its mark, the registrant has no legitimate interest, and the domain was registered and is used in bad faith.

The Copyright Claims Board, a small-claims tribunal at the Copyright Office, hears lower-value copyright disputes with capped damages and streamlined procedure. Participation is voluntary: a respondent may opt out, leaving federal court as the claimant's only route.

Platform programs, including DMCA notices and marketplace complaint systems, remove infringing content without any proceeding at all, within the limits described in our post on [how a DMCA takedown works](/dmca-takedown-notice-process).

## When litigation is still the answer

Some disputes need a court. If serious harm is ongoing and the other side will not stop, only a court can issue an injunction enforceable by contempt. If the infringer is an anonymous counterfeiter, there is no one to negotiate with, and court discovery is needed to identify them. If the dispute turns on the validity of a registration or the scope of a right that will govern many future disputes, a ruling may be worth more than a settlement. And sometimes the other side has made clear that only a filed complaint will bring them to the table, at which point filing is the negotiation. ## Choosing how to resolve the IP dispute

The decision rests on four questions: what outcome you need (stopping the use, getting paid, keeping a registration, or preserving a relationship), how urgent it is, how confidential the dispute should stay, and whether the other party will honor a voluntary agreement. A client who needs the use stopped this month and does not trust the other side is in court. A client who wants royalties from a partner it will keep working with is in mediation or arbitration. Most clients are somewhere between, and the route can change as the dispute develops.
