A contract playbook is a written set of positions for each clause your company negotiates repeatedly: the preferred language, the fallbacks you will accept, the point at which you walk away, and who has authority to approve each step. Companies that sign the same type of agreement every week without one renegotiate from scratch each time, route every redline to the same overloaded lawyer, and concede different terms to different customers for no reason other than who asked. The playbook fixes all three. It is one of the first things our [contract review and executive counsel](/practice-area/key-services/contract-review-and-executive-counsel) team builds for clients with deal volume, and this post explains what goes in it and how to build one.

## What a contract playbook contains

The playbook sits next to your template agreement and covers each clause the other side regularly marks up. For each one, it records:

- The preferred position, meaning the language in your template and the business reason for it
- One or more fallback positions, with the language to use for each, in the order you would concede them
- The walk-away line, the point past which the deal is not worth doing
- Talking points: the explanation a salesperson or contract manager can give the counterparty for why you hold the position
- Who can approve each fallback, and who must be consulted before going past it

A good playbook also includes a short section on the counterparty's paper: which of their clauses to look for when you are forced to sign their form instead of yours, and the minimum changes to request. The structure follows the clauses that get negotiated most, which our post on [which clauses every business contract needs](/essential-business-contract-clauses) lists in order.

## Who the playbook is for

The playbook is written for the people who negotiate, not for lawyers. In most companies that means sales, account management, procurement, and operations. If a salesperson can read the entry on limitation of liability and respond to the customer's redline the same afternoon with pre-approved fallback language, the deal keeps moving and legal is involved only when the customer wants more than the fallback allows.

That design choice shapes the writing. Entries should be short, in plain language, and specific about what to say and what to send. A playbook that reads like a treatise will be ignored, and the team will go back to forwarding every redline to the lawyer. The approach to the negotiation itself is covered in [how to negotiate a business contract without losing the deal](/how-to-negotiate-business-contract); the playbook is what turns that approach into something a team can run.

## Building one from your last twenty deals

Start with the signed agreements from the past year or two. Compare each against your template and note every clause that changed. The clauses that changed in most deals are your negotiated set, and they are usually fewer than fifteen: liability cap, indemnity scope, payment terms, termination for convenience, auto-renewal, IP ownership, confidentiality duration, insurance, governing law, and a few that are specific to your industry.

For each, look at what you ended up accepting. Those concessions are the empirical fallbacks. Some you will decide you should not have given, and the playbook is where you draw the line going forward. Others you gave in every deal, which means your template is asking for more than the market will bear and should be revised to the position you actually hold. Then set the walk-away for each clause with the input of whoever carries the risk: finance for payment and cap, operations for service levels, the CEO for exclusivity.

Companies using a master agreement with statements of work should build the playbook for both documents, since SOW-level terms such as acceptance criteria and change orders are negotiated as often as the legal terms. The relationship between those two documents is covered in [how an MSA and a statement of work fit together](/msa-vs-statement-of-work).

## Approval thresholds and escalation

The playbook only speeds things up if it says clearly who can agree to what. A typical structure has three tiers. The first fallback on each clause can be accepted by the negotiator without approval. The second requires a manager or the head of the function. Anything beyond the last fallback requires legal and, for certain clauses, the CEO or board. Overlay a deal-value threshold, so that a small contract can move through tiers quickly and a large one gets more review.

Record every deviation. A short deviation log noting which customer received which fallback, why, and who approved it, does two things. It tells you when a fallback has become the real position and the template should change. And it protects the company when a customer later asks for the same terms another customer received, or when a most-favored-customer clause makes that comparison legally relevant. CLP's [risk management and legal compliance](/practice-area/key-services/risk-management-and-legal-compliance) team uses the log to track how the company's aggregate contractual exposure is shifting across its customer base.

## Keeping the playbook current

A playbook decays. Insurance renewals change the cap you can afford. A new product line introduces data or IP issues the old entries do not address. State law shifts on noncompetes, privacy, or e-signatures. Review the playbook on a fixed schedule, at least annually, and whenever the template is revised. Contract lifecycle management software can enforce the approval tiers and surface the deviation log, but the positions in it still have to be set by people who understand the business and the law. ## Where playbooks go wrong

The common failures are predictable. The playbook is too long and nobody reads it. The negotiator opens with the fallback instead of the preferred position, so the fallback becomes the starting point and the next concession is below it. The playbook is applied to the counterparty's paper as if it were your template, missing clauses your form does not contain. Deviations are approved verbally and never logged, so no one knows what was given to whom. Each of these is a process problem rather than a legal one, and each is avoided by keeping the document short, training the team on how to use it, and reviewing the log quarterly.
