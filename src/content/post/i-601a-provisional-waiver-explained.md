Leaving the United States after more than 180 days of unlawful presence triggers a three-year bar on returning. Leaving after a year or more triggers a ten-year bar. For a spouse or parent who entered without inspection and therefore cannot adjust status here, that is the trap: the only way to finish the green card is a consular interview abroad, and walking out the door starts the bar. The I-601A provisional waiver exists to solve exactly that problem. It lets USCIS decide the unlawful presence waiver while the applicant is still in the United States, so the family knows the answer before anyone boards a plane.

## What the I-601A provisional waiver covers, and what it does not

The provisional waiver forgives one thing: unlawful presence under section 212(a)(9)(B) of the Immigration and Nationality Act. It does not forgive misrepresentation, criminal grounds, a prior removal order or the permanent bar that applies to people who re-entered without inspection after a year of unlawful presence or after being removed. If any of those issues is in the file, the I-601A is the wrong form or, at best, only part of the plan.

That is why our [family-based immigration practice](/practice-area/key-services/family-based-immigration) starts every waiver case with a full history of entries, exits and encounters with immigration authorities. A ground of inadmissibility that surfaces at the consulate after the I-601A is approved revokes the provisional waiver automatically. The applicant is then outside the United States, subject to the bar, and must start over with the regular Form I-601.

## Who can file

The requirements are narrower than most families expect. As of this writing, an applicant must:

- Be physically present in the United States and at least 17 years old.
- Be the beneficiary of an approved immigrant visa petition, usually a Form I-130 filed by a relative, with an immigrant visa case pending at the Department of State and the immigrant visa fee paid.
- Have a U.S. citizen or lawful permanent resident spouse or parent who would suffer extreme hardship if the waiver were denied.
- Not have a pending Form I-485, and not be subject to a final order of removal unless that problem has been addressed separately.

Notice who is missing from the qualifying relative list: children. A U.S. citizen son or daughter can file the I-130 for a parent, but the hardship that counts must fall on the applicant's own spouse or parent. We explain what that means for parent cases in [green cards for parents who entered without inspection](/parent-entered-without-inspection-green-card).

## What extreme hardship actually means

Extreme hardship is more than the sadness and expense of separation, which USCIS treats as the normal consequence of inadmissibility. The officer looks at two scenarios: the qualifying relative stays in the United States without the applicant, and the qualifying relative relocates abroad with the applicant. The waiver is strongest when both scenarios are documented and both are severe.

The factors USCIS weighs fall into predictable groups: the relative's medical conditions and who provides care, the financial consequences of losing the applicant's income or of relocating, the relative's own ties to the United States such as other family members, a job, a mortgage or a child's schooling, the relative's ability to function in the applicant's home country given language, safety and employment prospects, and country conditions. No single factor is required. A spouse with a chronic illness, a mortgage, two children in school and no ties to the applicant's country presents a stronger case than a spouse with any one of those facts alone.

The evidence should match the claims. Medical records, not a letter saying someone is sick. Tax returns and a household budget, not a statement that money is tight. A psychological evaluation where anxiety or depression is part of the hardship. Country-condition reports from recognized sources. A detailed declaration from the qualifying relative that ties the documents together, in that person's own voice.

## The sequence of filings

The waiver sits in the middle of a consular case, and the order matters.

1. The relative files Form I-130 with USCIS. For spouses, that is the same [marriage-based green card](/practice-area/key-services/marriage-based-green-card) petition any couple files; for parents, the adult citizen child files it. Our post on [what Form I-130 is](/what-is-form-i-130-2025-update) covers the petition itself.
2. After approval, the case moves to the National Visa Center. The applicant pays the immigrant visa fee, which is the trigger that makes the I-601A possible. Tell the NVC that a provisional waiver is being filed so the interview is not scheduled until the waiver is decided.
3. The applicant files Form I-601A with USCIS, attends biometrics, and waits. Check current USCIS processing times for the form; they change.
4. If the waiver is approved, the NVC schedules the interview and the applicant departs for the consulate. The approved waiver takes effect on departure.
5. The consular officer confirms admissibility and issues the immigrant visa. The applicant returns as a permanent resident.

## Where I-601A cases fail

Three patterns account for most of the denials and revocations we see.

First, an undisclosed problem. A prior removal order, a false claim to citizenship, a border apprehension with a voluntary return: each changes the analysis and some cannot be waived at all. These must be found before filing, not at the consulate.

Second, thin hardship. A waiver package that lists the family's circumstances without documenting them reads as separation alone. The declaration has to explain why this family, specifically, cannot absorb the loss.

Third, a hardship case built on the wrong person. Hardship to the applicant, to U.S. citizen children or to siblings is relevant only insofar as it causes hardship to the qualifying spouse or parent. The package has to make that link explicit.

A denial of the I-601A cannot be appealed, though a new waiver can be filed with new evidence. A spouse who was admitted on a visa and overstayed usually does not need this waiver at all; see [marriage green card after a visa overstay](/marriage-green-card-after-visa-overstay) for why adjustment of status, not a waiver, is the answer in that situation.
