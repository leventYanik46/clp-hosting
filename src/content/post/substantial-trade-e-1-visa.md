Substantial trade for an E-1 visa has no dollar threshold. That is the first thing to understand, and it cuts both ways. A modest company can qualify if it trades steadily, and a company with one large contract can fail because one transaction is not a flow. Consular officers ask whether there is a continuous stream of international trade with numerous transactions over time, and whether more than half of the company's international trade runs between the United States and the treaty country. Our [E-1 treaty trader visa services](/practice-area/key-services/e-1-visa) page covers the rest of the case; this post explains how each test is applied in practice and what records answer it.

## No minimum, but a continuous flow

State Department guidance describes substantial trade as a continuous flow of sizable international trade items, involving numerous transactions over time. Three ideas sit inside that phrase.

The trade must be continuous. A burst of shipments in the month before the interview looks like it was arranged for the visa. Officers prefer a history that shows the same pattern quarter after quarter.

It must be sizable, which officers judge against the type of business. A specialty food importer and a machinery distributor will not show the same numbers, and the officer knows that.

And it must involve numerous transactions. Guidance says greater weight goes to more numerous exchanges of larger value, but where two companies trade the same total, the one with many transactions usually presents better than the one with a handful. For smaller businesses, officers also ask whether the income from the trade is enough to support the trader and family.

None of this can be judged until the trade exists. The E-1 is not available for a business that plans to start trading. Our overview of the [E-1 treaty trader visa requirements](/e-1-treaty-trader-visa-requirements) covers that existing-trade rule along with nationality and ownership.

## The principal trade test: more than 50 percent

The second test is about direction. More than 50 percent of the trader's international trade must be between the United States and the treaty country.

The denominator is international trade only. Domestic sales inside the United States are not counted at all. That means a company can sell imported goods to U.S. customers all day, run a large local service business on the side, and still meet the test, as long as most of its cross-border trade is with the treaty country.

A worked example helps. A U.S. company owned by Spanish nationals imports olive oil and ceramics. Over the past year, its imports from Spain made up most of its import volume, a smaller share came from Italy, and a small export line went to Mexico. Add up all three international streams. If the Spain stream is more than half of that total, the company passes. If the Italian supplier grew to rival the Spanish one, the company could fail even though its business is thriving.

Services follow the same logic. A U.S. subsidiary of a German engineering firm that bills for design work exchanged with Germany, and occasionally with Austrian clients, measures the German share of its cross-border service revenue. We usually prepare a country-by-country summary of the trade so the officer does not have to do this math at the window.

## Records that prove substantial trade

The officer cannot see your trade. You have to show it, and the best evidence is the ordinary paper of international business:

- commercial invoices and purchase orders, organized by date and country
- bills of lading, air waybills, and U.S. customs entry documents
- wire confirmations and bank statements showing payment for each shipment or service
- master contracts, distribution agreements, or service agreements
- a ledger summary prepared by your accountant that totals trade by country and period

Bring samples, not everything. A well-organized selection with a clear summary does more than a box of unindexed invoices. Make sure the summary ties to the underlying documents; officers sometimes spot-check one line.

## Problems that sink E-1 trade cases

The most common problem is trade that is too new. A company that began importing a few months ago may have real transactions but not yet a pattern. Waiting a little longer, or filing for a different status in the meantime, can be wiser than filing early and being refused.

Another is routing. If goods from the treaty country reach the United States through a distributor in a third country, the officer may decide the trade is between the U.S. company and that third-country distributor. Contracts and shipping documents should show the treaty country as the counterparty where that is the reality.

Ownership can also undo a strong trade record. If treaty nationals hold less than half the company, or some owners have become U.S. citizens or green card holders, the company may lose its treaty nationality. Check the cap table before you check the invoices.

Finally, remember that renewals test the trade again. If the treaty-country share drops below half, or volume falls off, a renewal can fail even though the original visa was approved. Treat the trade summary as something you maintain every year, not something you prepare once.
