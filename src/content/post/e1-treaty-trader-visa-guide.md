The E-1 treaty trader visa lets a business from a treaty country send its owners and key employees to the U.S. to run trade between the two countries. The test that decides most cases is simple to state and hard to prove: more than half of the company's international trade has to be between the U.S. and the treaty country. If an E-1 visa lawyer is reviewing your file, that is the first number they will ask for.

Many importers and exporters never consider the E-1 because the E-2 investor visa gets most of the attention. That can be a mistake. If your business moves a steady volume of goods or services across the border but you have not made a large U.S. investment, the E-1 may fit better.

## Who qualifies for the E-1 visa

Three things have to line up.

**Treaty nationality.** The company must be at least 50 percent owned by nationals of a country that has a treaty of commerce and navigation with the U.S. that covers E-1 status. Not every E-2 treaty country is an E-1 treaty country, so check the State Department's treaty list for your country specifically.

**Substantial trade.** The trade has to be substantial, which consulates read as a continuous flow of numerous transactions over time. One large contract usually does not qualify. A pattern of regular shipments or service engagements does. There is no fixed dollar minimum, but smaller businesses need to show that the trade is enough to support the trader and family.

**Principal trade with the U.S.** Over 50 percent of the company's total international trade must be between the U.S. and the treaty country. Domestic sales inside either country do not count toward this ratio; only cross-border trade does.

## What counts as trade

Trade is broader than physical goods. It can include:

- Goods such as consumer products, raw materials, or equipment
- Services such as consulting, engineering, logistics, insurance, or tourism
- Technology transfers and licensing
- International banking and transportation

The common thread is an exchange between the U.S. and the treaty country with a traceable record: invoices, bills of lading, wire confirmations, contracts, and customs entries.

## E-1 vs. E-2: the core difference

The E-2 is about money you have put at risk in a U.S. business. The E-1 is about trade you already conduct. Both give renewable status, both allow employees of the same nationality to qualify, and both let spouses work. The practical differences:

- An E-2 applicant must show a substantial investment that is committed and at risk. An E-1 applicant does not need a U.S. investment at all.
- An E-1 company must prove the 50 percent trade ratio. An E-2 company does not care where its trade goes.
- E-1 evidence is mostly transactional records. E-2 evidence centers on source of funds, a business plan, and proof of spending.

Some companies qualify for both. In that case the choice often comes down to which story is easier to document and which will hold up at renewal.

## Employees on an E-1

Owners are not the only people who can use the E-1. An employee can qualify if they share the company's treaty nationality and will work in an executive or supervisory role, or bring skills essential to the U.S. operation. Line staff who are easy to replace with U.S. workers generally do not qualify.

## How the process works

Most E-1 applicants apply at a U.S. consulate abroad. Many consulates first register the company, then process individual employees against that registration. The package usually includes Form DS-160, the consulate's E-1 forms (DS-156E), a detailed cover letter, and evidence of trade volume and direction. If you are already in the U.S. in another status, a change of status through USCIS on Form I-129 is possible, though it does not give you a visa stamp for travel.

E-1 status is granted in periods that can be renewed as long as the trade continues. There is no cap on total time, but E status is not a green card and does not lead to one on its own.

## Common mistakes

- Counting domestic sales in the 50 percent calculation
- Submitting a handful of large invoices instead of evidence of steady, repeated trade
- Assuming that a country's E-2 treaty also covers E-1
- Letting trade drop between renewals, which can sink the next application


## Frequently asked questions

### Can a small business qualify for an E-1?

Yes. There is no minimum dollar amount. Officers look at volume and frequency of transactions and whether the income supports the trader. A small importer with steady monthly shipments can be a stronger case than a larger company with a few irregular deals.

### Does the E-1 company need a U.S. office?

The trade must be carried on from a U.S. presence, usually an office or warehouse. A mailing address alone rarely persuades an officer that the trade is run from the U.S.
