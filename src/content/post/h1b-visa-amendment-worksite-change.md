An H-1B petition approves a specific job at a specific place. When the job changes in a material way, the employer usually has to file an H-1B visa amendment before the change takes effect. The most common trigger is a move to a worksite outside the area covered by the original Labor Condition Application (LCA), and that now includes many remote work arrangements.

## What counts as a material change

USCIS treats a change as material when it could affect whether the worker still qualifies or whether the original LCA still protects U.S. wages. In practice, these situations usually require an amended petition:

- **A new worksite outside the original metropolitan statistical area (MSA).** This is the rule from the 2015 *Matter of Simeio Solutions* decision, and it applies whether the new site is a client office, a new company branch, or your home.
- **Significant changes to duties.** Moving from a software developer role to a sales or general management role, for example, may change the occupation code and the wage level.
- **A change from part-time to full-time, or the reverse.**
- **Corporate changes that alter the employer's identity,** unless the new entity qualifies as a successor in interest and assumes the H-1B obligations.

## When you don't need an H-1B amendment

Not every change requires a new petition:

- **A move within the same MSA.** The employer must post the existing LCA notice at the new location for 10 business days, but no amended petition is required.
- **Short-term placements.** An H-1B worker can generally work at a site outside the MSA for up to 30 days in a year (up to 60 days in some cases) without a new LCA, as long as the employer pays travel costs.
- **Non-speculative, incidental travel,** such as attending a conference or a short client meeting.
- **Raises and promotions within the same occupation** that don't change the basic nature of the role.

## Remote work and the H-1B amendment rule

Remote work caused more amendment questions than anything else in recent years. Your home counts as a worksite. If your home is in the same MSA as the office listed on your petition, a posting at home (often electronic) usually satisfies the rules. If you moved to another state or a different metro area and now work from there full time, the employer generally needs a new LCA for that location and an amended petition, filed before you start working from the new place.

Employers sometimes assume a remote arrangement is informal. USCIS and the Department of Labor do not see it that way. A site visit or an extension filing that lists an address the petition never covered can lead to questions about whether you maintained status.

## The amendment process and timing

The employer files a new Form I-129 marked as an amended petition, with a certified LCA for the new worksite. Regular processing can take months. Premium processing is available for amendments and guarantees action within 15 business days. As of March 1, 2026, the premium processing fee for most I-129 petitions is $2,965.

You can start working at the new location once the amended petition is properly filed. You do not have to wait for approval, but if the amendment is later denied you would need to return to the original terms or another lawful option.

A practical tip: if you are also due for an extension soon, the employer can file the extension and the amendment together. That saves a filing fee and keeps the record consistent.

## What happens if the amendment is skipped

An unfiled amendment is a status problem for the worker and a compliance problem for the employer. For the worker, it can surface during an extension, a transfer, a visa stamping interview, or a green card case, when the officer compares the work history to the approved petition. For the employer, the Department of Labor can treat work at an uncovered location as an LCA violation, with back wages and penalties.

The fix is usually to file the amendment as soon as the gap is discovered and explain the circumstances. USCIS has discretion to excuse a short lapse that was not the worker's fault, but that discretion is easier to get when the problem is corrected quickly rather than discovered by the agency.

## A short checklist before any H-1B change

Before you move, change roles, or start a remote arrangement, ask your employer these questions:

1. Is the new location in the same MSA as the current LCA?
2. Will my job title, duties, or occupation code change?
3. Will my hours change between part-time and full-time?
4. Is my company merging, being acquired, or changing its legal entity?

If the answer to any of them is yes or "not sure," the change needs a legal review before it happens, not after.
