Most green card holders must live in the U.S. to keep their status. A permanent resident in commuter status is an exception. Commuter status lets green card holders live in Canada or Mexico while commuting to work in the U.S. It has specific limits, especially for citizenship.

## What commuter status is

Commuter status applies to lawful permanent residents who reside in Canada or Mexico and commute to work in the U.S. Their green card is marked with a commuter designation. There are two types:

- **Daily commuters:** cross the border regularly for work
- **Seasonal workers:** work in the U.S. for part of the year, often in agriculture, and return home the rest of the year

## Who can use commuter status

You can use commuter status if you:

1. Are a lawful permanent resident
2. Live in Canada or Mexico
3. Work in the U.S. in lawful employment
4. Commute to U.S. employment on a regular basis

Commuters must report to U.S. Customs and Border Protection as needed and keep their documents current.

## Limits of commuter status

**Employment requirement.** You can lose commuter status if you're unemployed in the U.S. for more than six months. The status depends on maintaining U.S. employment.

**Naturalization.** Time spent as a commuter doesn't count toward the continuous residence required for citizenship. To naturalize, you must take up residence in the U.S. and meet the residence and physical presence requirements from that point.

**Strikes and labor disputes.** Commuter status may be restricted if you're working for an employer involved in certain labor disputes.

## Converting to regular resident status

If you move to the U.S., you can remove the commuter designation by filing Form I-90 to replace your card. You'll need to show you've taken up residence in the U.S.

## Abandonment risks

If you stop working in the U.S. or stop commuting, you could be found to have abandoned your permanent residence. Protect your status by:

- Keeping consistent U.S. employment
- Maintaining records of your commute and work
- Converting to regular resident status if you move to the U.S.

## Commuter status vs other options

- **Regular green card holder:** must live in the U.S.
- **TN visa:** for Canadian and Mexican professionals who want to work in the U.S. temporarily
- **Border crossing card:** for Mexican visitors, not for work

## Planning for citizenship

If citizenship is your goal, plan to move to the U.S. Your continuous residence clock starts when you take up residence. Gather evidence of your U.S. home, such as a lease or mortgage and utility bills.

## Practical example

A green card holder lives in Windsor, Ontario, and crosses into Detroit each workday as a nurse. That's a classic daily commuter case. If the hospital lays her off and she can't find U.S. work for more than six months, her commuter status is at risk, and with it her ability to use the green card to enter. If instead she moves to Michigan, she should file Form I-90 to update her card and keep records of her new residence, because her naturalization residence period will start from the move, not from the date she first got her green card.

## Seasonal commuters

Seasonal workers who come for part of the year face the same basic rule: they need to keep returning to qualifying U.S. employment. Long periods without U.S. work, or a decision to stop coming, can lead CBP to find the person no longer qualifies for commuter status.

## Commuter status and family members

A commuter's spouse and children who also hold green cards and live in Canada or Mexico must each qualify for commuter status on their own, usually through their own U.S. employment. A family member who doesn't work in the U.S. and lives abroad may have trouble keeping their green card. Families in this situation should plan together, because one person's commuter status doesn't protect the others.

## Taxes and commuter status

Commuters often have tax obligations in both countries. U.S. tax residency rules for green card holders can apply even if you live in Canada or Mexico. Speak with a tax professional who understands cross-border situations, since tax filings also become part of your immigration record when you apply for naturalization.
