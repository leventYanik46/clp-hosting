Mediation and arbitration are both alternatives to a lawsuit, but they work in opposite ways. In mediation, a neutral helps the parties negotiate, and nothing is decided unless both sides agree. In arbitration, a private arbitrator or panel hears evidence and issues a binding award that courts will enforce and rarely overturn. So choosing between mediation vs arbitration for a business dispute is really a choice about control: mediation keeps the outcome in your hands, while arbitration gives it to a decision-maker whose ruling you cannot easily appeal.

Often you do not get to choose from scratch, because your contract already requires one or both. Read the dispute resolution clause first, then think about strategy. ## How mediation works

A mediator, often a retired judge or an experienced business lawyer, meets with both sides, usually in a full-day session with each party in a separate room. The mediator carries offers back and forth, tests each side's assumptions, and points out risks that each party may be underweighting. If you reach a deal, it is written into a settlement agreement that is enforceable like any other contract. If you do not, you remain free to litigate or arbitrate.

Mediation is relatively inexpensive because it is short, and the parties usually split the mediator's fee. What you say is typically confidential under mediation rules, statutes, or the mediation agreement itself, though the scope of that protection varies by jurisdiction, so confirm it before the session.

Mediation works best when both sides have a reason to settle: a continuing relationship, a desire for privacy, or real uncertainty about how a judge or jury would see the facts. It works poorly when one side needs a ruling on a legal question, or when the other party is using the process to stall.

## How arbitration works

Arbitration is a private trial. The parties agree, in their contract or after a dispute arises, to submit claims to an arbitrator, usually under the rules of a provider such as the American Arbitration Association or JAMS. The process includes written claims, limited document exchange, witness testimony at a hearing, and a written award.

The Federal Arbitration Act makes most commercial arbitration agreements enforceable, and courts can vacate an award only on narrow grounds such as fraud, evident partiality, or an arbitrator exceeding their powers. A legal mistake by the arbitrator is usually not enough. That finality is both the main feature and the main risk.

Arbitration is not always cheaper than court. The parties pay the arbitrators by the hour or day and pay administrative fees to the provider, costs a court does not charge. Savings come from narrower discovery and a faster schedule, if the parties and the arbitrator keep it that way.

Arbitration is private, but private is not the same as confidential. Unless the agreement or the provider's rules require confidentiality, the parties may be free to discuss the case. If that matters to you, write it into the clause.

## Mediation vs arbitration compared on what matters

- Who decides: in mediation, the parties; in arbitration, the arbitrator.
- Binding effect: mediation produces nothing binding unless you sign a settlement; an arbitration award is binding and enforceable in court.
- Appeal: not relevant to mediation; very limited for arbitration.
- Cost: mediation is usually the lowest-cost option; arbitration costs vary widely and include fees courts do not charge.
- Speed: mediation can happen within weeks; arbitration is often faster than litigation, though complex cases can still run long.
- Relationship: mediation is less adversarial and more likely to preserve a working relationship.

## When court is still the better forum

Litigation makes sense when you want a jury, when you want a full right of appeal, or when your position depends on public precedent. Court can also be better when you need fast emergency relief, or when the dispute involves several parties who did not all sign the same agreement and cannot be forced into one arbitration. Many arbitration rules now provide for emergency arbitrators, but a court order is still the familiar tool for freezing a situation quickly. ## Drafting the clause for your next contract

The best time to think about forum is before a dispute exists. A tiered clause that requires executive negotiation, then mediation, then arbitration or litigation gives the parties several chances to settle. When drafting, specify the rules, the number of arbitrators, the location, the governing law, whether emergency court relief remains available, and whether the proceedings are confidential.
