The U.S. allows dual citizenship. You can become a U.S. citizen and keep another nationality, and U.S. citizens can acquire a second citizenship, as long as the other country permits it. The deciding factor is usually the other country's law, not U.S. law. That is the first question an immigration lawyer for dual citizenship will want answered.

## What the U.S. requires

During the naturalization oath, new citizens swear to renounce allegiance to foreign states. The U.S. does not enforce this as a requirement to give up your passport or formally renounce through your former country. In practice, U.S. law does not strip you of U.S. citizenship for holding another nationality.

U.S. citizenship can be lost only through a voluntary act done with the intent to give it up, such as formally renouncing at a U.S. embassy.

## What the other country decides

Some countries allow dual nationality freely. Others automatically revoke citizenship when a national voluntarily naturalizes elsewhere. Some require registration or permission to keep their nationality. A few allow it only for people born with both nationalities.

Before you file your N-400, check:

- Whether your country of origin permits dual nationality
- Whether you must notify its government or apply to retain citizenship
- Whether there are deadlines after naturalization
- Whether there are consequences for property ownership, inheritance, or military service

Your country's embassy or consulate is the official source.

## Passports and travel

- U.S. citizens must enter and leave the U.S. on a U.S. passport
- You can use your other country's passport to enter and exit that country
- Book airline tickets in a name that matches the passport you will show at check-in
- If you hold two passports, keep both valid if you travel frequently

## Taxes and obligations

U.S. citizens are taxed on worldwide income regardless of where they live, with credits and exclusions that can reduce what is owed. Your other country may also impose obligations, such as military service, voting, or tax filings. Plan with a tax professional if you will live abroad.

## Consular help abroad

When you are in your other country of citizenship, that country may treat you only as its national. U.S. consular assistance can be limited in that situation, for example if you are detained.

## Children and dual citizenship

Children born abroad to a U.S. citizen parent may acquire U.S. citizenship at birth while also holding the citizenship of their birth country. Children who become citizens through a parent's naturalization may also keep another nationality, depending on that country's rules. A Certificate of Citizenship or a Consular Report of Birth Abroad documents U.S. citizenship.

## Renouncing a second citizenship

If your other country does not allow dual nationality and you want to keep it, you may need to weigh whether to naturalize at all. Permanent residence offers many benefits without affecting your original citizenship.


## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need a lawyer for dual citizenship?

Many people do not. A dual citizenship lawyer can help when your country's rules are unclear, when you hold property or inheritance rights abroad, or when you need to restore a lost nationality.

### Will naturalization affect my property abroad?

It depends on the other country. Some limit property ownership by foreigners. If you lose your original nationality, those limits could apply to you.

### Can I vote in both countries?

U.S. law allows U.S. citizens to vote in foreign elections, but check the other country's rules. Voting in the U.S. is limited to citizens.

### Will I lose my original citizenship automatically?

Only if your other country's law says so. Confirm with that country's embassy before your oath ceremony, because the change takes effect when you take the oath.
