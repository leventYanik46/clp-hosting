People who come to us after escaping harm usually ask one question first: asylum or U visa? Sometimes the honest answer is both, filed in a particular order. Sometimes it is a third option they have never heard of, such as a VAWA self-petition or Special Immigrant Juvenile Status. Each humanitarian immigration route protects against a different kind of harm, asks for different proof, and ends in a different kind of status, and the wrong choice can waste a year or forfeit a deadline. The [asylum and humanitarian protection overview](/practice-area/key-services/asylum-and-humanitarian-protection) at Capitol Law Partners exists because these routes overlap, and this post sets out the questions that decide between them.

## What each humanitarian immigration route protects against

- Asylum protects people who fear persecution in their home country because of race, religion, nationality, political opinion, or membership in a particular social group. The harm is abroad; the fear is of return.
- The U visa protects victims of qualifying crimes committed in the United States, such as domestic violence, sexual assault, felonious assault, and extortion, who suffered substantial physical or mental abuse and have been helpful to the police or prosecutors.
- The T visa protects survivors of a severe form of trafficking, for labor or for sex, who are in the United States because of the trafficking and who would face extreme hardship involving unusual and severe harm if removed.
- A VAWA self-petition protects a spouse or child abused by a U.S. citizen or permanent resident, and a parent abused by an adult U.S. citizen child. It does not require police involvement at all.
- Special Immigrant Juvenile Status protects a person under 21 and unmarried whom a state court has found cannot reunify with one or both parents because of abuse, neglect, or abandonment, and for whom return is not in their best interest.

## The questions that decide

Where did the harm happen? Persecution in your home country points to asylum. A crime committed in the United States points to the U visa or, if the abuser is a citizen or resident family member, to VAWA. Trafficking into or within the United States points to the T visa.

Who caused it? Asylum requires that the persecutor be the government or a group the government cannot or will not control. The U visa and VAWA do not care whether a government was involved; they care about the crime and, for VAWA, the abuser's immigration status.

Can you get a certification? A U visa petition must include Form I-918 Supplement B, signed by a law enforcement agency confirming that you were a victim and have been helpful. Without a willing agency there is no U visa, however serious the crime. VAWA needs no certification and no police report. Asylum needs neither, though both help.

How long have you been here? Asylum must be filed within one year of your last arrival unless an exception applies; our post on the [one-year asylum deadline](/one-year-asylum-deadline-exceptions) covers those. The U and T visas have no filing deadline. A VAWA spouse who has divorced must file within two years of the divorce if the divorce was connected to the abuse.

How old are you? SIJS is available only while a state court still has authority over the young person, which in some states ends at 18. It must be started early, and the Form I-360 petition must be filed before the applicant turns 21.

## What you get at the end

A grant of asylum takes effect immediately: work authorization, a refugee travel document, Form I-730 petitions for a spouse and children within two years, and a green card after one year. It is the fastest of these routes to permanence when the claim is strong, which you can read about in our [affirmative asylum process guide](/affirmative-asylum).

The U visa is capped at 10,000 principal grants a year, and demand exceeds that cap, so approved petitioners wait on a list. As of this writing USCIS can grant deferred action and work authorization to petitioners whose filings it finds bona fide while they wait. U status lasts four years, and a green card follows after three years of continuous presence in that status. The T visa is similar in length, with a green card after three years or sooner if the investigation closes. Both allow derivatives, including, for applicants under 21, parents and unmarried minor siblings.

An approved VAWA self-petition brings deferred action and work authorization, and the green card follows under the family-based rules: immediately available if the abuser is a U.S. citizen, after a wait in the F2A category if the abuser is a permanent resident. An approved SIJS petition places the young person in the fourth employment-based preference, which has a visa backlog, so the green card can take years; the trade-off is that a SIJS recipient can never petition for either parent.

## Filing more than one

None of these routes excludes the others, and the strongest cases often run two at once. A woman who fled an abusive partner abroad and was then assaulted in the United States may file asylum and a U visa petition. A teenager with an asylum claim may also qualify for SIJS, which does not depend on proving persecution. A VAWA self-petitioner who fears return can file asylum too.

Order matters for three reasons. The U visa comes with the broadest inadmissibility waiver in the immigration code, on Form I-192, which can cure problems that would sink an asylum-based green card. A pending U visa or VAWA petition can support a request to close or continue removal proceedings while USCIS decides. And each filing creates a written record that the others must match, so the declarations have to be drafted together.
