If you are a U.S. citizen engaged to someone who lives abroad, you have two main options. You can bring your partner to the U.S. on a K-1 fiancé(e) visa and marry within 90 days, or you can marry first and petition for a spouse visa (IR-1 or CR-1). Both lead to a green card. They differ in cost, paperwork, how soon your partner can work and travel after arriving, and how long the whole process takes.

## How the K-1 fiancé(e) visa works

1. The U.S. citizen files Form I-129F with USCIS.
2. After approval, the case goes to the National Visa Center and then to the U.S. embassy or consulate.
3. Your fiancé(e) submits the online visa application, completes a medical exam, and attends an interview.
4. After entering the U.S. on the K-1 visa, you must marry within 90 days.
5. Your spouse then files Form I-485 to adjust status to permanent resident, usually together with applications for work authorization (Form I-765) and travel permission (Form I-131).

Key requirements: only U.S. citizens can file for a K-1, not green card holders. Both of you must be legally free to marry. You generally must have met in person within the two years before filing, although that requirement can be waived in limited cases, such as extreme hardship or strict cultural or religious customs.

The K-1 is a single-purpose visa. Someone who enters on a K-1 can only get a green card by marrying the person who filed the petition. They cannot switch to another status, and if the wedding does not happen within 90 days, they are expected to leave.

## How the IR-1 and CR-1 spouse visa works

1. After the legal marriage, the U.S. citizen files Form I-130.
2. After approval, the case moves to the National Visa Center, where you pay fees and submit civil documents and the Affidavit of Support (Form I-864).
3. Your spouse attends an immigrant visa interview at the embassy or consulate.
4. Your spouse enters the U.S. as a permanent resident. The physical green card arrives by mail after the USCIS immigrant fee is paid.

If you have been married less than two years when your spouse enters, the visa is a CR-1 and comes with a two-year conditional green card. If you have been married two years or more, it is an IR-1 and comes with a 10-year card. Green card holders can also petition for a spouse, unlike with the K-1, but their spouses wait in a separate preference category that can take longer.

## K-1 visa vs. spouse visa at a glance

| | K-1 fiancé(e) visa | IR-1 / CR-1 spouse visa |
|---|---|---|
| Where you marry | In the U.S., within 90 days of entry | Before filing, anywhere the marriage is legally valid |
| Status on arrival | Nonimmigrant; must still apply for a green card | Permanent resident on entry |
| Work after arrival | Only after work authorization is approved | Immediately |
| Travel abroad after arrival | Needs advance parole while the I-485 is pending | Can travel as a permanent resident |
| Government filings | I-129F, visa application, then I-485 (often with I-765 and I-131) | I-130, then immigrant visa application |
| Overall cost | Usually higher, because there are two stages | Usually lower |

## When the K-1 makes sense

The K-1 suits couples who want to marry in the U.S. with family present, or who cannot easily marry in the partner's country because of legal or practical barriers. At times the K-1 stage has moved faster than a spouse visa, letting couples reunite sooner. Processing times shift with USCIS and consular workloads, so check current estimates before choosing based on speed alone.

The tradeoff comes after arrival. Your partner waits in the U.S. while the green card application is processed, often without permission to work for months, and should not travel abroad until advance parole is approved. Leaving without it is generally treated as abandoning the green card application.

## When the spouse visa makes sense

The IR-1 or CR-1 route suits couples who are comfortable marrying abroad, or are already married, and want the immigrant spouse to arrive with full permanent resident status. Your spouse can work from the first day, travel freely, and skip a second round of USCIS processing inside the U.S. For couples who want fewer steps and lower overall cost, this is often the cleaner path.

Confirm the marriage is legally valid where it takes place. A religious ceremony that was never registered with civil authorities, or a proxy marriage that was never consummated, may not qualify.

## Issues that affect both paths

- **Income:** the U.S. citizen must meet the Affidavit of Support income requirement or use a joint sponsor.
- **Prior marriages:** both partners need proof that any earlier marriage legally ended.
- **Criminal or immigration history:** past overstays, visa refusals, or criminal records can complicate either path and may require a waiver.
- **Conditional residence:** if the green card is approved before your second wedding anniversary, either path leads to a two-year conditional card and a later petition to remove conditions.
- **International marriage broker rules:** K-1 petitioners who met through a marriage broker, or who have certain criminal histories, face extra disclosure requirements.
