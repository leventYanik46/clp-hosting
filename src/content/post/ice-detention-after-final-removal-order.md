After a removal order becomes final, ICE can detain you for a 90-day removal period during which detention is mandatory, and it may continue to hold you after that only as long as reasonably necessary to carry out your removal. The Supreme Court set the practical limit in *Zadvydas v. Davis*: six months of post-order detention is presumptively reasonable, and after six months, if you can show good reason to believe there is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future, the government must rebut that showing or release you. ICE detention after a final removal order is governed by INA 241, and the six-month mark is where a federal habeas petition becomes the tool that moves cases.

## The 90-day removal period and when it starts

INA 241(a)(1) gives ICE 90 days to remove you once the order is final. The removal period begins on the latest of three dates: the date the order became administratively final, which is usually the date of the Board of Immigration Appeals' decision or the expiration of the appeal period; if you filed a petition for review and the court of appeals stayed your removal, the date of the court's final order; or, if you were in criminal custody, the date you were released. During the removal period, detention is mandatory and no bond is available.

Two things can extend the removal period. If you fail or refuse to apply for travel documents or otherwise obstruct your removal, the statute suspends the period and ICE will argue that the time does not count. And a judicial stay of removal pauses the clock. Cooperation with the travel document process is therefore in your interest even while you fight the order on other fronts, and the record of your cooperation becomes evidence in any later habeas petition.

CLP's [habeas corpus practice](/practice-area/key-services/habeas-corpus) tracks the date the removal period began for every detained client with a final order, because every later argument counts from that date.

## Detention after a final order beyond 90 days: INA 241(a)(6)

Once the removal period ends, INA 241(a)(6) permits, but does not require, continued detention of three groups: people who are inadmissible, people removable on criminal or security grounds, and people ICE determines to be a risk to the community or unlikely to comply with the order. Everyone else is supposed to be released under an order of supervision with reporting conditions. In practice, most people with final orders fall into at least one of the three categories, so the question becomes how long 241(a)(6) detention can last.

ICE conducts its own custody reviews under its regulations: a review by the field office at around the 90-day mark, and a headquarters review at around 180 days. You can and should submit a written request for release with evidence of your ties, your cooperation and the reasons removal is unlikely. These reviews are administrative, often cursory, and not a substitute for a judge, but a denial from ICE helps show a court that you exhausted the agency process.

## The Zadvydas six-month rule in practice

*Zadvydas* read 241(a)(6) to contain an implicit reasonableness limit because indefinite detention of a person who cannot be removed would raise serious constitutional problems. The Court chose six months as the presumptively reasonable period for the sake of uniform administration. The rule works in two steps. First, you show good reason to believe removal is not significantly likely in the reasonably foreseeable future. Second, the burden shifts to the government to produce evidence rebutting your showing. The longer detention continues, the shorter the reasonably foreseeable future becomes.

What counts as good reason? The strongest cases involve countries that will not issue travel documents or will not accept returns, statelessness, a grant of withholding of removal or protection under the Convention Against Torture with no third country willing to accept you, and repeated failed removal attempts. Weaker cases involve routine consular delays where the country does accept its nationals and the paperwork is simply slow. ICE's position will usually be that a travel document is expected imminently; the court will want to know how long ICE has been saying that and what it has actually done.

The Supreme Court extended *Zadvydas* to inadmissible noncitizens in *Clark v. Martinez*, so the rule applies regardless of how you entered. In *Johnson v. Arteaga-Martinez* the Court held that 241(a)(6) itself does not require a bond hearing for prolonged post-order detention, leaving the constitutional question open; the Zadvydas release remedy remains, and some district courts additionally order a hearing on due process grounds.

## What a habeas court can order after the six months

Once six months have passed and the removal period has not been suspended, a petition under 28 U.S.C. 2241 asks the district court where you are held to apply the Zadvydas framework. The court can order your release under an order of supervision, set conditions, or order the government to show cause why you should not be released within a short period. If a travel document arrives while the petition is pending, ICE will remove you and the petition becomes moot, so a request to bar removal pending decision is appropriate where the removal order itself is also being challenged through a stay motion in the court of appeals.

Release under Zadvydas is not a visa or a status. You remain under a final order, you report to ICE on a schedule, and ICE may re-detain you if removal becomes likely, for example if your country changes its policy. People released this way often continue to pursue a [motion to reopen](/motion-to-reopen-deadline-exceptions) or other long-term options while reporting. ## Common mistakes in post-order detention cases

Filing before the six months run is the most common error; courts generally dismiss early Zadvydas petitions as premature. Failing to document cooperation with travel documents is the second, because the government's first response is to argue that your own conduct extended the removal period. The third is forgetting that a pending judicial stay pauses the clock, so a petitioner with a stayed removal order may have far less qualifying post-order time than the calendar suggests.
