An EB-1B case has two clients, the institution and the researcher, and the process runs smoothly only when each knows what it owes and when. The EB-1B process begins before any form is filed, with the offer letter and the department's decision to sponsor, and it ends with permanent residence for the researcher and any spouse and children. In between sit the evidence, the brief, Form I-140, possibly a Request for Evidence, and either Form I-485 or a consular interview. CLP's [EB-1B petition services](/practice-area/key-services/eb-1b-green-card) cover the whole sequence; this post walks through it in order.

## Step 1 in the EB-1B process: the offer and the sponsorship decision

Everything depends on a qualifying permanent position. Before the researcher gathers a single exhibit, the department and the researcher should confirm that the offer is tenured, tenure-track or of indefinite duration with an expectation of continued employment, and that the offer letter says so. Our post on [what counts as a permanent job offer for EB-1B](/eb-1b-permanent-job-offer) covers the position types. At the same stage, the institution decides who will sign the I-140, who will pay, whether premium processing will be requested, and whether its general counsel or an outside firm will prepare the petition. Many universities have a sponsorship policy; read it early.

The researcher, in parallel, should confirm the three years of post-degree teaching or research experience and gather employment verification letters for each position.

## Step 2: the case assessment

A candid assessment compares the record with the EB-1B standard and with the alternatives. Two of six criteria are needed, with international recognition as outstanding shown across the whole record. If the citation record is thin or the recognition is confined to one country, the assessment may recommend waiting, or pairing the EB-1B with a self-petitioned EB-2 National Interest Waiver. The assessment also checks status: a researcher on J-1 status subject to the two-year home residence requirement can have the I-140 approved but cannot adjust status until the requirement is waived or satisfied, so a waiver application belongs in the timeline. See our [J-1 visa](/practice-area/key-services/j-1-visa) page for that requirement.

## Step 3: evidence, letters and the brief

This is the longest stage, and recommenders set the pace. The researcher assembles publication lists, citation reports, peer review records, award documentation and press. Counsel identifies the criteria to claim, writes outlines for four to six recommendation letters with an emphasis on independent experts abroad, and collects the employer's documents: the offer letter, a description of the position, and ability-to-pay evidence under 8 CFR 204.5(g)(2), meaning annual reports, federal tax returns, audited financial statements or, for employers with 100 or more employees, a statement from a financial officer. Private employers also document the three full-time researchers and the research unit's accomplishments.

The brief ties the exhibits to the two-step analysis: the criteria first, then the final merits argument for international recognition. Every exhibit is indexed and foreign-language documents are translated.

## Step 4: filing Form I-140

The institution signs Form I-140 as petitioner, with the researcher as beneficiary, and the petition is filed with the current USCIS filing fee. Premium processing is available for EB-1B petitions; it commits USCIS to act within a short fixed window by approving, denying or issuing a Request for Evidence, and it is often worth requesting when the researcher's status or an H-1B extension depends on the outcome. The receipt date becomes the researcher's priority date.

If the first preference is current for the researcher's country of birth under the Visa Bulletin and the researcher is in the United States in lawful status, Form I-485 can be filed concurrently, together with Form I-765 for employment authorization and Form I-131 for advance parole. Our post on [how the Visa Bulletin works for EB-1](/visa-bulletin-eb-1-first-preference) explains how to check.

## Step 5: the RFE, approval and the green card stage

An RFE in an EB-1B case most often questions the permanence of the position, the private employer's research department, the three years of experience, or the final merits. The response deadline is stated in the notice and cannot be extended. The institution may need to supply a supplemental letter about the position; the researcher may need updated citation data or additional independent letters. Answer every point in the order raised, with documents.

After approval, the path depends on where the researcher is and whether a visa number is available. Inside the United States with a current priority date, the I-485 proceeds through biometrics, the medical examination and possibly an interview to approval. Abroad, the approved petition goes to the National Visa Center, the researcher files Form DS-260 and attends a consular interview, and the green card is produced after admission. Our post on [what happens after an EB-1 I-140 is approved](/after-eb-1-i-140-approved) covers this stage in detail, including job changes: once the I-140 has been approved for 180 days a withdrawal does not automatically revoke it, and once the I-485 has been pending 180 days the researcher may port to a same or similar position with a new employer. The spouse and unmarried children under 21 file as derivatives at the same stage.

## What each party owes at each step

- The institution: a qualifying offer letter, the sponsorship decision, ability-to-pay evidence, the I-140 signature, and any supplemental position letters during an RFE.
- The researcher: the experience letters, the publication and citation record, the recommender list, status documents, and the I-485 or consular paperwork for the family.
- Counsel: the assessment, the criteria selection, letter outlines, the brief, the filing, the RFE response and the green card stage.
