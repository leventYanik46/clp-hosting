Registering a business name with the state does not make it a trademark. When you form an LLC or corporation, the state checks that no other entity on its own records has the same or a confusingly similar entity name, approves the formation, and stops there. It does not search the USPTO register, it does not search other states, and it does not ask whether a company two towns over has sold products under that name for a decade. The business name vs trademark distinction catches founders every year, usually after they have printed the signage. Our [trademark law](/practice-area/key-services/trademark-law) overview explains the full set of brand protections; this post covers the one confusion that causes the most expensive mistakes.

## What a business name registration actually does

An entity name, whether it belongs to an LLC, a corporation or a partnership, exists so the state can identify the legal person that signs contracts, pays taxes and gets sued. The State Corporation Commission in Virginia, the Department of State in New York and the corporations office in the District of Columbia each keep their own list, and each will reject a new entity name only if it is not distinguishable from a name already on that list. "Distinguishable" is a low bar. Adding "Inc." or a single word is often enough.

A trade name or DBA registration works the same way at the county or state level. It puts the public on notice that a person or company is doing business under another name. It grants no exclusive right to the name and does not stop a competitor from using it.

None of these filings is examined for trademark conflicts. A Virginia LLC can be formed with a name that a Delaware company registered with the USPTO years earlier, and the Virginia filing will not protect the LLC when that company sends a demand letter.

## Business name vs trademark: what a trademark protects

A trademark identifies the source of goods or services in the marketplace. Rights arise from use in commerce and can be strengthened with a federal registration, as our post on [trademark rights without registration](/trademark-rights-without-registration) explains. A trademark can be enforced against anyone whose use of a similar mark on related goods is likely to confuse consumers, regardless of what entity name they filed with their state.

A business name can also function as a trademark, but only when it is used that way: on products, packaging, storefronts, websites and advertising as the brand customers see. A name used solely as the legal entity name on invoices and contracts is a trade name, and the USPTO will not register a term that appears only as a trade name. If you want your company name to be a protectable brand, use it as one and consider filing for a [USPTO trademark registration](/practice-area/key-services/trademark-and-intellectual-property-registration) covering the goods or services you actually sell.

## Domain names and social media handles

A domain registration is a contract with a registrar for the exclusive use of a string of characters as an address. It is first-come, first-served, and the registrar checks nothing. Owning the .com confers no trademark rights, and a trademark owner can take a domain away from you through a UDRP complaint or an anticybersquatting claim if your registration or use is in bad faith. The reverse is also true: owning the trademark gives you a tool to recover a domain that someone else registered to trade on your brand.

Social media handles sit even further from legal rights. Platforms assign them under their own terms of service and typically respond to a trademark registration, which is one practical reason brand owners file.

## Three ways the confusion goes wrong

- A founder forms the entity, buys the domain, prints packaging and then learns from a USPTO office action that a registered mark already covers the name for related goods. The entity name was never in question; the brand was.
- A company grows into a new state and receives a demand letter from a business that has used the same name there under common-law rights since before the company existed.
- A business chooses a descriptive name because the state accepted it, then discovers that the USPTO will refuse registration and that competitors can use the same descriptive words freely.

## The right order of operations

Clear the name before you spend money on it. A [trademark clearance search](/practice-area/key-services/trademark-search-and-clearance) covering the federal register, state registers, common-law uses and domains tells you whether the name is yours to build on. Our guide to [how to search for a trademark](/how-to-search-for-a-trademark) walks through what a self-search can and cannot find.

Then form the entity that will own the mark, so the trademark application can be filed in the right name from the start. Our [business formation and structuring](/practice-area/key-services/business-formation-structuring) practice coordinates the two filings, and our post on the [documents required to set up a company in America](/documents-required-to-set-up-a-company-in-america) covers the formation side. Buy the domain and handles once the name clears, and file the trademark application on an intent-to-use basis if launch is still ahead. Done in that order, the entity name, the domain and the trademark all point to the same brand, and each filing protects what it is designed to protect.
