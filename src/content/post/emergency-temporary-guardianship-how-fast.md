A court can appoint an emergency guardian in a matter of days, and in a true crisis sometimes the same day the petition is filed. The trade-off is scope and duration: a temporary or emergency order gives narrow powers for a short period, measured in weeks, and only bridges the gap until a full hearing can be held. To get one, you must show the judge that the adult faces immediate and substantial harm, that no one else already has authority to act, and that waiting through the normal notice period would cause that harm. Here is what qualifies, how each of the firm's jurisdictions handles it, and the mistakes that get fast petitions denied.

## What counts as an emergency

Judges see many petitions labeled urgent that are really just overdue. The situations that actually meet the standard tend to look like these:

- A hospital is ready to discharge the patient, the patient cannot consent to a rehabilitation placement, there is no advance directive, and no relative is on the default surrogate list or the relatives disagree.
- The person is refusing treatment that a physician says is needed to prevent death or serious injury, and cannot understand the consequences of refusing.
- Someone is actively exploiting the person: moving money out of accounts, changing beneficiary designations, or isolating them from family. Here the urgent need is usually a temporary conservator rather than a guardian, as the [conservatorship practice page](/practice-area/key-services/conservatorship) explains.
- The person is about to be evicted, has lost utilities, or is wandering, and adult protective services has become involved.

A diagnosis of dementia, a family argument over care, or a general worry that things are getting worse is not an emergency. Those cases belong on the regular track described in [how to file for guardianship of an adult](/how-to-file-adult-guardianship-petition).

## How the three jurisdictions handle emergency appointments

Virginia's circuit courts can appoint a temporary guardian or conservator when an emergency exists and the respondent's welfare requires immediate action, before the full petition is heard. Notice can be shortened, but the temporary order lasts only until the full hearing or the date the order sets, and the regular petition must still be proved.

The District of Columbia has the most detailed menu. Its statute provides for an emergency guardian, appointed on a showing of likely substantial harm, for a short fixed term; a health-care guardian for treatment decisions when a patient in a facility has no one to consent; and a temporary guardian when an appointed guardian is not performing. Each has its own limits, and the Probate Division expects the petition to pick the right one.

New York's Article 81 allows the court to appoint a temporary guardian at the time it signs the order to show cause, or at any point before the hearing, when there is a showing of danger to the person's well-being or property. The order must spell out the temporary guardian's powers, and the full Article 81 hearing follows on the statute's regular timetable.

## What the emergency guardian petition must show

Speed does not lower the burden; it compresses it. The petition and supporting affidavits should establish, with specifics, four things.

The harm. What will happen, and how soon, if no one is appointed. A physician's letter or a hospital social worker's statement carries far more weight than a family member's description.

The gap in authority. Why no existing document or surrogate can act. If there is a power of attorney, explain why it does not reach the problem or why the agent cannot be trusted.

The powers requested. Only what the emergency requires: consent to a specific placement, authority to freeze a specific account. Judges grant narrow emergency powers readily and broad ones reluctantly.

The plan. A full petition should be filed with, or immediately after, the emergency request. Courts are wary of petitioners who want the fast order and then let the regular case drift.

## What a temporary order does not do

A temporary guardian has only the powers listed in the order, only until the order expires, and remains subject to the same duties as any guardian. The respondent still gets counsel or a guardian ad litem, still gets notice as soon as it can practically be given, and still has the right to contest the appointment at the full hearing. The emergency order does not decide the permanent case, and it does not settle which family member will serve long term; judges sometimes appoint a neutral professional on a temporary basis precisely to avoid pre-judging that question.

If the full petition is later denied, the temporary appointment ends and the guardian must account for anything done under it.

## Mistakes that slow fast petitions

The most common failure is a vague affidavit. "Mom is in danger" gets a hearing date; "Mom was found by police at 2 a.m. on Route 7 in her nightgown on October 3, the hospital will discharge her Friday, and she has refused assisted living three times" gets an order. The second most common is missing medical evidence, which a court will almost never excuse even in an emergency. Third is asking for everything: full guardianship and conservatorship powers on an emergency basis, which signals that the petitioner has not thought about what the crisis actually requires. Fourth is forgetting the respondent: not naming where they are, not attempting any notice, not explaining why the petitioner is the right person. The legal standard behind all of this is laid out in [when an adult needs a court-appointed guardian](/when-adult-needs-court-appointed-guardian).
