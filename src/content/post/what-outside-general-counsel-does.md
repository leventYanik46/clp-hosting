An outside general counsel handles the legal work a growing company generates every month: reviewing and negotiating contracts, answering employment questions, keeping corporate records and filings current, tracking compliance deadlines, and stepping in early when a dispute starts. The role is less about any single task than about continuity. The same lawyers learn your business, your templates, and your risk tolerance, so each question gets answered faster and in context, and problems get caught before they reach a courtroom or a regulator.

That continuity is the core of our [outside general counsel and advisory services](/practice-area/general-counsel-services). Here is what the work looks like in practice.

## Contract review and negotiation

For most clients, contracts are the largest share of the work: customer agreements, vendor and software terms, leases, nondisclosure agreements, independent contractor agreements, and reseller or partnership deals. Over time, outside counsel builds templates and a playbook listing your standard positions and acceptable fallbacks. Routine contracts then move without a lawyer touching every one, and the unusual ones get real attention. Our [contract review and executive counsel](/practice-area/key-services/contract-review-and-executive-counsel) team handles the higher-stakes negotiations within the same relationship.

## Employment and people questions

Hiring, managing, and letting people go generate a steady stream of questions. Typical ones: Is this worker an employee or a contractor? Can we ask this new hire to sign a noncompete in this state? How should we handle a complaint about a manager? What does the offer letter need to say? Is this role exempt from overtime? A quick call before acting often prevents the most expensive kinds of claims.

When the company sponsors foreign workers, outside general counsel can coordinate with immigration attorneys so hiring plans and visa timelines line up rather than collide.

## Corporate governance and housekeeping

Corporate records are easy to neglect and painful to reconstruct during a financing or sale. A typical outside general counsel engagement includes:

- Preparing board and owner consents for major decisions such as equity grants, officer appointments, loans, and large contracts.
- Keeping the cap table, ownership ledger, and operating or shareholder agreements current.
- Tracking annual reports and registrations in each state where the company operates.
- Preparing materials for board meetings and keeping minutes.
- Maintaining the company's policy set and updating it as laws change.

Corporations are generally expected to hold an annual shareholders' meeting and keep minutes, while LLCs usually follow whatever their operating agreement requires. Skipping these formalities rarely causes trouble on its own, but thin records can weaken the company's position if someone later argues the owners and the business were not truly separate, and they slow down any financing or sale while the gaps are rebuilt.

## Compliance, risk spotting, and dispute triage

### Compliance calendar
A good outside general counsel keeps a calendar of license renewals, annual filings, policy reviews, and training, and watches for legal changes that affect your business, such as new privacy laws, wage rules, or industry regulations in your states. Because counsel sees your contracts and operations together, patterns stand out: the same risky clause accepted in several vendor deals, or a sales practice that creates advertising exposure. Bigger concerns can become a formal [legal risk assessment](/legal-risk-assessment-for-business).

### Dispute triage
When a demand letter, agency notice, or angry customer email arrives, outside general counsel is the first call. Counsel assesses the exposure, preserves documents, responds or negotiates, and brings in litigation specialists if the matter escalates. Early handling resolves many disputes before they become lawsuits, and the ones that do go forward start with a better record.

## What outside general counsel coordinates and what it leaves out

No single lawyer covers everything. Part of the job is knowing when a question needs a specialist (tax structuring, securities filings, patents, immigration petitions, litigation) and managing that work so the company is not juggling several firms that never talk to each other.

Most outside general counsel arrangements cover ongoing advisory work but treat large, discrete projects separately: litigation, acquisitions and financings, major real estate deals, and patent prosecution. Those projects are usually priced on their own terms, and clarity about scope at the start prevents surprise bills.
