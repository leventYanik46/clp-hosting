The usual H-1B to green card process has three stages: labor certification (PERM), an immigrant petition (Form I-140), and the final green card application (Form I-485 or consular processing). Each stage has its own agency, its own timeline and its own ways to go wrong, and the whole path can take years because of visa backlogs.

## Why start early

An H-1B is limited to a total of six years in most cases. A green card case that is moving can extend that limit, but only if the right filings are made in time. If the six-year clock is already running down, waiting to see how things go is the riskiest choice.

## Stage 1: PERM labor certification

For most employment-based green cards in the EB-2 and EB-3 categories, the employer first has to show that no qualified U.S. worker is available for the job. That process, called PERM, has three parts:

1. **Prevailing wage determination.** The Department of Labor sets the wage the employer must offer for the role and location.
2. **Recruitment.** The employer advertises the position in the ways the rules require and reviews the responses from U.S. applicants. A required waiting period follows before filing.
3. **Filing the labor certification** (Form ETA-9089) with DOL. DOL may certify, deny, or select the case for an audit.

PERM is employer-driven. The worker supplies information, but the employer files, pays for it and carries the legal responsibility. Employers are not allowed to shift certain PERM costs to the employee.

## Stage 2: The I-140 immigrant petition

Once PERM is certified, the employer files Form I-140 with USCIS. USCIS checks that the employer can pay the offered wage and that you meet the job requirements. Premium processing is often available. Approval of the I-140 does two useful things: it fixes your priority date, and it can allow H-1B extensions beyond the six-year limit.

Some people skip PERM because they qualify in a category that does not need it, such as EB-1 or an EB-2 national interest waiver. That is a different strategy and worth asking about if you have a strong record.

## Stage 3: Adjustment of status or consular processing

Your priority date has to be current under the Visa Bulletin before the final stage can start. For people born in countries with heavy demand, this can be a long wait. When your date is current, you file Form I-485 if you are in the United States, or go through consular processing if you are abroad. After filing, you can usually apply for a work permit and travel document while the case is pending.

## What to watch for

- **Job changes.** Moving to a new employer before a green card case is far along can reset the process. After an I-485 has been pending a certain period, rules on job portability may let you change employers within the same field.
- **Spouse and children.** Dependents generally get green cards with you, so their status and ages need attention.
- **Layoffs.** A layoff can affect PERM and I-140 differently, depending on how far the case has progressed.

## What to ask your employer

Ask when the company is willing to start PERM, whether it sponsors all employees or only certain levels, and who the attorney handling the case will be. Then bring your own questions to a lawyer if the answers leave your timeline unclear.
