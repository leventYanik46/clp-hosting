If your naturalization interview was more than 120 days ago and USCIS still has not decided your N-400, you do not need a conventional mandamus lawsuit. Congress wrote a specific remedy into the naturalization statute: under 8 U.S.C. 1447(b), you may ask the federal district court where you live either to decide the application itself or to send it back to USCIS with instructions, usually a deadline. The 120-day rule is one of the few places in immigration law where a statute hands the applicant a clock and a court. ## What the 120-day rule says

The statute provides that if there is a failure to make a determination on a naturalization application before the end of the 120-day period after the date on which the examination is conducted, the applicant may apply to the district court for a hearing. The court may either determine the matter or remand it to USCIS with appropriate instructions.

Three details drive how this works in practice. The examination is the interview, the day you sat with the officer and were tested on English and civics. Courts have overwhelmingly rejected the government's argument that the examination is the whole investigative process including background checks. The 120 days run from that interview date. And the right to go to court belongs to you as the applicant, so you may file without first exhausting anything with USCIS.

## Why USCIS cases stall after the interview

The most common reason is an unfinished FBI name check or other background investigation, which USCIS must complete before approval. Others include a request for evidence that you answered but that was never matched to the file, a second interview that was never scheduled, a hold tied to a prior immigration file, or an officer's referral for supervisory or fraud review. In many stalled cases the notice you received at the interview said a decision could not yet be made, and nothing followed.

Before filing, make a case inquiry, document the inquiry and the response, and ask whether the officer requested anything that is still outstanding. A file that is missing one document you can supply is not a case for court yet.

## What happens when you file

The petition is a short civil complaint filed in your home district, naming USCIS and the responsible officials. Once it is filed, most courts hold that USCIS loses jurisdiction to decide the application on its own; the case belongs to the court until the judge rules or remands. In practice the government almost always asks the court to remand with instructions to decide within a stated period, and most courts grant that request, since judges prefer that the agency finish the background check rather than conduct the naturalization inquiry themselves. The result is a court-ordered deadline, often paired with a requirement that USCIS report back.

A court can instead decide the application. That happens when the record is complete and the only obstacle is agency inaction, or when the applicant has reason to distrust the agency's good faith. If the court grants naturalization, the oath can be administered by the court.

## The 120-day rule versus mandamus

The difference matters when choosing a remedy. A mandamus suit asks the judge to weigh whether a delay is unreasonable under a multi-factor test, which the government contests case by case; see [how courts decide whether a USCIS delay is unreasonable](/how-courts-decide-uscis-delay-unreasonable). A 1447(b) petition requires no such showing. Jurisdiction exists on the face of the statute once the 120 days have passed, which is why the government typically moves straight to remand rather than to dismiss.

The limit is equally clear. The 120-day rule applies only after the interview. An N-400 that has waited a long time for an interview to be scheduled is a mandamus case, not a 1447(b) case, and the unreasonable-delay factors apply.

## After a denial: a different statute

If USCIS denies the N-400, 1447(b) no longer applies. The route is a request for a hearing before a USCIS officer on Form N-336, and if the denial stands, a petition under 8 U.S.C. 1421(c) for de novo review in district court, where the judge decides eligibility fresh on the evidence presented. That is one of the broadest forms of judicial review in immigration law, and it is also where the record in the A-file matters most, since inconsistencies in old applications surface at this stage. ## Practical steps if you are past day 120

- Confirm the interview date from your interview notice and count 120 days.
- Pull your USCIS online case status and submit a written inquiry; keep the response.
- Review your own file for anything that could justify a denial, including travel history and tax compliance, before you force a decision.
- Decide with counsel whether to seek remand with a deadline or to ask the court to decide.
- Calendar the deadline the court sets and be ready to report noncompliance.
