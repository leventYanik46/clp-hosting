The federal judge who reviews your immigration denial will never see the evidence you wish you had filed. In an Administrative Procedure Act suit against USCIS, and in a petition for review of a removal order, the court decides on the administrative record: the documents the agency had when it made its decision, nothing more. A powerful expert letter obtained after the denial is inadmissible. A fact you forgot to mention at the hearing does not exist. This record rule is the single most important thing to understand about federal immigration litigation, and it has consequences long before any lawsuit is contemplated. CLP's [federal immigration litigation practice](/practice-area/key-services/immigration-litigation) spends as much time on building records as on filing complaints, for exactly this reason.

## What the administrative record is in an immigration lawsuit

For a USCIS case, the record is everything USCIS considered: the petition and its exhibits, any request for evidence and your response, interview notes, and the denial itself. After you sue, the government compiles and files a certified administrative record, often running to hundreds of pages, and the case proceeds on that document.

For a removal case, the record is the record of proceedings from immigration court: the Notice to Appear, every exhibit admitted, the transcript of the hearings, the immigration judge's decision, the briefs to the Board of Immigration Appeals and the Board's decision. The statute tells the court of appeals to decide the petition only on that record.

## Why courts refuse new evidence

The rule comes from the Supreme Court's long-standing instruction that judicial review of agency action focuses on the record already in existence, not a new record made in court. The court's job is to ask whether the agency acted reasonably on what it had, not whether it would have decided differently on better evidence. The same logic bars the government from defending a denial with reasons it did not give at the time; the decision must stand on its own stated grounds.

Exceptions exist but are narrow: where the agency deliberately or negligently excluded documents it actually considered, where the record is so bare that review is impossible, or where there is a strong showing of bad faith. Courts grant these rarely. Plan as if they do not exist.

## Building the record at the USCIS stage

Every petition should be prepared as if a judge will one day read it cold. That means:

- A cover letter or brief that identifies each legal requirement, cites the regulation or precedent decision, and points to the exhibits that satisfy it.
- Exhibits that are indexed, labeled and complete, with translations certified.
- A response to any request for evidence that answers every point raised, restates the legal standard where USCIS has misstated it, and objects on the record if the request demands something the law does not require.
- Expert letters that explain the expert's qualifications, methodology and reasoning, since a conclusory letter carries little weight on review.
- A record of every submission, with proof of delivery and receipt dates.

A motion to reopen or reconsider on Form I-290B is the last opportunity to add to the record before suit, which is one reason [to file an I-290B before a federal lawsuit](/i-290b-appeal-vs-federal-lawsuit) in many cases.

## Building the record in immigration court

The same discipline applies with higher stakes. File all exhibits by the court's deadline, because late evidence can be excluded. Make objections on the record, and ask the judge to rule on them. Raise every legal argument you may want the Board or a court of appeals to consider, since issues not raised below are usually treated as waived. Keep your own copy of everything, including the hearing notice and proof of what was served on you. If you later need the court's file, [how to get your immigration court record from EOIR](/eoir-foia-record-of-proceedings) explains the process, and our [removal defense](/practice-area/key-services/removal-defense) team builds hearings with the appellate record in mind.

## What a thin record costs you

A denial that rested on a genuinely incomplete file is nearly impossible to overturn, because the agency's conclusion was reasonable on what it had. Judges say so in opinion after opinion. The reverse is also true: a complete, well-organized record that USCIS denied with boilerplate is the strongest APA case there is, because the court can see what the agency ignored. [How an APA lawsuit challenges a USCIS denial](/apa-lawsuit-uscis-denial) describes that standard.

## When the record is not the end

The record rule governs review. It does not stop you from starting over. New evidence that cannot be considered in court can support a new petition, a motion to reopen with the agency or the Board, or a changed-circumstances filing. Part of litigation strategy is deciding whether the stronger path is to attack the old decision on the old record or to build a new record and file again. Often the answer is both, pursued in parallel, so that a loss in court does not leave the client with nothing.
