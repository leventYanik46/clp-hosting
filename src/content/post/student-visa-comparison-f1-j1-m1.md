The choice between an F-1, a J-1 and an M-1 visa is usually made by the school, not the student, and it is made before anyone explains the consequences. A university that issues a DS-2019 instead of an I-20 may be attaching a two-year home residency requirement to your future. A flight school that admits you as an M-1 is closing the door to a later F-1 change. This post compares the three student visa categories on the points that matter after arrival: work, length of stay, grace periods, the J-1 home residency rule, and what each one lets you do next.

## What the F-1, J-1 and M-1 are for

The F-1 is the academic student visa. It covers universities, colleges, high schools, seminaries, conservatories and language programs at schools certified by SEVP, and the school issues Form I-20. The J-1 is the exchange visitor visa. It covers students, but also research scholars, professors, physicians in graduate medical training, interns, trainees, au pairs and summer work-and-travel participants, and a designated sponsor issues Form DS-2019. The M-1 is the vocational student visa for technical and non-academic programs such as flight training, culinary schools, cosmetology and trade schools, with its own Form I-20. All three require the SEVIS fee, proof of funding, and nonimmigrant intent at the consulate. The services on our [citizenship, status and travel](/practice-area/key-services/citizenship-status-and-travel) page cover each category and the moves between them.

## Work and practical training

The F-1 offers the most. Students may work on campus up to 20 hours a week during the term, use Curricular Practical Training for internships tied to the curriculum, and take 12 months of Optional Practical Training after each degree level, with a 24-month extension for qualifying STEM degrees. The J-1 student's work rights depend on the sponsor's written permission; academic training is available for up to 18 months, or 36 months for postdoctoral researchers, and must relate to the field of study. The M-1 is the most restricted: no employment during the program, and practical training only after completion, earned at one month for every four months of study with a cap of six months.

## Length of stay and grace periods

F-1 and J-1 nonimmigrants are admitted for duration of status, which means as long as the I-20 or DS-2019 is valid and the student follows the rules. The F-1 grace period after the program or OPT is 60 days; the J-1 grace period is 30 days. The M-1 is admitted for the length of the program plus 30 days, for no more than one year at a time, and extensions are capped at three years in total. The M-1 date is a fixed date on the I-94, so an M-1 who needs more time must file Form I-539 before it passes. As of this writing DHS has proposed replacing duration of status with fixed admission periods for F and J as well, which would make extension filings routine for everyone; our post on [I-94 expiration versus visa expiration](/i-94-expiration-vs-visa-expiration) explains how the admit-until date works today.

## The J-1 two-year home residency requirement

This rule has no equivalent in the F-1 or M-1 categories and is the main reason to think carefully before accepting a DS-2019. Under INA 212(e), a J-1 whose program was funded by the U.S. or home government, whose field appears on the skills list for the home country, or who came for graduate medical training must return home for two years in total before changing status inside the United States, or before receiving an H, L, K or immigrant visa. Waivers exist, including a no-objection statement from the home government, an interested government agency request, a hardship or persecution claim, and the Conrad 30 program for physicians. The [J-1 visa](/practice-area/key-services/j-1-visa) page explains each route. Students who expect to stay for graduate work or an H-1B should check the DS-2019 annotation and the skills list before the program starts, not after.

## Transfers, changes of status and the path afterward

F-1 students may transfer schools, move between degree levels, and change to H-1B with the cap-gap extension that keeps F-1 status and OPT alive while a timely H-1B petition is pending. J-1 participants not subject to 212(e) may change status with Form I-539, but those subject to the rule may change only to A or G status until they fulfill it or get a waiver. M-1 students face two hard limits: they may not change to F-1 at all, and they may not change to H-1B if the vocational training is what qualified them for the job. A visitor who wants to study should read our post on [changing from B-2 to F-1 status](/b2-to-f1-change-of-status), because the category on the I-20 the school issues will decide what is possible years later.

### Dependents

Spouses and children of F-1 students hold F-2 status, may study part time, and may not work. J-2 dependents may apply for employment authorization on Form I-765 and may study full time. M-2 dependents may study only in elementary or secondary school and may not work. For a family where the spouse plans to work, the J-1 is the only one of the three that allows it.

## Choosing when the school gives you a choice

Pick the F-1 if you want the widest work options and the easiest path to an employer-sponsored visa, provided the program is academic. Accept the J-1 when a sponsor's funding or a specific exchange program makes it the better fit, and confirm whether 212(e) will apply. Take the M-1 only for a true vocational program and plan to leave or apply from abroad afterward. Whichever category applies, the consular interview turns on funding and ties, as our post on [what to do after an F-1 visa refusal](/after-your-visa-was-refused-f-1-visa-process) describes.
