A medical malpractice case is measured in years rather than months, and most of the work happens before a complaint is ever filed. The stages are the same in Washington, DC, Virginia, and New York, with local paperwork at the front end, and knowing them in advance makes the long middle of the case easier to tolerate. This is the sequence CLP's [medical negligence practice](/practice-area/key-services/medical-negligence) follows, with what each stage asks of you and what can end the case at that point.

## Stage one: intake, records, and expert screening

The case begins with your account and ends this stage with a physician's written opinion, or without one. The firm requests the complete record from every provider involved, under your HIPAA right of access, including imaging files and the electronic audit trail. A physician in the relevant specialty then reviews it against the standard of care. This review costs money, which the firm advances in a contingency fee arrangement, and it ends a large share of inquiries, because the record shows the care was defensible or the harm cannot be tied to the error. A case that clears screening has an expert who can say, in writing, what the provider should have done and what difference it would have made. What that review looks for is covered in [what counts as medical negligence and what is a bad outcome](/medical-negligence-vs-bad-outcome).

## Stage two: naming the right defendants

Hospitals are liable for their employees, and most nurses, residents, and technicians are employees. Many physicians are not. Emergency physicians, radiologists, anesthesiologists, and hospitalists frequently work for independent groups that contract with the hospital, so the hospital will deny responsibility for them unless the facts support apparent agency: the hospital held the physician out as its own and the patient reasonably relied on that. The practice group, an outside laboratory, an imaging company, and a pharmacy may each need to be named separately. Getting this wrong is costly, because a defendant added after the limitations period has run is usually dismissed.

## Stage three: pre-suit requirements and filing

Each jurisdiction has a gate. Virginia requires the plaintiff to hold a written expert certification before serving the complaint, and either party may then request a medical malpractice review panel, whose non-binding opinion is admissible at trial. DC requires a notice of intention to sue served on each defendant at least 90 days before filing. New York requires a certificate of merit filed with the complaint. Claims against federal facilities require an administrative claim first under the Federal Tort Claims Act, and claims against New York public hospitals require a notice of claim within 90 days. The full set of clocks is in [medical malpractice statute of limitations in DC, VA, and NY](/medical-malpractice-deadlines-dc-va-ny).

The complaint itself sets out the facts, the departures from the standard of care, the causation theory, and the damages. The defendants answer, almost always denying everything, and the case enters discovery.

## Stage four: discovery, depositions, and expert reports

Discovery is the longest stage. Both sides exchange written questions and document requests. The defendants produce internal policies, credentialing files, and the complete chart, often more than you received on your own request. Depositions follow: the treating providers, hospital representatives, your family members, and you. Your deposition will cover your medical history, your symptoms, what you were told, and your life since the injury, and preparation for it is a day's work in itself. The defense may require an independent medical examination by a physician it chooses.

Experts are then disclosed, each side's physicians are deposed on their opinions, and the defense will move to exclude your experts or to dismiss the case on the ground that causation is unproven. Surviving those motions is the point at which most cases acquire real settlement value. How the experts build their opinions is explained in [how do you prove medical malpractice in court](/how-to-prove-medical-malpractice).

## Stage five: mediation, settlement, or trial

DC sends every malpractice case to mediation after the answer is filed, and courts elsewhere encourage it. Most cases that survive expert discovery settle, usually after the expert depositions, when both sides can price the risk. Settlement requires resolving liens: Medicare, Medicaid, and self-funded employer health plans have a right to be repaid from the recovery, and the amounts have to be negotiated before the money is distributed.

Cases that do not settle go to a jury. A malpractice trial is an expert contest, with each side's physicians explaining the standard of care and causation, and it runs one to several weeks. Virginia caps the total damages recoverable in a medical malpractice case at a statutory amount that adjusts annually, which affects settlement value from the start. DC and New York have no cap. Either side may appeal a verdict, which adds time before any payment. A fatal outcome changes the plaintiff and the damages but not the stages, as our [wrongful death](/practice-area/key-services/wrongful-death) page explains.

## What a medical malpractice case asks of you

Keep treating and follow medical advice, because gaps in care become arguments that you caused your own harm, a contributory negligence defense that bars recovery in Virginia and DC. Keep a journal of symptoms, limitations, and expenses. Stay off social media about your health. Tell the firm about every provider you see. Expect long stretches of silence between stages, and expect the defense to test your patience; delay is a tactic.
