An E-3 visa change of employer is not like an H-1B transfer. There is no portability rule, so you cannot start the new job when the new employer's paperwork is filed; you can start only when it is approved, or when a new visa is in your passport. Get that one fact wrong and you have worked without authorization, which is a status violation that follows you into every later filing. Our [E-3 visa group](/practice-area/key-services/e-3-visa) handles a steady stream of these moves, and the mechanics below are the ones that decide whether a job change goes smoothly or costs someone their status.

## Why the E-3 has no portability

H-1B workers can begin work for a new employer as soon as that employer's I-129 is received by USCIS, because Congress wrote that rule into INA 214(n) for the H-1B specifically. Nothing equivalent exists for the E-3. Your E-3 status is tied to the employer named on the Labor Condition Application and on your I-94 or approval notice. Working for anyone else, even for a day, is outside the scope of that status.

## Two ways to move to a new E-3 employer

### Option A: change of employer inside the United States

The new employer certifies a new Form ETA-9035 for the new job and files Form I-129 requesting that you be granted E-3 status with that employer. Premium processing is available, which keeps the gap manageable. You keep working for the old employer until the approval arrives, if the old employer will have you, and you start the new job the day USCIS approves. The approval gives you status with the new employer but does not change the visa stamp in your passport; on your next trip abroad you will need a new E-3 visa reflecting the new employer before you can return.

### Option B: a new consular application

The alternative is to resign, leave the United States, and apply for a fresh E-3 visa at a consulate with the new employer's certified LCA and offer letter. This is the same process described in our guide to [applying for an E-3 visa](/how-to-apply-e-3-visa). It can be faster than an I-129 when a consular appointment is available quickly, and it yields a visa you can travel on. The risk is a 221(g) hold or refusal while you are outside the country with no status to come back to. Choose this route only when the file is clean and the appointment is confirmed.

## Same employer, new role, or new location

Changing employers is the obvious trigger, but material changes with the same employer also need attention. A promotion into a different occupation, a move to a worksite in another metropolitan area, or a drop from full time to part time all take you outside the terms of the certified LCA. The employer needs a new LCA, and in most cases an amended I-129. USCIS's reasoning in Matter of Simeio Solutions, an H-1B decision about worksite changes, is the safe benchmark for what counts as material in any LCA-based category. A pay raise within the same role, or a move within the same metropolitan area already covered by the LCA, does not require a new filing.

## Losing your job: the 60-day grace period

If your E-3 employment ends, whether by layoff or resignation, you are not out of status the next morning. Under 8 CFR 214.1(l)(2), E-3 workers and their dependents receive a grace period of up to 60 days, or until the I-94 expires, whichever comes first. The grace period is available once per authorized validity period and is discretionary, meaning USCIS can shorten it for cause.

During those 60 days you may not work, but you may:

- Have a new employer file an I-129 to take over your E-3 status, which, if filed within the period, preserves your position while it is pending
- File Form I-539 to change to another status, such as B-2 to wind up affairs or F-1 to study
- Have a cap-exempt employer file an H-1B petition, since the H-1B lottery does not apply to the organizations described in our note on [H-1B cap-exempt employers](/h-1b-cap-exempt-employers)
- Depart the United States and re-apply for an E-3 later with a new sponsor

The one thing you cannot do is wait past day 60 and then file. Our [change and extension of status](/practice-area/key-services/change-and-extension-of-status) page explains what happens when a filing comes in late.

## Renewals when you stay put

Every two years an E-3 worker who stays with the same employer needs a renewal. The employer certifies a new LCA and either files Form I-129 for an extension inside the United States or sends you to a consulate for a new visa. For in-country extensions, a timely filed I-129 lets you keep working for the same employer for up to 240 days after your old I-94 expires while the extension is pending. Travel during a pending extension is unwise, because leaving the country means you will need the visa anyway and the extension request may be treated as abandoned.

## Mistakes that cost status

The errors we see are predictable: starting the new job the day the I-129 is filed rather than the day it is approved; letting the LCA expire before the renewal is submitted; traveling while a change of status is pending; and relying on an old visa stamp that names a former employer to re-enter for a new one. Each is avoidable with a calendar and a conversation before the change, not after.
