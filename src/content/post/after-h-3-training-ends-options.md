When your H-3 training ends, three rules decide what you can do next. The H-3 is capped at two years for a trainee (18 months for a special education exchange visitor), with no extension beyond that. Once you have spent the full period in H-3 status, you cannot be readmitted in H or L status, extend, or change to it until you have resided and been physically present outside the United States for the six months immediately before. And the H-3 carries no long grace period; your admission may include up to 10 days after the petition end date, and that time is for departure or a filed change of status, not for work. Everything else follows from those three points.

## The two-year maximum

An H-3 petition can be approved for the length of the training program up to two years. If the program was approved for a shorter period, the employer can file an extension on Form I-129 up to the two-year total, with evidence that the training continues according to the original plan. USCIS looks at whether the extension is consistent with the curriculum; a plan that was supposed to finish in twelve months and now needs twenty-four draws questions about whether the trainee became an employee. Our [H-3 visa](/practice-area/key-services/h-3-visa) page explains how we handle extensions so the record stays consistent with the original plan.

Time spent in H-3 status counts toward the maximum even if there were gaps between programs or employers. A trainee who did one year at one company and moves to a second company's H-3 program has one year left, and the second petition must say so.

## The six-month foreign residence rule

After 24 months in H-3 status (or 18 in the special education branch), the trainee is ineligible for any H or L classification until six months have passed outside the United States. The rule is categorical and applies to H-1B, H-2B, L-1, and a new H-3. It is also the single most important planning fact for employers who hope to keep a trained person on, because the obvious move, an H-1B change of status at the end of training, is barred for anyone who used the full two years.

Two ways to work with it:

- End the H-3 early. If the trainee leaves H-3 status before reaching 24 months, the six-month rule does not apply, and a change of status to H-1B can be filed while the H-3 is still valid. This requires the employer to plan the H-1B registration and petition around the training end date, which our post on [how the H-1B process works](/understanding-the-h-1b-visa-process) describes from the employer side.
- Use the six months abroad productively. A trainee who returns to the foreign affiliate and works there for a full year becomes eligible for an L-1 as a manager or specialized knowledge employee, which is often a better category than an H-1B anyway. The [L-1 transfer guide](/l-1-visa-guide-transfers) covers the one-year abroad requirement.

## Changes of status that remain open

The six-month rule covers H and L only. If you have not exceeded the maximum, or if you are moving to a different letter of the alphabet, the usual change-of-status filings on Form I-539 or I-129 remain available before your authorized stay ends:

1. F-1 for a degree program, with a Form I-20 from an SEVP-certified school. Students who later finish a U.S. degree gain access to OPT and a second H-1B lottery pool.
2. O-1 for a trainee with a record of achievement in the field, filed by a U.S. employer or agent.
3. B-2 to wind up affairs, with a clear departure plan.
4. Dependent status through a spouse who holds H-1B, L-1, F-1, or similar status.

The H-3 is not dual intent, and a trainee who files an immigrant petition should expect questions about intent at any later consular interview. Our [change and extension of status](/practice-area/key-services/change-and-extension-of-status) page covers the timing rules, including that departure abandons a pending I-539 and that a request filed after the authorized stay ends is generally denied.

## Timing an H-1B from H-3 status

For trainees who have not used the full two years, the H-1B route works like this. The employer registers in March for the cap, files the petition if selected with a change of status request, and the H-1B takes effect October 1. The H-3 must still be valid on the day USCIS approves the change. If training ends in June, the trainee needs a bridge: an H-3 extension to a date after October 1 (if the two-year cap and the curriculum allow it), or a change to another status in the meantime. Cap-exempt employers avoid the calendar problem entirely, and our post on [cap-exempt H-1B employers](/h-1b-cap-exempt-employers) lists who qualifies.

Remember the ground rule that the H-3 cannot be designed to recruit and train for U.S. staffing. An employer that files an H-1B for every trainee at the end of every program invites USCIS to treat the next H-3 petition as a hiring pipeline. Keep the two decisions separate in the record: the training plan as approved, and a later, independent hiring decision for a position the trainee happens to be qualified for.

## If you overstayed or worked past the end date

Working after the petition end date is unauthorized employment. Remaining after the admission period (including the 10 days) accrues unlawful presence once the I-94 date passes. Neither is fatal to every future application, but both must be disclosed, and both make a consular officer less willing to issue the next visa. If this has already happened, do not file a change of status in the hope it goes unnoticed; a denial on that record is worse than a departure and a well-documented new application. Trainees whose future plans involve a different U.S. program should also review our comparison of [H-3 and J-1 trainee programs](/h-3-visa-vs-j-1-trainee), since a J-1 may be available where a new H-3 is not.
