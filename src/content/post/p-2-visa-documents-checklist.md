A P-2 petition is unusual among work visas because the evidence is mostly about the exchange itself rather than the artist's fame. That makes the P-2 visa documents list shorter than an O-1 package, but each item does more work, and a missing one tends to produce a request for evidence rather than a quiet approval. Below is the checklist we use, grouped by what each document is meant to prove, followed by the gaps that most often draw an RFE.

## The exchange agreement and the sponsor's statement

The first exhibit is a copy of the formal reciprocal exchange agreement between the U.S. organization sponsoring the artist and the foreign organization that will receive the U.S. artists. If the governing document is a standing union program, include the program rules and the specific paperwork enrolling this engagement under them. Translate anything not in English with a certified translation.

The second exhibit is a statement from the sponsoring organization describing the exchange. Write it as a narrative: the names of the organizations, the artists moving in each direction, the dates and venues of both engagements, the compensation and working conditions on each side, and a short explanation of why the exchange is comparable. Adjudicators read this statement first and check the rest of the file against it. Inconsistencies between the statement and the contracts are the single most common RFE trigger we see, and it is the item our [P-2 visa petition](/practice-area/key-services/p-2-visa) team drafts most carefully.

## Proof the artists are comparable

The regulation asks for evidence that the beneficiary and the U.S. artists going abroad have comparable skills and that the terms and conditions of employment are similar. This is not an extraordinary ability standard, and it should not be documented like one. Useful exhibits include:

- Biographies and résumés of the beneficiary and the U.S. counterparts, showing training and performance history
- Programs, posters, or press for past engagements on both sides
- The contracts for each direction of the exchange, so the pay, length, and duties can be compared side by side
- Union membership cards or confirmation for both sets of artists where applicable

Keep the comparison honest. If the U.S. artists are principal players and the incoming artists are students, the exchange will fail on comparability no matter how good the paperwork looks. And if the beneficiary's real strength is a traditional art form rather than parity with a U.S. counterpart, the [P-3 visa for culturally unique artists](/practice-area/key-services/p-3-visa) may be the better category.

## Labor organization consultation and concurrence

Include the written advisory opinion from the appropriate labor organization regarding the nature of the work and the beneficiary's qualifications. Include, separately, evidence that the labor organization was involved in negotiating the exchange or has concurred with it. In a union-run program one letter may cover both points; say so explicitly in the cover letter rather than assuming the officer will connect them. If no appropriate labor organization exists for the discipline, include a short statement explaining why, with whatever support you can find, such as a letter from a national arts service organization.

## Contracts, itinerary, and petitioner authority

Attach a copy of the written contract between the petitioner and the beneficiary, or a summary of the terms if the agreement is oral. For multiple engagements, attach an itinerary listing dates, venues, and cities, together with the contracts or summaries for each. When a U.S. agent files on behalf of several employers, add evidence that the agent is authorized to act for them, usually a signed authorization from each.

For essential support personnel, prepare a separate petition with its own consultation, a statement describing the person's prior and current essentiality, critical skills, and experience with the principal artists, and their contract or a summary of its terms. The sequence for assembling and filing all of this is in [how the P-2 petition process works](/p-2-visa-petition-process-steps).

## Documents the artist brings to the consulate

After approval, the beneficiary needs the I-797 approval notice, a copy of the petition and supporting evidence, a valid passport, the DS-160 confirmation, and, for family members, marriage and birth certificates for P-4 visas. Consular officers can ask about the exchange, so the artist should be able to describe it in plain terms.

## The gaps that draw an RFE

In our experience, RFEs on P-2 petitions cluster around four problems: an exchange agreement that is vague about dates or numbers, a sponsor statement that does not match the contracts, missing evidence of union concurrence (as distinct from the consultation), and no proof of the U.S. artists' side of the exchange at all. Each is avoidable at the drafting stage. If an RFE arrives anyway, respond on every point it raises, resubmit the corrected exhibits, and keep the response inside the deadline; a denied petition can be refiled, but the engagement dates rarely survive.
