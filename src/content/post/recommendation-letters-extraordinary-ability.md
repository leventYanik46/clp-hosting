USCIS officers read recommendation letters with a specific skepticism: letters are written at the petitioner's request, by people chosen by the petitioner, and they are the one category of evidence the petitioner controls. That is why USCIS policy guidance tells officers that letters which merely repeat the regulatory language or praise the beneficiary in general terms carry little weight, while letters that explain a specific contribution and its effect on the field can be persuasive. Recommendation letters for EB-1 and O-1 petitions therefore have one job: to interpret the independent evidence, not to substitute for it. The firm's [talent visa practice](/practice-area/key-services/talent-and-extraordinary-ability-visas) treats letter planning as a drafting task, and this post explains how.

## What a recommendation letter for an EB-1 or O-1 must do

Each letter should do four things. It should establish the writer's own standing, briefly and verifiably, so the officer knows why this opinion matters. It should state how the writer knows your work, including whether you have ever worked together. It should describe one or two of your contributions in concrete terms: what problem existed, what you did, and what changed as a result, with references to the publications, products, patents or performances where the work can be seen. And it should connect that contribution to the standard in the petition, which differs across categories.

For an EB-1A, the letter speaks to original contributions of major significance and to your standing among the top of the field. For an [O-1A visa](/practice-area/key-services/o-1a-visa), the standard is distinction, and the letter can be somewhat less sweeping. For an EB-1B, the question is international recognition as outstanding in your academic area, so letters from researchers abroad who have built on your work are especially valuable; our [EB-1B outstanding researcher](/practice-area/key-services/eb-1b-green-card) page explains that standard. For an NIW, the letter should speak to the national importance of your proposed endeavor and your ability to advance it.

## Who should write

The most persuasive letter comes from someone who has never met you. An independent expert who learned of your work through publications, conferences or industry use and can say how it affected their own work demonstrates reach beyond your circle. A balanced set for most petitions includes:

- Two or more independent recommenders in your field, ideally in different countries, who can describe specific uses of your work.
- One or two people who supervised or collaborated with you and can describe the work from the inside, clearly identified as such.
- Where relevant, a user of your work outside academia: a company that licensed a method, a clinic that adopted a protocol, an agency that relied on a report.

Avoid a stack of letters from your own department or your own company. Officers notice when every signatory shares your employer, and the final merits determination asks whether the acclaim extends beyond the people who have a stake in your career.

## Drafting mechanics that hold up

Write an outline for each recommender, not a finished letter. Give each writer a different contribution to focus on so the letters do not repeat one another, and send them the exhibits they should cite so the letter and the record match. Ask them to write in their own voice. Identical phrasing across letters is a recurring Request for Evidence trigger, because it signals a single author.

Each letter should be on letterhead, signed, dated, and accompanied by the writer's CV or a short biography. If the writer composes the letter in another language, a certified translation is required. Length matters less than specificity; two focused pages beat five pages of adjectives. Keep the regulatory words out of the letter unless the writer genuinely addresses them. A letter that announces you have made original contributions of major significance without explaining what they were reads as coached.

## Letters cannot carry a petition alone

Every talent category rests on documentary evidence first. Citations, awards, press coverage, peer review invitations, contracts and pay records show what happened; letters explain why it mattered. An EB-1A with eight glowing letters and thin independent evidence will usually fail at the final merits stage, while a petition with strong documents and three precise letters often succeeds. Our [EB-1A evidence checklist](/eb-1a-evidence-checklist) lists the documents that should come first, and our post on [NIW evidence examples](/eb-2-niw-evidence-examples) shows how letters fit around them in a waiver case.

For the O-1, the letters also work alongside the mandatory consultation from a peer group or labor organization, which is a separate document with its own requirements; see our post on [proving extraordinary ability for the O-1](/how-to-prove-extraordinary-ability-o-1).

## Mistakes that draw RFEs

- Letters that describe you as brilliant, talented or world-class without naming a single specific result.
- Letters from recommenders whose own credentials are not documented, leaving the officer no way to weigh the opinion.
- Letters that claim independence when a co-authored paper in the record shows otherwise.
- Letters that address the wrong standard, such as a Dhanasar-style endeavor letter in an EB-1A.
- Letters written years earlier for an O-1 and reused unchanged for an EB-1A, when the EB-1A must show acclaim that is sustained up to the filing date.
