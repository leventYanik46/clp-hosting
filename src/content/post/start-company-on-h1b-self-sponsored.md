Many H-1B workers have an idea for a company. The good news: you can own a business on an H-1B. The catch: you generally can't work for it unless the business itself sponsors you. Understanding the difference between owning and working is the key to starting a company on an H-1B without putting your status at risk.

## Owning vs working

**Owning shares or forming a company** is passive investment, and it's allowed. You can incorporate a company, invest in it, hold equity, and serve as a shareholder.

**Working for the company** is employment, and H-1B status only authorizes work for the employer that filed your petition. Activities that count as work include writing code for the product, selling to customers, managing day-to-day operations, and hiring staff. Doing these for your own startup, even unpaid, can be unauthorized employment.

There is a gray area for limited strategic activity, such as attending an occasional board meeting as an investor. Be cautious. The more your activity looks like a job, the higher the risk.

## How a self-sponsored H-1B works

Your own company can sponsor you for an H-1B. This is often called a self-sponsored H-1B. To qualify, the company must show:

1. **A real employer-employee relationship.** USCIS will look at whether the company can control your work, even if you are a majority owner. A board of directors or other mechanism that can hire, fire, and supervise you helps.
2. **A specialty occupation.** The role must require a bachelor's degree in a related field.
3. **Ability to pay the prevailing wage.** The company needs funding or revenue to pay your salary.
4. **A valid LCA** for the worksite.

## The beneficiary-owner rule

A 2025 modernization rule codified how USCIS handles H-1B petitions for people who own a controlling interest in the petitioning company. If you own more than 50% or have majority voting rights, your initial H-1B approval and first extension are each limited to 18 months, instead of the usual three years. Later extensions can be approved for the full period.

The rule also confirmed that you can perform non-specialty duties, like business development, as long as most of your time is spent on the specialty occupation.

## The lottery still applies

A self-sponsored H-1B is still subject to the annual lottery for most new petitions. If you already hold H-1B status through another employer, you've been counted against the cap, so your startup can file a concurrent or change-of-employer petition without the lottery.

Concurrent employment is a useful route. Keep your current job and add a concurrent H-1B from your startup for part-time work.

## Common mistakes

- **Working before approval.** Starting to work for your company before its H-1B petition is filed (for a transfer) or approved (for a new petition).
- **Weak control structures.** A company where you have total, unchecked control may struggle to show an employer-employee relationship.
- **No funding.** USCIS expects proof the company can pay you.
- **Forgetting the LCA wage obligations.** If you're on the company's H-1B, you must actually be paid the required wage.

## Alternatives to the self-sponsored H-1B

- **O-1A,** if you have a record of extraordinary ability, can be sponsored by your startup.
- **E-2,** if you're a treaty national and can invest substantially.
- **EB-2 NIW,** for founders whose work has national importance, leading to a green card.

## A practical sequence for founders already on H-1B

Many founders follow a sequence like this:

1. Form the company and raise initial funding while working only as a passive investor.
2. Set up governance that gives a board real authority over the founder's employment.
3. Have the startup file a concurrent H-1B petition for part-time work, keeping the current employer's H-1B in place.
4. Once the startup can pay a full-time wage and has a track record, move the H-1B fully to the startup.

This approach avoids the lottery, because you were already counted against the cap, and it keeps a stable fallback if the startup takes longer than expected. Be realistic about the 18-month validity for majority owners: the startup will need to show real operations and funding when it files the extension.

## What USCIS looks for in a founder petition

Expect questions about funding sources, the business plan, the office location, and whether the specialty occupation role is genuine rather than invented to justify the petition. A company with investment documents, a payroll provider, and a defined product roadmap is in a much stronger position than one with only incorporation papers.
