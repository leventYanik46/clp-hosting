How much an immigration lawyer costs depends on what you need, who your lawyer is and how the fee is structured, so no single figure applies to everyone. What you can do is understand the models, know what drives the price and ask the right questions before you sign. This article explains how immigration lawyer prices are typically set, without quoting numbers that vary from firm to firm and year to year.

## The three common fee structures

**Flat fee.** The lawyer quotes one price for a defined piece of work, such as a marriage-based green card filing or an N-400. This is the most common model for routine cases because it lets you budget. Check exactly what is included. A flat fee may or may not cover Requests for Evidence, interviews, appeals or follow-up filings.

**Hourly billing.** You pay for the lawyer's time, usually at different rates for partners, associates and paralegals. This is more common for complex or unpredictable matters, such as immigration court defense or a case in crisis. Ask for an estimate of the likely hours and how often you will be billed.

**Staged or milestone fees.** The case is divided into phases, with a fee for each. For example, one fee for preparation and filing and another for the interview. This spreads the cost over time and lets you see progress.

Many firms use a retainer, an advance deposit that is applied to fees as work is done. Ask whether any part is refundable.

## What affects the price

- **Case type.** An uncomplicated renewal costs less work than an employment-based petition or a removal defense.
- **Complexity.** Prior overstays, arrests, previous denials or unusual facts add work.
- **Number of applicants.** Adding a spouse and children generally increases cost, though sometimes at a lower rate.
- **Evidence needed.** Cases that require expert letters, extensive documentation or translations take more time.
- **Government response.** If USCIS issues an RFE or a denial, there may be additional fees for responding.
- **Court appearances.** Hearings add time and cost.
- **Urgency.** Rushed filings can require more work.
- **The firm.** Location, experience and staffing all play a role.

## Consultation fees

Some firms offer free initial consultations. Others charge a fee, sometimes credited toward the case if you hire them. Ask before you book, and ask how long the meeting lasts and who you will meet with. A paid consultation with an experienced lawyer can be good value, especially when you need a candid view of whether to file at all.

## Costs that are not the lawyer's fee

Legal fees are usually separate from:

- **Government filing fees** paid to USCIS, the Department of State or the court, which change from time to time
- **Biometrics fees,** where applicable
- **Medical exam fees** from a designated physician
- **Translation and document costs**
- **Courier and postage**
- **Expert witnesses or evaluations**

Ask for a written estimate that separates the legal fees from these outside costs, so you can plan.

## How to compare quotes

Compare scope, not only price. A lower quote that excludes interview preparation or RFE responses may cost more in the end. Ask:

- What exactly does the fee cover?
- What is billed separately?
- What happens if the case is denied or I decide not to proceed?
- Who will work on my file?
- Is there a payment plan?

Always get a written fee agreement.

## Beware of cheap shortcuts

Be careful with anyone who is not a licensed attorney or accredited representative, even if they offer a very low price. Mistakes can lead to a denial or worse, and it may be hard to fix. Verify the person's license with the state bar.

## Is it worth it

Not every case needs a lawyer, but when the stakes are high or the facts are complicated, good advice often costs less than fixing a mistake. A consultation is a way to find out where your case falls.
