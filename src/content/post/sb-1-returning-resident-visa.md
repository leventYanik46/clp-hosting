A permanent resident who has been outside the United States for a year or more without a re-entry permit, or whose permit expired while abroad, cannot use the green card to board a flight home. The SB-1 returning resident visa is the consular remedy for that situation, and it is available only to residents who can show that the long absence was caused by circumstances beyond their control and that they never gave up the intent to return. This post explains when the SB-1 returning resident visa is needed, the eligibility test, the two-stage process at the consulate, the evidence that persuades, and the alternatives if the consulate says no.

## When a green card stops working at the border

A green card is a valid entry document after a temporary absence of less than one year. A re-entry permit extends that to the permit's validity, usually two years. Beyond those limits, an airline will often refuse boarding, and a resident who does reach a port of entry is treated as an applicant for admission who must prove that residence was not abandoned. The SB-1 moves that decision to a consular officer before travel, which is safer than arguing at the airport. The [travel and re-entry permits](/practice-area/key-services/travel-and-reentry-permits) page explains how the permit prevents the problem in the first place, and our post on [how long a green card holder can stay abroad](/how-long-green-card-holder-stay-abroad) covers the shorter thresholds.

## The SB-1 eligibility test

The consulate asks four questions. Were you a lawful permanent resident when you left the United States? Did you leave intending to return, and have you kept that intent throughout? Was the stay abroad extended by reasons beyond your control, rather than by choice? And are you otherwise eligible for an immigrant visa, with no new inadmissibility problem? The third question is where most applications are decided. A medical emergency with hospital records, an employer's unexpected extension of an overseas assignment, a legal matter that barred departure, caring for a dying parent, a pandemic border closure, or a passport confiscation can all qualify. Deciding to stay another year because a job was good, or waiting for a child to finish school, usually cannot.

## Step by step at the consulate

1. File Form DS-117, Application to Determine Returning Resident Status, with the consulate, with the fee the consulate charges, your green card, any re-entry permit, your passport showing departure and travel dates, and evidence of your ties and of the reason for the delay.
2. Attend the returning resident interview, where the officer decides whether you qualify for SB-1 status.
3. If approved, complete the immigrant visa application on Form DS-260, obtain police certificates where required, undergo a medical examination with a panel physician, and attend a second interview.
4. Receive the SB-1 immigrant visa, enter the United States, and be admitted as a returning permanent resident. A new green card follows.

The second stage mirrors the consular process for any immigrant, which our guide to the [U.S. green card process](/understanding-the-u-s-green-card-process-a-step-by-step-guide) describes.

## Evidence that persuades

The officer wants proof of ties that continued through the absence and proof of the obstacle. Collect U.S. tax returns filed as a resident for every year abroad, a lease or deed on a U.S. home, bank statements showing activity, a driver's license and insurance kept current, evidence of a job held open or family who remained, and anything showing that you tried to return: cancelled flights, denied boarding records, correspondence with an employer about the assignment end date, medical records with dates, court orders. A short, dated statement that tells the story in order, with each claim tied to a document, does more than a thick file without a narrative.

## If the SB-1 is refused

A refusal does not strip your status; only an immigration judge can find abandonment. It leaves three paths. A U.S. citizen or permanent resident relative, or an employer, can file a new immigrant petition, and you process a new immigrant visa abroad, subject to priority dates in the preference categories; the [family-based immigration](/practice-area/key-services/family-based-immigration) page covers the relative route. You can travel to a port of entry with your green card and evidence, request admission as a returning resident, and if CBP disputes it, decline to sign Form I-407 and ask for a hearing, where the government bears the burden of proving abandonment by clear, unequivocal and convincing evidence; that path requires an airline willing to board you and a tolerance for a possible period of proceedings. Or you can sign the I-407 voluntarily to close the chapter and start a fresh immigrant or nonimmigrant application without an abandonment finding on the record. The right choice depends on how strong the evidence is and how quickly you need to return.

## Avoiding the problem next time

Residents who travel for long periods should obtain a re-entry permit before each extended absence, as our post on [getting a re-entry permit before a long trip](/re-entry-permit-before-long-trip) explains, return before any permit expires, keep a documented U.S. home base, and file resident tax returns. Those who expect to live abroad for years should talk to counsel about whether to naturalize first, since a citizen can leave for any length of time and come back.
