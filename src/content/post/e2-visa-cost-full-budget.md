The E-2 visa cost has four parts: the investment in your U.S. business, government fees, professional fees such as legal work and the business plan, and the money you need to live while the business grows. Most investors focus on the first and underestimate the rest. Here is how to build a realistic budget.

## The investment itself

There is no fixed minimum E-2 investment in the regulations. The amount must be substantial in relation to the total cost of buying or starting the business. A lower-cost business needs a higher percentage of its cost invested. The money must be committed and at risk, and spent on the business, not held as personal savings.

Plan your investment based on what the business really needs to start and operate, not on a number you read online.

## E-2 government fees

**Visa application fee.** The State Department charges a fee for the E visa application, paid before the interview.

**Reciprocity fee.** Depending on your nationality, you may owe an additional reciprocity fee when the visa is issued. Some nationalities pay nothing; others pay significant amounts. Check the State Department's reciprocity table for your country.

**USCIS fees, if applicable.** If you are already in the U.S. and change status to E-2 through USCIS, you file Form I-129 and pay the filing fee and Asylum Program Fee. Premium processing is optional.

**Dependents.** Your spouse and children each need their own visa application.

## Business plan cost

A business plan written for the E-2 is a core part of the application. Adjudicators expect hiring timelines, financial projections, and market analysis that show the business is not marginal. Professional E-2 business plan writers charge varying amounts depending on complexity and research. A simple service business costs less than a manufacturing operation or a franchise.

A cheaper plan that repeats generic language often costs more in the end, because weak plans lead to refusals.

## Legal fees

Attorney fees vary by complexity. Factors that increase legal work:

- Buying an existing business, which requires due diligence and transaction review.
- Complex source of funds, such as money from several countries or relatives.
- A business structure with multiple owners of different nationalities.
- Prior visa refusals.

Some firms charge a flat fee for preparing the application, with separate fees for business formation or contract review.

## Business startup costs

Beyond the investment counted for immigration, you will pay for:

- Entity formation and registered agent fees.
- Business licenses and permits.
- Commercial insurance.
- Accounting and payroll setup.
- Lease deposits and build-out.

Many of these can count toward the investment if they are spent on the business. Track them carefully.

## Living costs

Your E-2 business should eventually support you, but most new businesses take time. Budget for housing, health insurance, schools for children, and transportation while revenue builds. E-2 dependents can generally study, and spouses are authorized to work incident to status.

## Renewal costs

E-2 visas are issued for a period set by reciprocity with your country, and E-2 status is granted in increments of up to two years at entry. You will pay for renewals, which require showing the business is operating as planned. Factor renewal fees and legal costs into your multi-year budget.

## How to keep E-2 costs under control

- Confirm eligibility, especially nationality and source of funds, before spending on a business.
- Use contracts that make the purchase or lease contingent on visa approval where possible.
- Choose professionals experienced with E-2 cases to avoid refusals.
- Keep receipts and bank statements for every dollar.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I count legal fees toward my E-2 investment?
Generally no. Legal and immigration fees are personal costs of obtaining the visa, not investments in the business. Business formation costs and operating expenses are treated differently.

### Is an E-2 cheaper than an EB-5?
Usually, in up-front investment. But E-2 requires repeated renewals and does not produce a green card, so long-term costs and outcomes differ.

### Can I use a loan for the E-2 investment?
Only if the loan is not secured by the business's own assets. A loan secured by personal assets, such as your home, can count.

### Do I pay the reciprocity fee every renewal?
Reciprocity fees are charged when a visa is issued, so they recur with each new visa.

### Can I invest gradually after approval?
No. The investment must be substantially in place, or irrevocably committed, before you apply.

### Do I need to keep the business plan updated?
Yes. Renewals compare actual performance against the original plan, so track hiring and revenue.

### Should I budget for health insurance?
Yes. E-2 holders do not have access to most public benefits, and private health insurance is a significant cost for many families.

### Does it cost more to buy an existing business?
Often, because of due diligence, valuation, and contract work. It can also make the case stronger because the business already operates.
