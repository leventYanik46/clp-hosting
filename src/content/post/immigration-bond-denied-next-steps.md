A bond denial feels final because the person stays locked up, but the law gives a detained person four routes forward: an appeal to the Board of Immigration Appeals, a new request when circumstances change, a release request to ICE itself, and a habeas corpus petition in federal court. Which one fits depends first on why bond was denied. If your family member's immigration bond was denied, here is how to read the denial and what each option involves.

## First, understand why the immigration bond was denied

Immigration judges deny bond for two very different reasons, and they call for different responses. The first is a finding that the judge lacks jurisdiction: the person is held under INA 236(c) because of a criminal ground, or is treated as an applicant for admission under INA 235(b), or has a final removal order. In those cases the judge never weighed danger or flight risk, and the response is a legal challenge to the custody classification. The second is a discretionary denial under INA 236(a): the judge had authority but found the person to be a danger or a flight risk, or set a bond so high the family cannot pay it. The response there is better evidence or a reviewing body that sees the facts differently. The judge announces the reason orally; the written reasons come in a bond memorandum only if someone appeals. Our [immigration bond practice](/practice-area/key-services/immigration-detention-and-bond) begins by pinning down which kind of denial occurred, because an appeal of a jurisdictional ruling is argued very differently from an appeal of a discretionary one.

## Route one: appeal to the Board of Immigration Appeals

Either party may appeal a bond decision to the Board by filing Form EOIR-26 within 30 days of the decision. The bond appeal is separate from any appeal in the removal case and has its own record. After the notice of appeal is filed, the immigration judge prepares a bond memorandum explaining the reasons for the decision, and the Board sets a briefing schedule. The Board reviews the judge's factual findings for clear error and legal conclusions, including the custody classification, without deference. A bond appeal takes months, and the person remains detained unless released by another route in the meantime. The appeal is most valuable where the judge made a legal error about jurisdiction or applied the wrong standard, and least valuable where the dispute is about how to weigh a sponsor letter. Our [BIA appeals and motions](/practice-area/key-services/bia-appeals-and-motions) team files these appeals routinely, often alongside one of the other routes below.

## Route two: a new bond request based on changed circumstances

The regulations allow a second custody redetermination request when circumstances have changed materially since the first hearing. The change must be documented and new. Examples that have reopened custody include approval of a Form I-130 filed by a citizen spouse, a conviction that was vacated or reduced, a new sponsor with a stable address after the original sponsor fell through, a serious medical diagnosis, completion of a treatment program, or the grant of an application that shows the person now has a clear path to status. Length of detention alone is sometimes argued as a changed circumstance, with mixed results. The request is made by written motion to the immigration court, with the new evidence attached, and the hearing that follows looks like the first one, described in our post on [what happens at a bond hearing](/ice-detention-bond-hearing-explained).

## Route three: ask ICE directly

The immigration judge is not the only decision-maker. ICE has its own authority to release people on recognizance, on an order of supervision, or on parole. For people held under INA 235(b), including arriving asylum seekers who passed a credible fear interview, parole from ICE is the primary release mechanism, and the request goes to the deportation officer and the field office with evidence of identity, a sponsor and community ties. For people with serious medical conditions, pregnant detainees, primary caregivers of U.S. citizen children and others whose detention serves little purpose, a written humanitarian release request can succeed where a judge's hands were tied. ICE may also place a released person on Alternatives to Detention, such as telephone check-ins or an ankle monitor. These requests are discretionary and often denied, but they cost nothing to make and build a record that a federal court will later see.

## Route four: habeas corpus in federal court

A petition for a writ of habeas corpus under 28 U.S.C. 2241 asks a federal district judge to review the lawfulness of the detention. It is filed in the district where the person is confined, against the warden and ICE officials. Habeas is the main tool when the immigration judge found no jurisdiction to set bond: a petitioner can argue that they are in fact held under INA 236(a) and entitled to a hearing, that the criminal ground relied upon for INA 236(c) does not fit their conviction, or that detention has lasted so long without a hearing that due process requires one. As of this writing, many federal courts have ordered bond hearings for long-term residents the government classified under INA 235(b), while others have agreed with the government, so the outcome depends heavily on the district of confinement. Courts that grant relief typically order a bond hearing within a set number of days, often with the burden placed on the government to justify continued detention. Our [habeas corpus](/practice-area/key-services/habeas-corpus) practice handles these petitions, the jurisdiction scenarios are broken down in [when a judge refuses to hold a bond hearing](/bond-hearing-denied-habeas-corpus), and the classification issues that drive them are explained in our post on [mandatory detention under INA 236(c)](/mandatory-detention-ina-236c).

## Meanwhile: win the case itself

Detained removal cases move on an accelerated docket, with individual hearings scheduled in weeks rather than years. For some clients the fastest way out of detention is a grant of asylum, cancellation of removal or adjustment of status by the immigration judge, which ends the case and the custody at once. While the bond fight continues, the application for relief should be prepared with the same urgency, which is how our [removal defense](/practice-area/key-services/removal-defense) team approaches every detained matter. A denial of bond is a setback in a case that still has several moves left.
