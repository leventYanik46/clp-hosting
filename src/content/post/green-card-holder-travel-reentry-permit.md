Green card holders can travel freely, but permanent residence assumes the U.S. is your permanent home. Long or frequent absences can lead officers to conclude you have abandoned residence. How long a green card holder can stay outside the U.S. depends on the length of the trip, your ties here, and whether you planned ahead with a reentry permit.

## Trips under six months

Short trips rarely cause trouble at the border. Bring your green card and a valid passport. Even short trips can add up for naturalization purposes, so keep track.

## Trips between six months and one year

You can usually reenter with your green card, but CBP may ask questions about why you were gone and whether you still live in the U.S. Be ready to show ties: a home or lease, a job, tax returns filed as a resident, bank accounts, and family here.

Absences over six months can also break continuous residence for naturalization, unless you can show you did not abandon residence.

## Trips over one year

A green card alone is generally not enough to reenter after more than a year abroad. Without a reentry permit, you may be treated as having abandoned residence. Absences of a year or more also break continuous residence for citizenship.

## How a reentry permit works

A reentry permit, requested on Form I-131, lets a permanent resident stay abroad for up to two years and return without the green card alone being questioned on abandonment grounds. It does not guarantee admission, but it is strong evidence that you intended to keep your residence.

Requirements to know:

- **You must be physically in the U.S. when you file.**
- **You must attend biometrics in the U.S.**, so plan to stay until that appointment or return for it.
- You can ask that the permit be sent to a U.S. consulate or USCIS office abroad for pickup.

## Reentry permit processing time

Processing can take several months. Check USCIS processing times for Form I-131 before planning. Since you can leave after biometrics, many people file well before the trip.

## Green card holder travel tips

- File U.S. tax returns as a resident while abroad. Filing as a nonresident can be used as evidence of abandonment.
- Keep a U.S. address, bank account, and driver's license.
- Keep evidence of why you are abroad, such as caring for a sick parent or a temporary work assignment.
- Carry your green card and passport on every trip.

## The SB-1 returning resident visa

If you stayed abroad longer than one year without a reentry permit, or beyond the permit's validity, for reasons outside your control, you may apply for an SB-1 returning resident visa at a U.S. consulate. You must show:

- You were a lawful permanent resident when you left
- You left with the intention of returning and kept that intention
- The extended stay was beyond your control, such as a medical emergency or travel restrictions

You file Form DS-117 and attend an interview, and may need a new medical exam. SB-1 approvals are discretionary and can be hard to get. If refused, other options may include a new immigrant petition or, in some cases, arguing at the border.


## Frequently asked questions

### Can I renew a reentry permit from abroad?

No. You must be in the U.S. to file a new reentry permit.

### Does a reentry permit protect my citizenship timeline?

No. It helps with reentry, but long absences still break continuous residence for naturalization unless you file Form N-470 for certain qualifying work abroad.

### Can I keep my green card if I work abroad for a U.S. company?

Possibly. A temporary assignment abroad with clear ties to the U.S. is a recognized reason for absence. A reentry permit is strongly recommended for assignments longer than a few months.
