Yes, if you married the child's parent before the child turned 18. Immigration law treats a stepchild as your child, with the same rights as a biological child, whenever the marriage that created the step relationship took place before the stepchild's 18th birthday. If the wedding happened after that birthday, there is no step relationship under the Immigration and Nationality Act, however close you are, and you cannot file for that child. CLP's [stepchild and child green card](/practice-area/key-services/child-green-card) practice sees this rule decide cases every month. Here is how it works and what USCIS wants to see.

## The before-18 rule for a stepchild green card

Section 101(b)(1)(B) of the INA defines a child to include a stepchild, whether or not born out of wedlock, as long as the child was under 18 when the marriage creating the step relationship took place. Two things follow. First, the date that matters is the date of your marriage, not the date you file the petition. A stepchild who was 17 at the wedding and is 20 when you file still qualifies. Second, there is no exception for a long relationship. A stepparent who raised a child from age 12 but married the parent when the child was 18 and a month has no petition to file.

The scenario we see most often: a U.S. citizen marries someone abroad whose children are 15, 17 and 19. The spouse is an immediate relative. The 15- and 17-year-olds are stepchildren and get their own immediate relative petitions. The 19-year-old is not a stepchild. That child's route is through the parent, once the parent is a permanent resident, as an F2A beneficiary with a priority date wait. If the petitioner is a permanent resident rather than a citizen, the spouse's petition is itself an F2A case and the 19-year-old can ride along as a derivative without being a stepchild at all, as our post on the [F2A category for a permanent resident's child](/f2a-green-card-child-of-permanent-resident) explains.

## You petition in your own right

A stepparent files Form I-130 as the petitioner. The child's biological parent does not need to be a citizen or permanent resident, and the biological parent's own case does not need to be finished first. The stepchild's category follows your status: if you are a U.S. citizen, an unmarried stepchild under 21 is an immediate relative (IR-2 at a consulate), and if you are a permanent resident the stepchild is in F2A.

Each stepchild needs a separate I-130. Immediate relative petitions carry no derivatives, so a citizen sponsoring a spouse and two stepchildren files three petitions, each with its own fee and evidence. The Child Status Protection Act freezes an immediate relative stepchild's age on the day USCIS receives the petition, so file before any 21st birthday; our post on [how the CSPA age calculation works](/cspa-age-out-calculation-explained) covers what happens if you cannot. The form itself is covered in [what Form I-130 is and how it works](/what-is-form-i-130-2025-update).

## Evidence USCIS expects

The petition has to prove three things: your status, a valid marriage to the child's parent, and the parent-child relationship between your spouse and the child. The documents that do that:

- Proof you are a U.S. citizen or permanent resident (passport, naturalization certificate, birth certificate, or green card).
- Your marriage certificate, with a certified translation if needed.
- Proof that every prior marriage of yours and of your spouse ended: divorce decrees, annulments or death certificates. A missing decree is the most common Request for Evidence in stepchild cases.
- The stepchild's birth certificate naming your spouse as a parent. If your spouse is the father and the child was born out of wedlock, add legitimation or relationship evidence for that link.
- Evidence that your marriage is genuine: a joint lease or deed, joint accounts, insurance, photographs, affidavits. The step relationship rests on the marriage, so USCIS looks at the marriage even in a petition for the child.

Evidence of your own relationship with the stepchild is not required while the marriage is intact, but it helps, and it becomes essential if the marriage later ends.

## Divorce, separation, or the death of the parent

A stepchild petition does not automatically die with the marriage. The Board of Immigration Appeals has long held that a step relationship can survive divorce or the death of the biological parent for immigration purposes if the stepparent and stepchild continue a genuine family relationship. That is a fact-specific inquiry: USCIS looks at whether you still support the child, live with or visit the child, and act as a parent. If the marriage ends and contact ends with it, the step relationship ends too. If you are in this situation, gather proof of the continuing relationship before you file or respond to any notice.

## After approval: adjustment or a visa, and the citizenship gap

A stepchild who is in the United States after a lawful admission, with a U.S. citizen stepparent, can usually file Form I-485 together with the I-130 and adjust status here. A stepchild abroad completes the case through the National Visa Center and a consular interview; our [family reunification](/practice-area/key-services/family-reunification) page covers that stage. If the other biological parent is alive and not immigrating, expect the consulate to ask about custody and consent for a minor.

One difference from a biological child matters later. The automatic citizenship rule in section 320 of the INA, which makes a child under 18 a citizen when the child becomes a permanent resident in a citizen parent's custody, does not apply to stepchildren, because the citizenship definition of "child" excludes them. A stepchild who immigrates at 15 is a permanent resident, not a citizen, and will later apply for naturalization on Form N-400 in their own right. Our [citizenship and naturalization](/practice-area/key-services/citizenship-and-naturalization) page explains that path.
