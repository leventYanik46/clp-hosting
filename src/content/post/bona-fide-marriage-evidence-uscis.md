A marriage certificate proves you married. It does not prove why. USCIS approves a spousal petition only when the record shows the couple intended to build a life together, and the officer decides that from documents, not from how nice the wedding looked. Strong bona fide marriage evidence shows a shared life across time and across categories: money, housing, family, insurance, and the ordinary paper trail two people generate when they actually live together.

## What bona fide marriage evidence has to show

The legal question is whether the marriage was entered in good faith at the time it took place. USCIS looks for the markers that fraud cases lack: mingled finances, a common residence, each spouse named in the other's important paperwork, and people outside the couple who know them as married. The regulations for Form I-130 name several categories outright: joint ownership of property, a shared lease, commingled financial resources, birth certificates of children born to the marriage and affidavits from third parties. Our [marriage-based green card](/practice-area/key-services/marriage-based-green-card) practice builds files around those categories first and then adds what is specific to the couple.

Two cautions before the checklist. First, volume is not strength. Three hundred photographs with no joint bank statement raise more questions than they answer. Second, the evidence should cover the whole period from the wedding to the filing, and later to the interview. A joint lease signed the week before filing looks like what it is.

## The documents that carry the most weight

Financial and housing records come first because they are hard to fake and easy to verify.

- Joint bank or credit union statements showing both names and real activity over months, not a single opening deposit.
- Jointly filed federal and state tax returns, or, if you filed separately, returns showing the same address and the married filing status.
- A lease, deed or mortgage with both names, plus renter's or homeowner's insurance listing both spouses.
- Health, dental, auto or life insurance that names the other spouse as a dependent or beneficiary, and retirement or employer benefit designations.
- Utility, phone or internet bills in both names at the shared address, or in one name with the other spouse's mail delivered there.
- Birth certificates of children born to or adopted into the marriage.

Where a category is missing, explain why in a short cover note rather than leaving a gap. A couple that keeps separate accounts for cultural or practical reasons can still show shared expenses through transfers and receipts.

## Photographs, messages and affidavits

Photographs belong in the file, but curated. Choose a modest set that spans time and places and includes family and friends: the wedding, holidays, trips, ordinary evenings at home. Label each with the date, place and who appears. Messages, call logs and travel itineraries matter most for couples who spent time apart, because they show the relationship continuing during separation.

Affidavits from people who know you as a couple carry weight when they are specific. A good affidavit says who the writer is, how they know the couple, how often they see them and what they have observed: the Sunday dinners, the move into the apartment, the hospital visit. It is signed, dated and includes contact information. A one-paragraph form letter from six friends saying the marriage is real adds little.

## Evidence for the harder fact patterns

Some marriages are genuine and still look thin on paper. Couples who married quickly after meeting, couples with a large age gap, couples who do not share a language fluently, couples who married while one spouse was in removal proceedings, and couples where one spouse travels for work all draw closer review.

The answer is the same in each case: document the actual life. If you married fast, show how the relationship developed and why the timing made sense. If you live apart for work, show the travel between homes, the shared finances and the plan to live together. If the marriage took place after a Notice to Appear was issued, the law requires clear and convincing evidence, and the file should read like one prepared for a judge. If you are in that situation, our [family-based immigration](/practice-area/key-services/family-based-immigration) team coordinates the petition with removal defense counsel.

Keep in mind that USCIS also checks the petitioner's and the beneficiary's prior petitions. An earlier spousal petition for someone else is not disqualifying on its own, but it will be in the officer's file, and the current petition should account for it.

## Keep collecting after you file

The evidence job does not end when the I-130 and I-485 go in the mail. Bring updated documents to the interview: the most recent statements, the current lease, new photographs, anything that happened since filing. The officer will ask about the shared home and daily routines, and the interview questions track the documents; our post on [marriage-based adjustment of status step by step](/marriage-based-adjustment-of-status-steps) explains what that appointment looks like. If the marriage is under two years old when residence is granted, you will do all of this again when you file Form I-751, which we cover in [when to file Form I-751](/form-i-751-removal-of-conditions-timing). Start a folder now and keep adding to it; the same categories apply.
