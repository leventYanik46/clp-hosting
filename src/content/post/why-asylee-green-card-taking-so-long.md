If your asylee green card is taking so long that the USCIS processing-times page now allows a case inquiry, the delay almost always traces to one of five causes, and each has a different fix. Some are cured by sending a document. Some are cured by waiting. One or two are warning signs that the asylum grant itself is under review. Our [green card through asylum](/practice-area/key-services/green-card-through-asylum) practice handles stalled Form I-485 cases for asylees as a distinct service, because the right move depends on diagnosing which cause you are facing, and the wrong move, such as suing before the file is clean, can convert a delay into a denial.

## The five usual causes of an asylee green card delay

- Background and security checks. Fingerprint results, FBI name checks, and interagency checks run on every applicant, and they take longer for common names, prior aliases, travel to certain countries, or any record that needs manual resolution. This is the most frequent cause and the one you can do least about.
- A review of the asylum file. USCIS compares the I-485 with the I-589, the declaration, and the interview record. A date that does not match, a trip abroad that was not disclosed, a new passport, or a change in country conditions can send the file to the asylum office for a termination review, which is slow and serious.
- A missing or deficient Form I-693. An examination not submitted with the application, a civil surgeon's error, or an expired form produces a request for evidence and, until it is answered, nothing moves.
- Field office workload. Asylee adjustments are often adjudicated at the local field office, and the interview queue and officer availability vary by office. Cases also stall when the file is in transit between the National Benefits Center and the field office.
- An unresolved issue in your record. A pending criminal charge, an unanswered question about a derivative relationship, or an inadmissibility ground that needs a Form I-602 waiver will hold the case until it is resolved.

## Reading the signals

The case status page and your mail tell you which cause is likely. A request for evidence means a content problem, and the fix is a complete response, by the deadline, that addresses every item. An interview notice means the case is moving and the file is being read closely; prepare as you would for the original asylum interview, with the I-589 in hand, because the officer will ask about it. A notice to appear at the asylum office, or a notice of intent to terminate, means the grant is under review, and that notice needs a lawyer the day it arrives. A status that has not changed in many months, with no mail, usually means security checks or a file that has not reached the right desk. A biometrics notice that never arrived, or that arrived for a date you missed, means the case may already be treated as abandoned, and a reschedule request should go in immediately.

Three habits keep a slow case from becoming a lost one. Keep your address current with USCIS on Form AR-11 and with your attorney. Do not travel without a valid refugee travel document, and do not travel to your home country at all. And keep your asylum-based employment authorization renewed, because a pending I-485 does not by itself keep you working unless you filed Form I-765 with it.

## The inquiry tools, in order

Start with the USCIS processing-times page for Form I-485 at your field office. Once your case is older than the posted time, submit a case inquiry through your USCIS account; the response is usually a form letter, but it creates a record. An expedite request is available for defined reasons, such as severe financial loss, a humanitarian emergency, or a USCIS error, and must be documented; an ordinary long wait does not qualify. The CIS Ombudsman accepts requests for case assistance on Form DHS-7001 for cases outside normal processing, and a member of Congress's office can submit a congressional inquiry on your behalf after you sign a privacy release. Each step takes weeks to produce an answer. Keep copies of every inquiry and every response, because they become exhibits if the case ends up in federal court.

## When a mandamus lawsuit makes sense

A mandamus action under 28 U.S.C. 1361, usually paired with a claim under the Administrative Procedure Act for unreasonable delay, asks a federal judge to order USCIS to decide the application. It does not ask the judge to approve it. Courts weigh the length of the delay against the agency's competing priorities, the effect on your health and welfare, and whether any statute sets a timetable, under a framework known as the TRAC factors. For an asylee whose status can be terminated at any time and whose citizenship clock is waiting on the green card, the human cost of delay is a strong argument, and in practice the government often adjudicates the application after the complaint is served rather than defend the delay.

The risk is that a decision can be a denial. Before filing suit, the case must be clean: the I-693 current, any waiver filed, the travel history complete, and the I-485 consistent with the asylum file. ## What not to do while you wait

Do not file a second I-485 to see whether it moves faster; it creates two files that must be reconciled and can lead to both being denied. Do not respond to a request for evidence with part of what was asked. Do not miss a biometrics or interview appointment, and if you must, reschedule in writing before the date. Do not renew your home-country passport without advice, and do not visit your home country. And do not assume a long delay means a problem with your claim; a slow case is usually a slow case. The one-year rollback of an asylee's permanent residence date applies however long the case took, but a later approval still means a later residence date and a later citizenship date, which is one more reason to press for a decision.
