To become a conservator for a parent, you petition the court where your parent lives, prove by clear and convincing evidence that they can no longer manage their own property, show that you are a suitable person to do it, and then qualify by taking an oath and posting a bond. That is the legal skeleton. The practical work, which decides whether the case takes one hearing or six, happens before you file: understanding what your parent owns, finding out whether a power of attorney already exists, and getting your siblings on the same page. Here is the sequence in Virginia, Washington, DC, and New York, where the same role is called guardian of the property.

## Before you file

Start with the documents. Look for a durable power of attorney, a revocable trust, and any advance directive. If a valid, broad power of attorney names you as agent and the bank honors it, you may not need a conservatorship at all. If it names someone else, that person has priority and the court will want to know why they are not acting. If a trust holds most assets, the successor trustee controls them, and a conservatorship may only be needed for what sits outside the trust.

Then build the financial picture: accounts, income sources, real estate, debts, insurance, and tax filings. The petition will ask for an estimate of the estate's value, the bond will be sized to it, and the inventory you file after appointment must match it. Finally, talk to your parent if they can participate, and talk to your siblings. A contested petition costs several times what an agreed one does, and judges notice when a family arrives united.

## Who the court will appoint

Courts choose the conservator; petitioners only propose. The statutes set priorities, and the general pattern is: a person nominated by your parent in a power of attorney or other writing, then a spouse, then an adult child, then other relatives, then a professional or public conservator. Within a priority level, the court picks the most suitable person. A nomination in your parent's power of attorney is the strongest card you can hold, and a sibling who objects to you will have to show good cause to overcome it.

Suitability questions cut the other way. If you owe your parent money, are a beneficiary in a disputed will, have a bankruptcy or a conviction involving dishonesty, or live far away, expect those facts to be raised. They are not automatic disqualifiers, but you should address them in the petition rather than let the guardian ad litem discover them. ## The petition, notice, and hearing

The petition itself follows the same path as a guardianship, which we walk through in [how to file for guardianship of an adult](/how-to-file-adult-guardianship-petition). You will need a written capacity evaluation that speaks specifically to financial judgment: can your parent understand a bank statement, recognize a scam, remember which bills are due. Virginia requires the evaluation report before the hearing; New York appoints a court evaluator after filing; DC appoints an examiner.

Your parent must be personally served and has the right to counsel and to object. Siblings and other close relatives get notice by mail. At the hearing, the judge decides incapacity, decides whether a limited order would suffice, and decides who serves. If personal decisions are also at issue, the same petition can request a [guardianship for medical and living decisions](/practice-area/key-services/legal-guardianship), though you should ask for both only when the evidence supports both.

## Qualifying: oath, bond, and letters

An order appointing you is not yet authority. You qualify by appearing before the clerk, taking an oath to perform the duties faithfully, and posting a bond. The bond is a surety policy that protects your parent's estate if you mismanage it; its size tracks the value of the assets, and the premium is paid from the estate. Courts can reduce or waive the surety in some circumstances, for example when your parent's power of attorney asks them to, but do not assume it. Once the bond is accepted, the clerk issues the certificate of qualification or letters that banks, brokerages, and the pension administrator will require. New York adds a training course and a commission to the guardian of the property.

## Your first months after you become conservator

The first ninety to one hundred twenty days set the tone for everything that follows.

- Open a dedicated conservatorship account in your parent's name with you as conservator. Never run money through your own accounts.
- Present your letters to every institution, retitle accounts, and redirect income. Apply separately to the Social Security Administration to become representative payee; a court order alone does not accomplish that.
- Inventory everything. Virginia conservators file the inventory with the commissioner of accounts within four months of qualification; DC and New York set their own deadlines, both measured in months. Get date-of-qualification values for every asset.
- Set up recordkeeping from day one: a ledger, receipts for every payment, and statements for every account. The annual accounting is built from these, as described in [what a conservator's annual accounting must show](/conservator-annual-accounting-requirements).
- Learn the approval list. Selling the house, making gifts, paying yourself, and settling claims generally need advance court permission.

What you may not do is as important as what you must. No commingling, no loans to family, no changes to your parent's will or beneficiary designations, no spending on your own household even if your parent would have wanted it. Each of these is grounds for removal and surcharge, and each shows up in an accounting.
