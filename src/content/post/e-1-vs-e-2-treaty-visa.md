The E-1 vs E-2 choice is simpler than it looks, because the two visas share almost every rule except one. The E-1 treaty trader visa asks whether your company already carries on substantial trade, mostly with your treaty country. The E-2 treaty investor visa asks whether you have made a substantial, at-risk investment in a U.S. business you will develop and direct. Same treaty nationality test, same two-year admissions, same rights for spouses and children. So the real question is which record you can prove more cleanly today, and which one will hold up at renewal. Our [E-1 trade-based visa](/practice-area/key-services/e-1-visa) page covers the trade side in depth.

## What the E-1 and E-2 share

Both visas require treaty nationality for the applicant and for the company. At least 50 percent of the U.S. enterprise must be owned by nationals of the treaty country who live abroad or hold E status in the United States. Both are applied for at a consulate with Forms DS-160 and DS-156E, or through a change of status on Form I-129 if you are already in the United States in valid status.

Both give a two-year admission on each entry, with no limit on extensions while the business qualifies. Both let the company bring same-nationality staff in executive, supervisory, or essential roles. In both, the spouse is employment authorized incident to status and the children can study. And in both, you must intend to depart when your status ends, which does not stop you from pursuing a green card later but does require planning.

## The core difference: trade versus investment

The E-1 tests trade. Your company must already exchange goods, services, or technology across the border for payment, in a continuous flow with numerous transactions, and more than half of its international trade must be with the treaty country. No investment is required. You could run a lean import company out of a leased warehouse with modest capital and qualify, if the trade is there.

The E-2 tests investment. You must have committed funds to the business, in an amount that is substantial relative to its total cost, and the business must not be marginal. Trade is irrelevant. A business that has not opened yet can qualify, if the money is spent or irrevocably committed and the plan shows growth. Our [E-2 treaty investor visa](/practice-area/key-services/e-2-visa) page describes the investment test in full.

## Which visa is easier to document

A new venture almost always points to the E-2. The E-1 requires existing trade, so a company that has not yet shipped or billed anything cannot use it.

An established importer or exporter with years of steady volume often points to the E-1. It avoids the E-2's source-of-funds tracing, which can be the hardest part of an investor case when money came from many places over many years.

Service companies can go either way. A consulting or engineering firm whose U.S. office bills clients in the treaty country may meet the E-1 if most of its cross-border revenue comes from that country. If its international work is spread across many countries, the principal trade test may fail, and the E-2 becomes the safer route. Our explainer on [what counts as substantial trade for an E-1](/substantial-trade-e-1-visa) covers how that share is calculated.

## Check the treaty list for both

The two visas rest on different treaty provisions, and the State Department lists them separately. Some countries qualify for the E-2 but not the E-1, and a few the other way around. Confirm your country's eligibility for the specific visa before you build the case. If your country appears on neither list, neither visa is available, and the realistic options shift to routes like the L-1A or the EB-5.

## When your company qualifies for both

Some companies both invest and trade. A family manufacturer that sets up a U.S. distribution subsidiary may have invested heavily in the subsidiary and also ship steadily to it. In that case, pick the visa whose record is stronger now and less likely to weaken.

Think about renewal risk. An E-1 renewal can fail if the trade mix shifts, for example if a new supplier in a third country grows to rival the treaty-country supplier. An E-2 renewal can fail if the business has not grown beyond marginality, or if the original investment has been withdrawn. Pick the risk you can control. Moving from one E classification to the other later requires a new application, so it is better to choose carefully at the start.

## Neither one is a green card

Both E visas are nonimmigrant. Neither converts into permanent residence, no matter how many times you renew. Many E-1 and E-2 holders later pursue the EB-1C as multinational managers, the EB-2 National Interest Waiver, or the EB-5 investor green card. Our post on [how E-2 investors move to a green card](/e-2-visa-to-green-card-paths) maps those routes, and most of it applies to E-1 traders as well.
