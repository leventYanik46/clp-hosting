What is an R-1 visa? It is a temporary work visa for ministers and other religious workers coming to work for a nonprofit religious organization in the U.S. Churches, mosques, temples, synagogues, and religious schools use the R-1 to bring clergy and staff whose work is rooted in their faith. The rules are specific, and USCIS takes fraud prevention in this category seriously.

## Who qualifies for an R-1 visa

The worker must meet four requirements:

1. **Membership.** The worker must have been a member of the same religious denomination as the U.S. employer for at least two years immediately before the petition is filed.
2. **Qualifying role.** The worker must be coming as a minister, or to work in a religious vocation or religious occupation.
3. **Hours.** The position must be at least 20 hours per week on average.
4. **Qualifying employer.** The employer must be a bona fide nonprofit religious organization in the U.S., or an organization affiliated with one.

## Ministers, vocations, and occupations

**Ministers** are individuals authorized by a recognized denomination to conduct religious worship and perform other duties usually performed by clergy. Evidence usually includes ordination certificates and proof of the denomination's requirements.

**Religious vocations** involve a lifelong commitment, such as nuns, monks, and religious brothers and sisters.

**Religious occupations** are roles primarily related to religious beliefs, such as religious instructors, cantors, missionaries, and liturgical workers. Administrative and support jobs, like janitors, fundraisers, and clerical staff, don't qualify even when the employer is religious.

## The R-1 petition process

Every R-1 case now requires the employer to file Form I-129 with USCIS. Since 2008, religious workers can't apply directly at a consulate without an approved petition.

The petition includes:

- Proof of the employer's tax-exempt status, usually an IRS determination letter (or proof of membership in a group exemption)
- An attestation signed by an authorized official describing the job, the compensation, and the worker's qualifications
- Evidence of the worker's denomination membership for the past two years
- Evidence of how the worker will be compensated, such as salary or room and board, and how the employer will pay for it

## The site inspection

USCIS routinely conducts site inspections for R-1 petitions, often before approval. An officer may visit the place of worship, review the facilities, interview staff, and confirm the organization exists and the job is real. Make sure the address on the petition is accurate, the people listed are reachable, and the organization's records are in order.

## Length of stay

R-1 status is granted for up to 30 months initially. It can be extended for up to another 30 months, for a total maximum of five years. After five years, the worker must leave the U.S. and remain abroad for at least one year before returning in R-1 status, unless the time was spent intermittently.

## Premium processing

Premium processing is available for R-1 petitions, with a 15 business day decision timeframe. The premium processing fee for R-1 petitions is lower than for most other I-129 categories, at $1,780 as of March 1, 2026. Premium processing doesn't skip the site inspection, though. USCIS can still conduct a visit.

## R-2 dependents

The R-1 worker's spouse and unmarried children under 21 can accompany them in R-2 status. R-2 dependents can attend school, but they can't work in the U.S.

## Paths to a green card

Religious workers who want to stay permanently may qualify for an EB-4 special immigrant religious worker green card. That category has its own requirements and has faced visa backlogs, so planning early is important if permanent residence is the goal.

## Common mistakes

- Treating an administrative role as a religious occupation
- Gaps in the two-year membership record
- Compensation plans that don't show how the worker will be supported
- Inaccurate addresses or contacts that make the site visit fail

## Compensation: the detail USCIS looks at closely

The petition must explain exactly how the worker will be supported. That can be a salary, or non-salaried support such as housing, food, and medical care, which is common for religious vocations. USCIS expects evidence that the organization has done this before, such as past payroll records, IRS documentation, or budgets showing room and board for members. If the worker will be self-supporting, the petition must show the role is part of an established missionary program. Vague promises of support are among the most frequent reasons for Requests for Evidence in R-1 cases.

## Changing employers

R-1 status is tied to the petitioning organization. A religious worker who moves to a different congregation, even within the same denomination, needs a new petition from the new employer before starting work there. Starting at the new location first is a status violation, and it can complicate a later green card case.
