Doctors are allowed to be wrong. A misdiagnosis becomes medical malpractice only when the provider reached the wrong answer by failing to do what a careful colleague would have done with the same patient and the same information, and when the resulting delay made the outcome worse. That second part is where most delayed diagnosis claims are decided, and it is the part patients rarely think about when they learn that a symptom they reported a year ago was the first sign of the disease they now have. CLP's [medical negligence practice](/practice-area/key-services/medical-negligence) screens diagnosis cases on both questions, because an obvious error with no provable consequence is not a case.

## The misdiagnosis test: what a careful provider would have done with the same information

The standard is not whether the diagnosis was correct but whether the process was. Providers are expected to take a history, examine the patient, build a list of possible explanations (the differential diagnosis), order the tests that would distinguish among them, follow up on the results, and refer when the problem is outside their competence. A diagnosis that was wrong despite that process is usually defensible. A diagnosis that was wrong because the process was skipped is not.

Typical breaches look like this: a patient with chest pain and risk factors sent home from the emergency department without an ECG and cardiac enzymes; a lump that was felt but never imaged; a positive test result that was filed without anyone calling the patient; a radiologist's report that flagged a finding no one acted on; a child with fever and a stiff neck treated for a virus without a lumbar puncture. In each, the standard of care called for a specific next step, and it was not taken.

The analysis has to resist hindsight. The question is what the provider knew at the visit, not what the later scan showed. Experts in the same specialty make that judgment, as we explain in [how do you prove medical malpractice in court](/how-to-prove-medical-malpractice).

## The conditions behind most delayed diagnosis claims

Some conditions generate claims because they are both commonly missed and time-sensitive:

- Cancers, especially breast, colorectal, lung, cervical, and skin, where a missed screening result or an unimaged symptom delays treatment by months.
- Stroke and heart attack, where emergency departments attribute symptoms to anxiety, migraine, or indigestion.
- Sepsis and serious infections, including meningitis in children and post-surgical infections.
- Pulmonary embolism, aortic dissection, and appendicitis, which present with vague symptoms and kill when missed.
- Ectopic pregnancy and complications of pregnancy that were not monitored.

## Who missed it: the physician, the lab, the radiologist, or the system

A delayed diagnosis is often a chain failure rather than a single person's mistake. The lab reported a critical value to a fax machine. The radiologist's finding was in the body of the report but not the impression. The covering physician never saw the result because it was routed to a doctor on leave. The patient was discharged before the culture came back and no one followed up. Each link may belong to a different defendant: the physician, the practice group, the hospital, an outside lab or imaging company. Identifying every link requires the full record, including the audit trail showing who saw what and when, and it determines who gets sued.

## Causation: proving the delay changed the outcome

The defense in nearly every diagnosis case is that the delay did not matter. The cancer was already advanced. The stroke had already done its damage. The infection would have required the same surgery. To win, your expert must show that, more likely than not, a timely diagnosis would have produced a materially better result: a smaller surgery instead of a larger one, chemotherapy avoided, a limb saved, a survival chance that was probable rather than possible.

Staging is the usual battleground in cancer cases. If the expert can show the tumor was at an earlier stage when it should have been found, and that treatment at that stage would probably have been curative, causation is established. If the tumor was already metastatic, the claim may fail even though the error was clear. Jurisdictions differ on whether the loss of a less-than-probable chance of recovery is itself compensable, and that difference can decide a case that is otherwise identical in DC, Virginia, and New York. The general framework is in [what counts as medical negligence and what is a bad outcome](/medical-negligence-vs-bad-outcome).

## Deadlines written for diagnosis cases

Legislatures recognize that a missed diagnosis is often discovered long after the error. Virginia allows one year from the date a malignant tumor diagnosis is communicated to the patient for a claim that the provider negligently failed to diagnose it, within an outer limit of ten years from the act. New York's Lavern's Law allows a cancer misdiagnosis claim within two years and six months of discovery, capped at seven years. DC's general discovery rule covers all malpractice claims, including diagnosis errors. Outside those exceptions, the ordinary clocks apply and run from the error, not from the diagnosis, which is why a patient who has just learned the truth may already be near a deadline. The full set is in [medical malpractice statute of limitations in DC, VA, and NY](/medical-malpractice-deadlines-dc-va-ny).

## What to gather before a review

Collect the records from the visit where the symptom was first reported, not just from the provider who finally made the diagnosis, along with every test result and imaging study in between. Write down the timeline from memory while it is fresh: what you reported, what you were told, what follow-up was promised. If the delay ended in a death, the claim proceeds under our [wrongful death](/practice-area/key-services/wrongful-death) practice, with the same causation proof and a two-year clock from the date of death.
