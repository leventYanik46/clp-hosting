If an immigration judge or the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) decides against you, you may be able to file a motion to reconsider or a motion to reopen. They serve different purposes, and the deadlines are strict.

## Motion to reconsider

A motion to reconsider asks the judge or BIA to look again at the decision based on the same record. It argues the decision contained errors of law or fact.

**Requirements:**
- Identify specific errors of law or fact
- Be filed within **30 days** of the final order
- Generally only one motion to reconsider is allowed

Use a motion to reconsider when the judge misapplied the law, misread the evidence, or overlooked an argument.

## Motion to reopen

A motion to reopen asks to reopen the case to consider new facts or evidence.

**Requirements:**
- Present new, material evidence that wasn't available and couldn't have been discovered at the earlier hearing
- Be filed within **90 days** of the final order
- Generally only one motion to reopen is allowed

Use a motion to reopen when circumstances have changed, such as new eligibility for relief through marriage to a U.S. citizen, or new evidence.

## Exceptions to deadlines

**In absentia orders:** If you were ordered removed for missing a hearing, you can file a motion to reopen within 180 days by showing exceptional circumstances, or at any time if you didn't receive proper notice.

**Changed country conditions:** For asylum, a motion to reopen based on changed conditions in your home country isn't subject to the 90-day limit.

**Sua sponte reopening:** Judges and the BIA have limited discretion to reopen on their own motion.

**Equitable tolling:** Deadlines may be extended in some cases, such as ineffective assistance of counsel.

## Motion vs appeal

**Appeal to the BIA:** Filed within 30 days of an immigration judge decision. The BIA reviews the decision.

**Motion to reconsider:** Filed with the same decision-maker.

You can sometimes file both, but each has strategic implications.

## Stays of removal

Filing a motion doesn't automatically stop removal, except for some in absentia motions. You may need to request a stay.

## Common mistakes

- Missing deadlines
- Filing a motion to reconsider with new evidence
- Filing a motion to reopen without new evidence
- Not requesting a stay

## Practical example

An immigration judge denies a woman's cancellation of removal application after concluding her U.S. citizen son's medical condition doesn't meet the hardship standard. Three weeks later, his doctor diagnoses a more serious condition that requires ongoing treatment. A motion to reconsider wouldn't fit, because the judge's decision was based on the record at the time. A motion to reopen within 90 days, with the new diagnosis and medical records, is the right tool. If she also believes the judge applied the wrong legal standard, an appeal to the BIA within 30 days may be needed as well, filed in parallel.

## Filing fees and forms

Motions filed with the immigration court and the BIA usually require a filing fee or a fee waiver request. Check the current EOIR fee schedule, since several immigration court fees increased in 2025.

## Ineffective assistance of counsel

If you missed a deadline or lost your case because of a prior attorney's mistakes, a motion to reopen based on ineffective assistance of counsel may be possible. These motions generally require an affidavit describing the agreement with the prior attorney, notice to the prior attorney, and in many cases a complaint to the relevant bar authority. Courts take these motions seriously, but the procedural requirements must be followed closely.

## Number limits and exceptions

Because you generally get only one motion to reconsider and one motion to reopen, choose carefully. Filing a weak motion early can use up your only opportunity. Some motions, such as those based on changed country conditions for asylum, aren't subject to the number limits.
