Managerial capacity for an L-1A visa does not depend on a job title. A "General Manager" who spends the day answering customer emails, invoicing clients and running the warehouse is not a manager for immigration purposes, and USCIS will say so. What matters is how the employee spends the working day, who does the operational work, and whether the person exercises real authority. This is the single most litigated question in L-1A practice, so it pays to understand the definition before writing the job description.

## The legal definition of managerial capacity

The Immigration and Nationality Act defines managerial capacity as an assignment in which the employee primarily does four things:

- Manages the organization, or a department, subdivision, function or component of it.
- Supervises and controls the work of other supervisory, professional or managerial employees, or manages an essential function.
- Has authority to hire and fire, or recommend those and other personnel actions, if others are supervised; or functions at a senior level within the hierarchy or with respect to the function managed.
- Exercises discretion over the day-to-day operations of the activity or function.

The word "primarily" carries the weight. USCIS does not expect a manager to do no hands-on work at all, but most of the time must go to managing. A first-line supervisor does not qualify unless the people supervised are professionals, which USCIS generally reads as positions that require a bachelor's degree. Our [L-1A visa for executives and managers](/practice-area/key-services/l-1a-visa) page covers the parallel definition of executive capacity, which focuses on directing the organization and setting policy rather than supervising people.

## Personnel manager versus function manager

There are two ways to be a manager under the statute.

### Personnel manager

A personnel manager supervises a team, and that team includes supervisors, managers or professionals. Proof is concrete: an organizational chart with names, titles, education and duties for each subordinate, payroll records that show the people exist, and examples of the manager's hiring, firing or performance decisions. If subordinates are lower-level staff, the chart should show the professional or supervisory layer between them and the beneficiary.

### Function manager

A function manager manages an essential function, such as a product line, a regional sales strategy or a compliance program, without necessarily supervising a team. USCIS looks hard at these cases. The petition has to identify the function, explain why it is essential to the company, show that the beneficiary manages it at a senior level, and show who performs the routine work of the function. If the answer to that last question is "the beneficiary," the case usually fails. Staff in the U.S. company, contractors, or employees of the foreign parent who carry out the day-to-day tasks can help, as long as the record documents them.

## The small company problem

Most L-1A denials involve small U.S. offices. With two or three employees, USCIS asks a fair question: who is doing the selling, the bookkeeping and the customer service, if not the manager? The statute gives some protection. When staffing levels are used as a factor, USCIS must consider the reasonable needs of the organization in light of its overall purpose and stage of development. A company that outsources payroll, logistics and accounting to vendors can show those vendors as part of its operating structure.

That protection only works with evidence. Useful items include vendor contracts and invoices, a breakdown of the beneficiary's duties with the percentage of time spent on each, and an explanation of how the company's model works without a large in-house staff. Vague phrases like "oversees operations" and "ensures success" add nothing. Specific statements do: "approves the annual budget for the U.S. subsidiary," "hired and supervises the sales director and the controller," "sets pricing for the North American market."

## The role abroad must qualify too

The definition applies twice. The beneficiary's job at the foreign company, during the qualifying year, must have been managerial or executive, and so must the U.S. role. Many petitions spend pages on the U.S. job and a single paragraph on the foreign one. Treat the foreign role with the same detail: an organizational chart of the foreign entity, the people the beneficiary supervised there, and the decisions the beneficiary made.

If the employee's real value is deep product or process expertise rather than management, an [L-1B specialized knowledge petition](/practice-area/key-services/l-1b-visa) may be the better fit. Our comparison of [L-1A and L-1B visas](/l-1a-vs-l-1b-comparison) explains how to choose between them.

## Why this matters for the green card

The same definitions control the EB-1C multinational manager green card. A petition that clears L-1A review on a thin record can still fail at the I-140 stage, when USCIS looks at the U.S. company again with more time and a different officer. Building the managerial record carefully from the start makes the later [EB-1C petition requirements](/eb-1c-multinational-manager-requirements) easier to meet, and protects extensions in the meantime.
