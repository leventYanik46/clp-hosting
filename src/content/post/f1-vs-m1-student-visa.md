The F-1 visa is for academic study at a college, university, language program, or accredited high school. The M-1 visa is for vocational or technical training, such as flight school, culinary programs, or mechanical training. The two look similar on paper, but the M-1 is far more restrictive, and picking the wrong one can limit what you can do after graduation. A student visa lawyer will usually start by asking what you want to do once the program ends.

## F-1 vs. M-1 at a glance

**Program type.** F-1: academic degrees and programs. M-1: vocational and non-academic training.

**Length of stay.** F-1 students are admitted for "duration of status," meaning as long as they are enrolled and following the rules. M-1 students are admitted for a fixed period tied to the program, generally capped at one year at a time with limited extensions.

**On-campus work.** F-1 students can work on campus up to 20 hours a week during the school term. M-1 students cannot.

**Practical training.** F-1 students can use Curricular Practical Training during the program and up to 12 months of Optional Practical Training after each degree level, plus a 24-month extension for STEM degrees. M-1 students get practical training only after completing the program: one month for every four months of study, up to six months total.

**Changing status.** An M-1 student cannot change to F-1 inside the U.S. An M-1 student also cannot change to H-1B if the M-1 training helped qualify them for the H-1B job. F-1 students have far more flexibility.

## Which visa do you need?

The school decides. Schools certified by SEVP issue either a Form I-20 for F-1 or a Form I-20 for M-1 depending on the program. If you have a choice between an academic program and a vocational one, think about your post-program goals before you enroll.

## Applying for the visa

Both require a Form I-20, payment of the SEVIS I-901 fee, a DS-160, and an interview at a U.S. consulate in your country of nationality or residence. You must show that you have funds for tuition and living costs and that you intend to return home after your studies. Student visas are nonimmigrant visas, so immigrant intent at the interview can lead to a refusal.

## Paths from a student visa to a green card

There is no direct student-to-green-card route, but common paths include:

- **F-1 to OPT to H-1B to employment green card.** The most traveled route. OPT gives time to enter the H-1B lottery, and the H-1B allows dual intent so an employer can sponsor a green card.
- **F-1 to O-1 or EB-1A.** For students and recent graduates with exceptional achievements, such as significant publications or awards.
- **F-1 to EB-2 NIW.** For researchers and advanced-degree holders whose work has national importance. This does not require an employer.
- **Family-based paths.** Marriage to a U.S. citizen can lead to adjustment of status, but the relationship must be genuine and timing questions can arise if the student entered with nonimmigrant intent.

M-1 students have fewer options because of the restrictions on changing status. Some return home and pursue employment visas from abroad.

## Common mistakes

- Choosing an M-1 program without realizing it rules out an in-country switch to F-1 or many H-1B cases
- Working off campus without authorization
- Dropping below full-time enrollment without approval from the DSO
- Expressing an intent to stay permanently at a student visa interview


## Frequently asked questions

### Can F-1 students work off campus?

Only with authorization, such as CPT, OPT, or a severe economic hardship authorization. Unauthorized work can end your status and harm future applications.

### Can I study part time on an F-1?

Generally no. F-1 students must maintain full-time enrollment, with limited exceptions approved by the DSO, such as a reduced course load for medical reasons or a final term.

### Can I bring my family on a student visa?

Spouses and children can come in F-2 or M-2 status. They cannot work. F-2 children can attend school, and F-2 spouses can take limited avocational classes.
