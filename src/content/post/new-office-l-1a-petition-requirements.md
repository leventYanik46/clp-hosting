A new office L-1A petition is the tool a foreign company uses to send one of its executives or managers to the United States to start a subsidiary, affiliate or branch that has been doing business for less than a year. USCIS treats it differently from an ordinary L-1A: the approval is capped at one year, the evidence focuses on the plan rather than the track record, and the extension at the end of that year is where many companies run into trouble. If you are opening your first U.S. office, the way you set up the company in the months before filing decides most of the case.

## What makes a petition a new office case

The regulation defines a new office as a U.S. entity that has been doing business through a parent, branch, affiliate or subsidiary for less than one year. "Doing business" means the regular, systematic and continuous provision of goods or services, so a company that was incorporated eighteen months ago but only signed its first contract last month is still a new office. The label matters because it switches the standard of proof. Instead of showing that the U.S. operation already supports a managerial role, the petitioner has to show that it will within a year. Our [L-1A intracompany transfer visa](/practice-area/key-services/l-1a-visa) practice spends most of its new office work on that single forward-looking question.

Three things must be in place before the petition is filed. First, the company must have secured sufficient physical premises to house the new office. A signed lease or deed is the normal proof; a virtual office or a mailing address is usually not enough. Second, the transferee must have worked for the foreign qualifying entity for one continuous year in the three years before filing, in an executive or managerial capacity. Third, the U.S. entity must show that it will support an executive or managerial position within one year of approval. For that, USCIS looks at the proposed nature of the office, its scope, organizational structure and financial goals, the size of the U.S. investment, the foreign entity's ability to pay the transferee and to begin doing business in the United States, and the organizational structure of the foreign entity.

## The business plan is the petition

In an established office case, payroll records and organizational charts carry the argument. In a new office case, the business plan does. A plan that works for USCIS is specific about four things: what the U.S. company will sell, who it will hire and when, how the transferee's job will become primarily managerial once that staff is in place, and where the money comes from. A credible staffing timeline is the part most often missing. If the plan says the manager will supervise five professionals by month twelve, the petition should show that the foreign parent has the funds to pay them and the market to justify them.

It helps to form and capitalize the U.S. entity properly before filing. Articles of organization, an EIN, a U.S. bank account funded by the parent, and ownership documents that trace control back to the foreign company all go into the exhibit list. ## What the first year approval allows

A new office L-1A is approved for one year. The transferee can enter in L-1A status, spouses can work on L-2 status, and the company can start operating. The one-year clock is also the deadline: by the time the extension is filed, the U.S. operation needs to look like a business that supports a manager, not a plan for one.

Keep records from day one with the extension in mind:

- Payroll records and Forms W-2 or 1099 for every U.S. hire, with start dates.
- Organizational charts showing who reports to the transferee and what each subordinate does.
- Contracts, invoices, bank statements and tax filings that prove the office is doing business.
- Evidence that the premises are in use, such as the lease, utility bills and photographs.
- A short description of the transferee's duties, with the percentage of time spent on managerial tasks.

## The extension at month twelve

The extension petition is a second adjudication, and USCIS reviews it against what the original plan promised. The regulation asks for evidence that the U.S. and foreign entities are still qualifying organizations, that the U.S. entity has been doing business for the past year, a statement of the transferee's duties during that year and the duties for the coming period, a staffing table with dates of hire and job descriptions, and financial statements showing the operation's status. If the office hired two people instead of six, say so and explain how the manager still spends most of the day managing. Silence on a shortfall reads worse than a candid explanation.

Established-office extensions come in two-year increments, up to the seven-year L-1A maximum. The first new office extension is the one with the highest failure rate, because small staffs invite the argument that the transferee is doing the work rather than directing it. Our post on [the L-1 visa as a transfer tool](/l-1-visa-guide-transfers) covers the general qualifying rules; the new office extension turns on operations.

## Alternatives and what comes next

Not every first U.S. hire needs a new office L-1A. A founder from a treaty country who is investing personal funds may fit the [E-2 treaty investor visa](/practice-area/key-services/e-2-visa) better, with no one-year-abroad requirement and no new office cap. A company with three or more related entities and an established U.S. presence can skip individual petitions entirely under a [blanket L petition](/blanket-l-petition-explained), though a brand-new U.S. office will not qualify for a blanket until it has operated for a year.

The long-term payoff of a new office L-1A is the EB-1C multinational manager green card. That category requires the U.S. company to have been doing business for at least one year, which is why most new office transferees file the I-140 after the first extension is approved. Our article on [EB-1C multinational manager requirements](/eb-1c-multinational-manager-requirements) explains how the evidence overlaps, and the [EB-1C green card](/practice-area/key-services/eb-1c-green-card) page describes how we plan both filings together.
