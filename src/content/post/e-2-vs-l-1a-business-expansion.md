The E-2 vs L-1A question comes up whenever a foreign company or founder opens a U.S. office. Both visas can put a leader on the ground to run it, and both let a spouse work. But they rest on different foundations. The E-2 depends on treaty nationality and a substantial investment in the U.S. business. The L-1A depends on a qualifying relationship between the U.S. company and a company abroad, and on the transferee having worked for that foreign company for a year. Which one fits turns on your nationality, your corporate structure, how long you plan to stay, and whether you want a green card.

## What each visa requires

The E-2 requires that you, and at least 50 percent of the U.S. company's ownership, hold the nationality of a country with a qualifying treaty. The company must receive a substantial investment that is committed and at risk, it must be a real operating business that is not marginal, and you must be coming to develop and direct it. No prior employment abroad is required. Employees who share the treaty nationality can join you in executive, supervisory, or essential roles.

The L-1A requires a parent, subsidiary, affiliate, or branch relationship between the foreign employer and the U.S. company. The transferee must have worked for the foreign company for at least one continuous year within the three years before the petition, in an executive, managerial, or specialized knowledge capacity, and must fill an executive or managerial role in the United States. Nationality does not matter, and there is no investment threshold. The employer files Form I-129 with USCIS. ## The new office problem

For a company opening its first U.S. location, the L-1A has a special set of new office rules. The initial approval is limited to one year. The company must show it has secured physical premises, and that the U.S. operation will support an executive or managerial position within that first year. At the extension, USCIS looks for real staff, real revenue, and a manager who manages rather than performs the day-to-day work. Many new office extensions draw Requests for Evidence on exactly that point.

The E-2 has no equivalent one-year test. A new E-2 business must be operating or about to operate, and the business plan has to show growth beyond marginality, generally within five years. Each admission lasts two years. For a founder building slowly, that longer runway can make a real difference. Our guide to [what an E-2 business plan should include](/e-2-visa-business-plan-requirements) explains how that growth is shown.

## Duration, renewals, and intent

The L-1A has a hard limit: seven years total in L-1A status. The E-2 has no cap; you can renew as long as the business qualifies.

The intent rules run the other way. The L-1 is a dual intent visa, so filing a green card petition does not undermine your status. The E-2 requires an intent to depart when status ends. That does not prevent a green card case, but it requires more careful timing of filings and travel.

## Family members

Both visas let spouses work incident to status, with the I-94 annotated to show it. Children under 21 can come as dependents and attend school under either visa, but cannot work. E-2 dependents do not need to share the investor's nationality.

## Green card paths

The L-1A lines up directly with the EB-1C green card for multinational managers and executives. The requirements are close to identical, no labor certification is needed, and dual intent makes the transition smooth. For many companies, that alone decides the question. Our [L-1A intracompany transfer visa](/practice-area/key-services/l-1a-visa) page covers that pairing.

An E-2 holder can also reach the EB-1C if the U.S. company is related to a foreign company where the investor worked as a manager. Otherwise, E-2 investors usually look to the EB-2 National Interest Waiver or the EB-5.

## E-2 or L-1A: which fits your expansion

The L-1A tends to fit when:

- an established company abroad is opening a U.S. subsidiary or branch
- the person moving has run part of that company for at least a year
- the company is ready to staff the U.S. office quickly
- a green card through the EB-1C is the goal

The E-2 tends to fit when the founder is a treaty national investing personal or company funds, the foreign business is small or not related to the U.S. one, the founder wants an open-ended stay without the seven-year cap, or the U.S. business will grow on its own timeline rather than within one year.

Some companies use both. A treaty-national owner can hold E-2 status while staff of other nationalities transfer on L-1 visas. For a deeper look at the transfer side, see our [L-1 visa guide for transfers](/l-1-visa-guide-transfers).
