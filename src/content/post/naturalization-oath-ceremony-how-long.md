The oath ceremony is the final step in becoming a U.S. citizen. You're not a citizen until you take the Oath of Allegiance, even if your N-400 has been approved. Many applicants want to know how long it takes for a citizenship oath ceremony to be scheduled and what happens on the day itself.

## How long after approval

The time between N-400 approval and the oath ceremony varies widely:

- **Same-day oath:** Some USCIS field offices offer the oath on the same day as the interview, especially when they hold administrative ceremonies.
- **A few weeks:** Many applicants receive an oath ceremony notice (Form N-445) within several weeks of approval.
- **Several months:** In busy areas, or where ceremonies are held in federal court, the wait can be longer.

If you were recommended for approval at your interview, you may see a "case was approved" update online before receiving the ceremony notice. If months pass without a notice, contact USCIS.

## Judicial vs administrative ceremonies

- **Administrative ceremonies** are conducted by USCIS and are often scheduled more quickly.
- **Judicial ceremonies** are conducted by a federal court. Some districts require judicial ceremonies, and they may also be required for name changes, since a court must approve them.

## How long the ceremony takes

The ceremony itself usually lasts one to two hours, including check-in. Some large ceremonies take longer. Plan to arrive early, as there's often a security line.

## What to bring

- **Form N-445,** the oath ceremony notice, with the questionnaire on the back completed
- **Your green card,** which you'll return at check-in
- **Any immigration documents** USCIS asked you to bring, such as a reentry permit
- **Photo identification**

## The N-445 questionnaire

The back of the notice asks about events since your interview, such as:

- New arrests or citations
- Travel outside the U.S.
- Changes in marital status
- Claims to be a U.S. citizen

Answer honestly. If something happened, such as an arrest, talk to an attorney before the ceremony.

## What happens at the ceremony

1. **Check-in:** You hand in your green card and N-445.
2. **Opening remarks:** Officials welcome the new citizens.
3. **The Oath of Allegiance:** You take the oath as a group.
4. **Certificate of Naturalization:** You receive your certificate, which you should check for errors before leaving.

## After the ceremony

- **Apply for a U.S. passport.** You'll need your original Certificate of Naturalization.
- **Update your Social Security record** so your citizenship status is reflected.
- **Register to vote.** Many ceremonies have voter registration tables.
- **Keep your certificate safe.** Don't laminate it, and make copies for your records.

## If you can't attend

If you can't make your scheduled ceremony, return the N-445 to your field office with a letter explaining why and asking to be rescheduled. Missing multiple ceremonies without explanation can lead to your application being denied.

## Name changes at the ceremony

If you asked to change your legal name on your N-400, the change is usually made through a judicial oath ceremony, because a court must approve it. That can affect the timing of your ceremony. If you no longer want the name change, tell USCIS, since it may allow you to attend an earlier administrative ceremony.

## Checking your certificate

Before leaving the ceremony, read your Certificate of Naturalization carefully. Check your name, date of birth, country of former nationality, and other details. If something is wrong because of a USCIS error, ask about correcting it right away. Fixing an error later requires filing Form N-565 and can take months.

## Events between the interview and the oath

You must remain eligible until the moment you take the oath. A new arrest, a long trip abroad, or a change in marital status for someone applying under the three-year rule can all affect eligibility. If something like that happens, get advice before the ceremony rather than answering the N-445 questions on the day without preparation.

## How long does the whole naturalization process take?

From filing to oath, the naturalization process typically takes several months to more than a year, depending on the field office. The main stages are filing the N-400, biometrics, the interview, and the oath. The interview and oath can happen on the same day at some offices. Delays at any stage, such as a request for additional evidence or a continued interview, push back the oath date.

## If USCIS delays scheduling your oath

If you were approved and several months have passed without an oath notice, first check your online account and confirm your address is current. Then submit a case inquiry. If the delay continues, options include contacting your congressional representative's office or the CIS Ombudsman. In some long-delayed cases, federal court action may be available, but most oath scheduling delays resolve with follow-up.
