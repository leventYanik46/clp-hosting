A legal risk assessment is a structured look at where your business could get sued, fined, or stuck, ranked so you know what to fix first. Done well, it takes a few weeks of interviews and document review, not a year of consultants, and it ends with a short list of concrete fixes with owners and deadlines. Done badly, it produces a binder nobody opens. The difference is mostly in how you scope it and what you do with the results.

Most companies ask for one after a scare: a demand letter from a former employee, a state agency notice, a customer audit request, or an investor's due diligence questionnaire the team could not answer. You get more value by doing it before that moment, when the fixes are cheap. ## What a legal risk assessment covers

The scope depends on what you do and where you do it. A software company selling to hospitals has different exposure than a restaurant group or a government contractor. Most assessments for growing businesses cover the same core areas:

- Entity and governance: good standing, annual reports, registrations in each state where you operate, current ownership records, and signed operating or shareholder agreements.
- Contracts: your customer terms, top vendor agreements, leases, and any agreement with exclusivity, auto-renewal, uncapped liability, or a personal guarantee.
- Employment: worker classification, wage and hour practices, Form I-9 records, handbook policies, restrictive covenants, and the visa status of sponsored workers.
- Data and privacy: what personal data you collect, whether your privacy policy matches what you actually do, and how you would respond to a breach.
- Industry regulation: licenses, permits, and agency rules specific to your field.
- Intellectual property: who owns the code, brand, and content your business depends on.

The point is to catch the problems that cross categories. A sales team promising features in emails can create contract claims and advertising claims at once. A contractor who wrote your core software without signing an IP assignment is both an ownership problem and a fundraising problem.

## The step-by-step process

### 1. Scoping and document request
Counsel meets with leadership to define the business lines, jurisdictions, and regulators in play, then sends a document request. Expect to pull formation documents, your largest contracts by revenue or spend, policies, insurance certificates, and any past claims or agency correspondence.

### 2. Interviews
Short interviews with the people who run sales, operations, HR, finance, and IT usually surface more than the documents do. People describe what actually happens, which is often not what the policy says.

### 3. Gap analysis
Each obligation is compared to current practice. A gap might be a missing registration, an expired license, a template contract that no longer matches how you sell, or a policy that promises something nobody does.

### 4. Ranking
Each gap is scored on two axes: how likely it is to cause a problem and how much it would cost if it did, counting fines, damages, lost deals, and management time. Some teams use a simple heat map. The ranking matters more than the format, because it turns a long list into a plan.

### 5. Remediation plan
The output is a prioritized plan: what to fix, who owns it, and by when. Quick wins, such as filing a missed annual report or adding a limitation of liability clause to your template, go first. Larger projects, such as rebuilding a privacy program or reclassifying contractors, get a timeline and a budget.

## Who should run it, and why privilege matters

You can run an internal checklist yourself, and for a very small business that may be enough. The case for having counsel lead it is less about expertise than protection. An assessment is a written list of your company's weak points. If it is prepared by or at the direction of a lawyer for the purpose of giving legal advice, there is a stronger argument that it is protected by attorney-client privilege. If a consultant or an employee writes it as a business document, it may be discoverable in the next lawsuit. We explain the limits of that protection in our post on [whether privilege covers an internal investigation](/internal-investigation-attorney-client-privilege).

## What to do with the results

The assessment is the starting point. The value comes from three follow-up steps:

1. Assign every high-priority item to a named person with a deadline, and track it like any other operating project.
2. Turn recurring obligations (annual reports, license renewals, policy reviews, training) into a compliance calendar so they stop depending on memory.
3. Decide whether you need a formal program. Many gaps come from the absence of basic structure: no owner for compliance, no reporting channel, no training. Our guide to [what a small business compliance program should include](/small-business-compliance-program-elements) covers that next step.

Plan to repeat a lighter version every year and a full version when something big changes: a new state, a new product line, a financing round, an acquisition, or a new regulator. Companies that use [outside general counsel](/practice-area/general-counsel-services) often fold the annual refresh into that relationship, so the review reflects what counsel already sees in daily work.

## Common mistakes

The most common mistake is scoping too broadly and finishing nothing. Start with the areas where a problem would hurt most. The second is treating the report as the deliverable. A risk you documented but never addressed can look worse later than one you never noticed, because it shows the company knew. The third is leaving out the people closest to the work. Policies rarely tell you where the risk is; the sales lead, the payroll manager, and the IT administrator usually do.
