The R-1 visa maximum stay is five years, and the clock is less forgiving than it sounds. USCIS grants an initial period of up to 30 months, allows one extension of up to 30 months more, and then stops. After five years in R-1 status, the worker must live outside the United States for a full year before a new R-1 petition can be approved, unless a narrow exception applies. Congregations that treat the R-1 as a renewable permit discover this in year four, often with a green card process that is nowhere near done.

## The initial 30 months

A new R-1 petition is approved for the period the employer requests, up to 30 months. The I-94 record issued at the port of entry, or on the change-of-status approval, sets the actual end date. The visa stamp in the passport may show a different validity; the I-94 controls. Check it after every entry and diary the expiration, because the extension must be filed before that date, not before the visa stamp expires. Our [R-1 visa](/practice-area/key-services/r-1-visa) team sets that reminder at approval for every client.

## The extension to the R-1 visa maximum stay

The employer files a new Form I-129 with the R-1 supplement to extend the stay, requesting up to 30 additional months so that the total does not exceed five years. The extension petition repeats the eligibility showing: the organization's tax-exempt status, the religious nature of the position, the worker's continued membership, and compensation. It must also prove the worker was actually paid as the original petition promised, which is why USCIS asks for IRS documentation such as W-2 forms or, for non-salaried workers, evidence of the housing and support actually provided. An employer that promised a salary and paid nothing will not get an extension and may have trouble with the next petition for anyone.

A timely filed extension keeps the worker in a period of authorized stay while USCIS decides, and employment with the same employer may continue for a limited period under the regulations. Do not rely on that cushion as a filing strategy; file early enough that the decision arrives before the current period ends. If the organization has previously passed a site inspection, ask whether premium processing is available for the extension; when it is, it removes most of the uncertainty. The process is otherwise the same as the first filing, described in [how the R-1 process works](/r-1-visa-process-i-129-site-visit).

## Time spent outside the United States

The regulation measures the limit as the total period of time spent in R-1 status. For H-1B and L-1 workers, the regulations expressly allow full days spent abroad to be recaptured and added back; the R-1 rules are less explicit, so treat a recapture request for an R-1 worker as an argument to document rather than an entitlement. Either way, keep passport stamps, boarding passes, and other travel records for every trip. For missionaries and clergy who travel frequently, the difference can be months, and without records there is nothing to argue from.

## The one-year reset and its exceptions

After five years, the worker must reside outside the United States for at least one year before being eligible for a new R-1. Brief trips abroad do not count; the residence must be continuous. Two exceptions exist. The first is for workers who did not reside continually in the United States and whose R-1 employment was seasonal or intermittent, or for an aggregate of six months or less per year. The second is for workers who reside abroad and regularly commute to the United States to perform part-time employment. Both must be documented with evidence of the pattern of residence and travel. For a full-time minister living in the United States, neither applies, and the year abroad is mandatory.

## Changing employers and changing jobs

An R-1 worker may only work for the petitioning organization. A move to a different congregation, even within the same denomination, requires the new employer to file its own petition before the worker starts there. Time in R-1 status with the first employer counts toward the same five-year total; a new employer does not reset the clock. A material change in the position, such as a shift from ministerial duties to administration, requires an amended petition and may take the job outside the category entirely.

## R-2 family members

The spouse and unmarried children under 21 of an R-1 worker may hold R-2 status for the same period. R-2 dependents may study but may not accept employment; a spouse who wants to work needs an independent basis. R-2 status ends when the principal's does, so each extension for the worker should be paired with a Form I-539 extension for the family.

## Planning for what comes after five years

For workers who intend to stay permanently, the five-year limit is the deadline that organizes everything else. The special immigrant religious worker green card requires two years of qualifying experience before the Form I-360 is filed, has its own evidentiary standards, and depends on visa availability under the EB-4 category, which has had waiting lines in recent years. The path and its timing are set out in [from R-1 to a green card through EB-4](/r-1-visa-to-green-card-eb-4). The short version: start the immigrant case early in the R-1 period, not in year four, and consider alternatives such as a cap-exempt H-1B if the position supports it.
