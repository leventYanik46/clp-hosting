Yes, a green card holder can be deported in some circumstances, and no, a naturalized citizen cannot be deported as such, although citizenship can be taken away in narrow cases. A green card gives you the right to live and work in the United States permanently, but it does not make you immune from removal. Understanding the grounds helps you avoid mistakes that cost status.

## Can a green card holder be deported?

Permanent residents are protected from removal on most grounds, but not all. The law lists categories of conduct that make a permanent resident deportable. The government must prove its case in immigration court, and the resident can raise defenses and seek relief.

## Common grounds for deportation

- **Aggravated felonies.** A category defined by statute, not by how serious a crime sounds. Some offenses that are misdemeanors under state law can qualify.
- **Crimes involving moral turpitude.** One such crime committed within five years of admission, where a sentence of a year or longer could be imposed, or two such crimes at any time, can lead to removal.
- **Controlled substance offenses.** Most drug convictions qualify, with a narrow exception for a single offense of simple possession of a small amount of marijuana for personal use.
- **Firearms offenses.**
- **Domestic violence, stalking, child abuse and violation of protection orders.**
- **Fraud or misrepresentation** in obtaining your status or a false claim to U.S. citizenship.
- **Abandoning your residence.** Staying abroad for long periods can lead the government to treat your green card as abandoned.
- **Security-related grounds.**

## What about a felony

People often ask whether a permanent resident can be deported for a felony. The label alone does not decide it. Some felonies do not trigger removal, while some offenses that are not felonies do. What matters is the statute, the sentence and the way the law classifies the offense. Anyone with a criminal charge should speak to an immigration attorney before pleading. A criminal defense lawyer has a duty to advise a noncitizen client of the immigration consequences of a plea, and a plea that looks minor can have serious effects.

## Defenses and relief

A permanent resident in removal proceedings may be able to:

- Argue that the charge does not apply
- Seek cancellation of removal for permanent residents, if the requirements are met
- Apply for a waiver, depending on the ground
- Seek asylum or other protection if they fear return
- Challenge a conviction that was obtained with defective advice

## Can a naturalized citizen be deported?

A U.S. citizen, including a naturalized one, is not subject to deportation. But the government can seek denaturalization, which strips citizenship, in limited circumstances. The most common are citizenship obtained through fraud or misrepresentation of a material fact, or when the person was not eligible when naturalized. Denaturalization is a court process with a high burden on the government, and it is uncommon. If citizenship is revoked, the person goes back to the status they had before, or to no status, and may face removal proceedings.

## How to reduce risk

- **Talk to counsel before any criminal plea,** even for a minor charge.
- **Keep your travel records,** and avoid very long absences without advice.
- **Answer immigration forms truthfully.** Past errors should be reviewed with an attorney before you file for citizenship.
- **Renew documents on time** and update your address with USCIS.
- **Do not claim to be a citizen** on any form, including employment forms.
