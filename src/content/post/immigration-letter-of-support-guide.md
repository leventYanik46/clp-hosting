An immigration letter of support is a statement from someone who knows an applicant personally, written to help a USCIS officer or immigration judge understand who that person is. These letters appear in marriage green card cases, waivers, asylum cases, bond hearings, cancellation of removal, and naturalization. The best letters are specific and honest. The weakest ones read like a form anyone could have signed.

## When these letters are used

- **Marriage-based green cards.** Friends and family describe the couple's relationship to support that the marriage is genuine.
- **Waivers.** Letters help show hardship to a U.S. citizen or permanent resident relative.
- **Immigration court.** Character reference letters support bond requests, cancellation of removal, and other discretionary relief.
- **Asylum.** Letters can corroborate facts, such as membership in a group or events the applicant experienced.
- **Naturalization.** Letters can support good moral character when an issue in the applicant's history needs context.

## What to include

### 1. Who you are

Your full name, address, phone number, and immigration status or citizenship. If you are a U.S. citizen or permanent resident, say so and include a copy of your passport or green card if the attorney asks for it.

### 2. How you know the person

How long, in what role, and how often you see them. "I have been Maria's neighbor for six years, and we have dinner together most Sundays" is far stronger than "I have known Maria for a long time."

### 3. Specific examples

This is the heart of the letter. Describe things you have seen yourself:

- For a marriage case: attending the wedding, visiting the couple's home, how they handle hard times together
- For a character letter: how the person treats family, coworkers, or community; volunteer work; times they helped someone
- For a hardship letter: concrete effects on the family if the person were removed

### 4. Your honest assessment

A short statement of why you believe what you are saying, grounded in the examples you gave.

### 5. Signature and date

Sign by hand. Some cases call for a notarized letter or a declaration under penalty of perjury. Ask the attorney which format to use.

## What to avoid

- **Exaggeration or anything you do not know firsthand.** Officers and judges notice, and false statements can harm the case.
- **Legal conclusions.** Do not write "she qualifies for asylum" or "this marriage is bona fide." Describe what you have seen and let the evidence speak.
- **Copying a template word for word.** Several letters that read the same suggest someone else wrote them.
- **Discussing the applicant's legal problems** unless the attorney asks you to address them.

## Character letters for immigration court

A character reference letter for immigration court often addresses a specific concern, such as a past arrest. In that case, acknowledge that you know about it, describe changes you have seen, and explain the person's role in the family and community. Judges give more weight to writers who are candid about the full picture.

## Format and language

Type the letter if possible. If it is in a language other than English, include a certified translation. Keep it to one or two pages.


## Frequently asked questions

### How many letters should I submit?

Quality beats quantity. A few detailed letters from people who know the applicant well usually carry more weight than a stack of short, similar letters.

### Can someone outside the U.S. write a letter?

Yes. Letters from family or community members abroad can help, especially in asylum or hardship cases. Include a certified translation if not in English.

### Should the letter be addressed to a specific officer?

A general salutation, such as "To the U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services" or "To the Honorable Immigration Judge," is fine.
