Cancellation of removal is a form of relief that an immigration judge can grant to someone who is in removal proceedings, and it is one of the most important defenses available. It comes in two main forms, and the requirements are very different. Which one applies depends on whether you are a lawful permanent resident, and the details of your history decide whether you qualify.

## What cancellation of removal does

If the judge grants it, the removal case is ended. A permanent resident keeps their green card. A non-permanent resident who wins is granted permanent resident status. It is available only in court, as a defense, and it is discretionary, meaning the judge can deny it even if you meet the requirements.

## Cancellation for permanent residents (LPR cancellation)

A green card holder who faces removal may qualify if they:

- Have been a lawful permanent resident for at least five years
- Have resided in the United States continuously for at least seven years after being admitted in any status
- Have not been convicted of an aggravated felony

The seven-year period is subject to the stop-time rule. The clock stops when the person is served with a Notice to Appear or when they commit certain offenses that make them inadmissible or deportable, whichever comes first. Supreme Court decisions have addressed what counts as a valid notice for this purpose, so the exact documents you received matter.

Even when the requirements are met, the judge weighs favorable factors, such as family ties, length of residence, hardship, work history, tax payment and rehabilitation, against negative ones, such as the offenses behind the case. LPR cancellation can generally be granted only once.

## Cancellation for non-permanent residents (non-LPR cancellation)

This form is for people without a green card, and the standard is much tougher. The applicant must show:

- **Ten years of continuous physical presence** in the United States immediately before applying, subject to the stop-time rule
- **Good moral character** during that period
- **No disqualifying convictions** under the statute
- **Exceptional and extremely unusual hardship** to a qualifying relative, which means a U.S. citizen or permanent resident spouse, parent, or child under 21

Two points surprise people. First, the hardship must be to the qualifying relative, not to the applicant. Second, the standard is higher than ordinary hardship. Ordinary difficulties, such as financial strain or the emotional pain of separation, are generally not enough on their own. Serious medical conditions, special educational needs of a child, or other significant circumstances may be relevant.

There is also an annual cap on how many non-LPR cancellations can be granted. In practice, judges sometimes reserve a decision until a number becomes available.

## Comparing the two

| Issue | LPR cancellation | Non-LPR cancellation |
|---|---|---|
| Who | Green card holders | People without a green card |
| Time requirement | 5 years as LPR, 7 years continuous residence | 10 years continuous physical presence |
| Hardship | Not required | Exceptional and extremely unusual hardship to a qualifying relative |
| Good moral character | Not a formal element | Required |
| Annual cap | None | Yes |

## What to prepare

- Proof of how long you have lived in the United States, such as leases, tax returns and school records
- Evidence of family ties and the needs of qualifying relatives
- Medical and educational records, where relevant
- Letters from employers, family and community
- Records of any arrests or convictions, with court documents

## Why representation matters

These cases depend on detailed evidence and on legal arguments about the stop-time rule and the criminal history. A lawyer can assess early whether you meet the threshold, gather proof and present the hardship case. If you have received a Notice to Appear, speak to counsel before your first hearing.
