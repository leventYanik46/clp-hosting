Every Form I-589 is an application for three things at once: asylum, withholding of removal, and protection under the Convention Against Torture (CAT). They are not three names for the same relief. The comparison of asylum vs withholding of removal comes down to how much you must prove, who is allowed to grant it, and what you hold at the end. Asylum is the only one of the three that leads to a green card and to status for your spouse and children, which is why our [asylum and humanitarian protection](/practice-area/key-services/asylum-and-humanitarian-protection) team treats withholding and CAT as fallbacks rather than alternatives. A bar that closes the asylum door, though, often leaves one of the other two open, so every case is prepared with all three in view.

## Asylum vs withholding of removal: the standard of proof

Asylum under INA 208 requires a well-founded fear of persecution on account of race, religion, nationality, political opinion, or membership in a particular social group. The Supreme Court held in INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca that a well-founded fear means a reasonable possibility of persecution, a standard that can be met even when the odds of harm are well below even. If you prove past persecution, the law presumes you have a well-founded fear of future persecution, and the government must rebut that presumption by showing changed conditions or a safe place to relocate.

Withholding of removal under INA 241(b)(3) protects against the same five grounds but demands more: you must show that persecution is more likely than not if you return. That is a clear majority of the probability, not a reasonable possibility.

CAT protection, found in the regulations at 8 CFR 1208.16 through 1208.18, requires you to show it is more likely than not that you would be tortured, and that the torture would be inflicted by a public official or with a public official's consent or acquiescence. There is no protected-ground requirement. The trade is that torture is narrower than persecution: severe physical or mental pain or suffering, intentionally inflicted, not every form of serious harm.

## Who is allowed to grant each one

An asylum officer at USCIS can grant asylum in an affirmative case, and an immigration judge can grant it in removal proceedings. Withholding of removal and CAT protection are different. Only an immigration judge can grant them, because both are forms of relief from a removal order.

That has a practical consequence for people who file with USCIS. If the officer does not grant asylum and you have no lawful status, the case is referred to immigration court, and the judge considers all three forms of protection on the same I-589. A person who is barred from asylum but files an [affirmative asylum application](/practice-area/key-services/affirmative-asylum-applications) anyway is, in effect, using the USCIS filing as the on-ramp to a withholding or CAT claim before a judge. That can be the correct strategy, but it should be a decision, not an accident.

## What you hold at the end

A grant of asylum gives you asylee status: you can work, apply for a Social Security number, petition for a spouse and unmarried children under 21 on Form I-730 within two years, travel on a refugee travel document, and apply for a green card after one year of physical presence. Asylum is also discretionary: a judge or officer can deny it to someone who meets the definition, though that is uncommon absent serious negative factors.

Withholding of removal is mandatory if you meet the standard, but it comes with an order of removal. The judge orders you removed and then withholds removal to the specific country where you face persecution. The government may still remove you to a third country that agrees to accept you. You receive work authorization that must be renewed, you cannot petition for family members, you cannot obtain a refugee travel document, and leaving the United States executes the removal order. Withholding never ripens into a green card on its own.

CAT comes in two forms. Withholding of removal under CAT works like statutory withholding. Deferral of removal under CAT is available even to people subject to the bars described below, and it is the easiest of the three for the government to terminate if conditions change.

## The bars, and why one claim survives when another fails

The bars are where the three diverge most sharply, and they are why a withholding or CAT claim can succeed after asylum is foreclosed.

- The one-year filing deadline applies to asylum only. If you filed late and no exception fits, withholding and CAT remain fully available. Our post on [missing the one-year asylum deadline](/one-year-asylum-deadline-exceptions) explains the exceptions.
- Firm resettlement in a third country bars asylum, not withholding or CAT.
- A conviction for a particularly serious crime bars both asylum and withholding, but the threshold differs. Any aggravated felony is a particularly serious crime for asylum purposes; for withholding, an aggravated felony is automatically a particularly serious crime only if the aggregate sentence was at least five years, and lesser offenses are examined case by case.
- The persecutor bar, the serious nonpolitical crime bar, and the security and terrorism bars apply to asylum and withholding alike.
- Deferral of removal under CAT has no bars at all, because the Convention's prohibition on returning a person to torture is absolute.

A prior asylum denial, a safe-third-country agreement, and discretion also apply only to asylum.

## How we sequence the three in practice

The order is always asylum first, withholding second, CAT third, because the benefits decline in that order. The strategy question is where to put the weight. If the one-year deadline is the problem, the hearing will turn on the withholding standard, so the evidence must push the probability of harm past the halfway mark, not merely make it plausible. If the persecutor is a gang or a cartel and the protected-ground link is weak, the CAT claim may be the strongest piece, and the evidence should focus on official acquiescence: police who refuse to act, officials on the payroll, a documented pattern of impunity.

A withholding grant should never be the end of the plan; it keeps you safe but leaves you without a path to permanence, so we keep looking for a route to a green card.
