Expanding a business to another state usually involves four legal steps: registering your company as a foreign entity in the new state, opening tax and payroll accounts there, adapting your employment practices to that state's law, and obtaining any state or local licenses your industry needs. The trigger is usually doing business in the state, which typically means having an office, employees, or regular in-person operations there. Selling into a state remotely may not require registration, but it can still create tax obligations. Getting the order right saves money and avoids penalties that are easy to incur and tedious to unwind.

## Step 1: Decide whether you need to register, and how

Each state requires out-of-state companies that are transacting business there to register, a process often called foreign qualification. States define the term differently, and most list activities that do not count on their own, such as holding a bank account, defending a lawsuit, or an isolated transaction. Opening an office, hiring employees who work there, or keeping inventory there usually crosses the line.

Registration means filing with the state's business filing office (in Virginia, the State Corporation Commission; in New York, the Department of State), appointing a registered agent with an address in the state, and providing proof of good standing from your home state. Some states add their own requirements. New York, for example, requires newly formed and newly registered LLCs to publish notice in newspapers within 120 days, and failing to publish can suspend the LLC's authority to do business there.

Skipping registration has consequences. In most states, an unregistered company cannot bring a lawsuit in that state's courts until it registers, and it may owe back fees and penalties. Its contracts usually remain valid, but enforcing them gets harder.

The alternative is forming a separate subsidiary in the new state. That can isolate liability for a riskier operation, but it adds a second entity to maintain, so most companies simply register the existing one.

## Step 2: Set up tax, payroll, and insurance accounts

Having employees or property in a state usually creates income tax nexus there. Since the Supreme Court's decision in South Dakota v. Wayfair, states can also require remote sellers to collect sales tax based on economic activity, such as sales volume, without any physical presence. Work with your accountant to map the new obligations, which can include:

- State income or franchise tax registration.
- Sales and use tax registration, if you sell taxable goods or services.
- State payroll withholding and unemployment insurance accounts.
- Workers' compensation coverage that meets the new state's rules.
- Local business taxes, which some cities and counties impose separately.

## Step 3: Adjust employment practices to the new state

Employment law is where expansion most often goes wrong, because the rules follow the employee's work location. A remote employee living in another state is generally covered by that state's wage, leave, and notice rules, even if your headquarters is elsewhere.

Review minimum wage and overtime rules, paid sick leave, pay frequency, final paycheck deadlines, required posters and notices, and pay transparency laws. New York, for example, requires pay ranges in job postings. Restrictive covenants also vary: Virginia and Washington, DC both limit noncompetes for lower-wage workers, and other states go further. Our post on [legal steps before hiring your first employee](/hiring-first-employee-legal-steps) covers the onboarding basics that apply in any new state, and our earlier post on [the FTC and noncompetes](/the-ftc-is-coming-for-noncompetes-what-business-owners-need-to-know-now) explains the federal side. Update your handbook with state supplements rather than relying on one policy that fits nowhere.

## Step 4: Licenses, permits, and local contracts

Professional and industry licenses are usually state-specific. Contractors, health care providers, financial services companies, and many other businesses need a license in each state where they operate. Cities and counties may also require a general business license, zoning approval, or an occupancy permit for a new location.

Review the contracts that come with expansion: an office lease (watch for personal guarantees and assignment limits), local vendor agreements, and customer contracts that specify governing law. Our [contract review and executive counsel](/practice-area/key-services/contract-review-and-executive-counsel) attorneys can review a new-location lease before you commit.

## Plan the order of an expansion to another state

A practical order is: decide the structure, register, open tax and payroll accounts, line up insurance, sign the lease, then hire. Hiring before registration and payroll setup is the most common misstep, because it creates obligations in a state where you are not yet set up to meet them.
