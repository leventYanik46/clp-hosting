To appeal an immigration judge's decision, your Notice of Appeal on Form EOIR-26 must be received by the Board of Immigration Appeals within 30 calendar days of the decision. Not postmarked. Received. The Board does not extend that deadline for any reason, and an appeal that arrives on day 31 is dismissed without reaching the merits. If you were ordered removed, the timely filing also does something immediately valuable: it automatically stays your removal for as long as the appeal is pending.

## Step 1: Lock down the deadline and the record

The 30 days run from the date of the decision, whether the judge announced it orally at the end of your hearing or mailed a written decision later. Write the date down the same day. Then gather the written order or the summary order form, the Notice to Appear, your exhibits and the DHS exhibits. If prior counsel handled the hearing and you are changing lawyers, request the file in writing; if you cannot get it, our [FOIA practice](/practice-area/key-services/foia-requests) can obtain the court record and your immigration file, although FOIA timing is never fast enough to replace the 30-day deadline. Our [BIA appeals practice](/practice-area/key-services/bia-appeals-and-motions) calendars the deadline the day the decision arrives.

Do not leave the United States. Under the regulations, departure after an appeal is filed is treated as withdrawing it, and the removal order is then executed.

## Step 2: File Form EOIR-26 to appeal the immigration judge's decision

Form EOIR-26 is a short form, but one box on it decides many appeals. You must state the reasons for your appeal specifically enough that the Board can tell what the judge got wrong. A statement that the judge erred in denying the case is a summary dismissal waiting to happen. Instead identify the ruling (for example, the adverse credibility finding, the hardship determination, the particular social group analysis) and say in a sentence or two why it was wrong, citing the applicable standard. You should also check the box indicating whether you will file a brief, and then actually file one; indicating a brief and not filing it is a separate ground for summary dismissal.

The form is filed with the Board in Falls Church, Virginia, with proof of service on the DHS Office of the Principal Legal Advisor that handled your case. The Board charges a filing fee for most appeals, payable through EOIR's payment system, and you may request a fee waiver on Form EOIR-26A; check the current EOIR fee schedule before filing. Your representative enters an appearance before the Board on Form EOIR-27. The Board's ECAS electronic filing system is available to represented parties and avoids mail delays entirely.

We prepare the EOIR-26 and the statement of reasons as a single piece of work, because the reasons stated on the form frame what the brief can argue later.

## Step 3: The transcript and briefing schedule

Once the appeal is docketed, the Board orders a transcript of your hearings. This is the step that takes time, and there is nothing to file while you wait. When the transcript is ready, the Board mails it with a briefing schedule. The appealing party ordinarily has 21 days to file a brief, and the other side has 21 days to respond. A single extension of 21 days is available on request, and the Board grants it routinely, but it rarely grants a second one. Missing the brief deadline is treated like not filing a brief.

The brief is where the appeal is won. It applies the record to the standards of review:

- Findings of fact, including credibility, are reviewed for clear error. The Board will not reweigh testimony, so a brief that argues the judge should have believed you will fail unless it shows that the judge's finding has no support in the record, rests on a misreading of the testimony, or ignored corroborating evidence.
- Questions of law, discretion and judgment are reviewed de novo. A misapplied legal standard, a hardship analysis that skipped a required factor, a particular social group framed incorrectly, or a due process violation at the hearing gives the Board room to rule for you without second-guessing the judge's view of the facts.

The Board decides on the record made before the judge. It will not accept new evidence on appeal. If something important happened after the hearing (a new medical diagnosis, an approved I-130, a change in country conditions), the vehicle is a motion to remand filed with the Board, which asks it to send the case back to the judge to consider the new evidence. Our comparison of [motions to reopen and motions to reconsider](/motion-to-reopen-vs-motion-to-reconsider) explains how those motions differ from the appeal itself.

## Step 4: The decision and what comes next

Most appeals are decided by a single Board member in a short order; a three-member panel hears cases that present novel legal questions or where the panel intends to publish a precedent. The possible outcomes are dismissal of the appeal (you lose), sustaining the appeal (you win, and the Board either grants relief or remands with instructions), or a remand for further proceedings. Remands are common and are often the realistic goal: a second hearing before the judge with the legal error corrected.

If the Board dismisses your appeal, the removal order becomes administratively final that day and the automatic stay ends. You then have 30 days to file a [petition for review in the federal court of appeals](/petition-for-review-after-bia-decision), which does not carry an automatic stay, so a separate stay motion is filed with it. If you are detained during the appeal, a bond request through our [detention and bond](/practice-area/key-services/immigration-detention-and-bond) practice runs on a separate track and does not affect the appeal.

## Common mistakes that lose appeals before the merits

Three mistakes account for most summary dismissals and procedural losses: a generic statement of reasons on the EOIR-26, a missed brief deadline after the box was checked, and an appeal mailed too close to day 30 with no tracking. A fourth, less obvious one is failing to appeal at all because a prior lawyer advised that nothing could be done. Legal errors apparent on the face of the decision can be raised on appeal even where prior counsel did not object at the hearing, and ineffective assistance can itself be a basis for a later motion if the appeal window is missed.
