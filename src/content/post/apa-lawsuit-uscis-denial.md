When USCIS denies a petition for reasons that misread the statute or ignore evidence sitting in the file, the Administrative Procedure Act lets you take the denial to a federal judge who owes the agency no loyalty. An APA lawsuit over a USCIS denial is not an appeal in the ordinary sense and not a chance to try again with better documents. It is a request that the court declare the agency's decision unlawful and set it aside. Knowing exactly what that means, and what it does not, is the difference between a strong case and an expensive disappointment. CLP's [immigration litigation practice](/practice-area/key-services/immigration-litigation) brings these suits for employers, investors and families after denials of I-140, I-129, I-130 and other petitions.

## What an APA lawsuit over a USCIS denial asks the court to do

Section 706 of the APA directs a reviewing court to hold unlawful and set aside agency action that is arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law. The complaint is filed in U.S. district court against USCIS, its Director and the Secretary of Homeland Security. There is no jury. The case is decided on cross-motions for summary judgment based on the certified administrative record, which the government files. If you win, the court vacates the denial and remands the case to USCIS for a new decision consistent with the court's opinion. Courts rarely order approval outright.

Two threshold requirements apply. The denial must be final agency action, which a denial is and a request for evidence or notice of intent to deny is not. And the decision must be one the statute allows courts to review; [which USCIS decisions a federal court can review](/which-uscis-decisions-federal-court-review) covers the discretionary bars that take some decisions off the table.

## What arbitrary and capricious means in practice

The standard is deferential but not toothless. A court will set aside a denial where USCIS:

- Applied the wrong legal standard, for example demanding evidence the regulation does not require or reading a statutory term more narrowly than its text allows.
- Failed to consider evidence that was in the record, or dismissed it with boilerplate rather than analysis.
- Reached a conclusion that the record does not support.
- Departed from its own precedent decisions, policy manual or prior treatment of the same petitioner without acknowledging or explaining the change.
- Relied on reasons that appear for the first time in the litigation rather than in the denial itself.

That last point reflects a long-standing rule: an agency decision stands or falls on the reasons the agency gave at the time. Government lawyers cannot repair a thin denial with a better rationale in their brief.

On questions of statutory interpretation, as of this writing courts decide for themselves what the immigration statute means rather than deferring to the agency's reading of ambiguous language, a shift that has made legal-error arguments more powerful than they were a few years ago.

## What the court will not do

The judge will not weigh new letters, new expert opinions or new financial statements. The review is confined to the record USCIS had when it decided, which is why [the administrative record decides your immigration lawsuit](/administrative-record-immigration-lawsuit) and why the time to build the file is during the petition and the response to any request for evidence, not after the denial. The judge also will not substitute their own judgment on matters the statute commits to USCIS discretion, and will not review a consular officer's refusal of a visa.

## APA suit, I-290B, or refile?

A denial usually leaves three choices. An administrative appeal or motion on Form I-290B is cheaper and lets you add evidence through a motion to reopen, but it is decided by USCIS. A refile with a stronger record is often fastest for time-sensitive employment cases. The APA suit fits when the error is legal rather than evidentiary, when the AAO has already rejected the argument in similar cases, or when the petitioner needs a decision that USCIS cannot simply repeat. [I-290B appeal or federal lawsuit](/i-290b-appeal-vs-federal-lawsuit) compares the two in detail. A lawsuit can follow an unsuccessful I-290B, and the general six-year limitations period for suits against the United States leaves room for that sequence, though status consequences rarely do.

## Timeline and cost realities

After service, the government has 60 days to respond. Many cases then settle: USCIS agrees to reopen and reconsider, sometimes with a commitment to a fresh look at specific evidence, in exchange for dismissal. Cases that proceed go to summary judgment briefing over several months. Attorney fees are sometimes recoverable under the Equal Access to Justice Act where the government's position was not substantially justified, though a voluntary reopening usually does not qualify. Our [immigration appeals, delays and federal court](/practice-area/key-services/immigration-appeals-delays-and-federal-court) page outlines how this suit fits alongside the other federal remedies.

## When an APA suit is the wrong tool

If the denial rests on a factual finding the record supports, if the petitioner's status has already lapsed and a refile is available, or if the decision is one the statute shields from review, the suit will fail or will not solve the real problem. A candid assessment of the record before filing is the single most valuable step, and it is the one most often skipped by people who are angry at a denial and want to act.
