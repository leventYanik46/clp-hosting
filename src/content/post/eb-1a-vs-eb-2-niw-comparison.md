The EB-1A and the EB-2 National Interest Waiver are the only two employment-based green cards you can file for yourself, with no employer, no job offer and no PERM labor certification. That shared feature makes the EB-1A vs EB-2 NIW question the first one most researchers, founders and professionals ask. The answer turns on three things: what each category asks you to prove, how your country of birth fares under the Visa Bulletin in each preference, and how much risk you can carry. Our [talent and extraordinary ability visa overview](/practice-area/key-services/talent-and-extraordinary-ability-visas) describes every route the firm handles; this post compares the two self-petitions head to head.

## EB-1A vs EB-2 NIW: what each category tests

The EB-1A, under INA 203(b)(1)(A) and 8 CFR 204.5(h), tests the person. You must show sustained national or international acclaim and a record placing you among the small percentage at the very top of your field. Unless you hold a major internationally recognized award, you document at least three of ten regulatory criteria, and USCIS then weighs the whole record in a final merits determination under Kazarian v. USCIS. The question is whether you have already arrived at the top.

The NIW, decided under Matter of Dhanasar, tests the endeavor. You must first qualify for EB-2 as an advanced-degree professional or a person of exceptional ability. Then you show that your proposed work has substantial merit and national importance, that you are well positioned to advance it, and that on balance it would benefit the United States to waive the job offer and labor certification. The question is whether the work matters and whether you are the right person to do it. Our post on [the three Dhanasar prongs](/eb-2-niw-eligibility-three-prongs) walks through each one.

The practical difference: a mid-career engineer with a strong record but no awards or press can often make a persuasive NIW case and rarely a persuasive EB-1A case. A scientist with heavily cited work, peer review service and media coverage may qualify for both.

## The Visa Bulletin usually favors EB-1

Each preference category receives its own share of immigrant visa numbers, and the Department of State's monthly Visa Bulletin shows whether numbers are available by category and country of birth. The first preference is generally more favorable than the second. For applicants born in countries with heavy demand, EB-2 dates can sit years behind EB-1 dates, which means an NIW approval can leave you waiting far longer before you may file Form I-485 or attend a consular interview.

That gap is the strongest argument for the EB-1A when you have a plausible case. An approved I-140 in either category gives you a priority date, and under 8 CFR 204.5(e) that date can be carried to a later petition in the other category, so the early filing is rarely wasted. Check the current bulletin before you decide; the relative position of the two categories changes from month to month.

## Evidence: same record, different story

The exhibits overlap heavily. Publications, citation reports, letters from experts, patents, press, awards and leadership roles appear in both petitions. What changes is the argument built on them.

- For the EB-1A, each exhibit is mapped to a criterion, and the brief then argues that the pattern across all of them shows acclaim and a position at the top of the field.
- For the NIW, the same exhibits support the second prong, that you are well positioned to advance your endeavor, while a detailed statement of the proposed endeavor and evidence of its national importance carry the first prong.
- NIW recommendation letters focus on the significance of the work and your ability to carry it out; EB-1A letters focus on your standing and the influence of your past contributions.

Because the underlying record is shared, many clients prepare both petitions from one evidence set. Our post on [filing an EB-1A and an NIW together](/file-eb-1a-and-niw-together) explains how that works in practice.

## Risk and the cost of a denial

The EB-1A is the harder case. Petitions commonly satisfy three criteria and still fail at the final merits stage because the officer does not see sustained acclaim. The NIW is more forgiving for a solid but not yet famous record, though it fails when the endeavor is described vaguely or when the petitioner's role in it is unclear.

A denial in either category does not bar you from filing again, and it does not affect the other petition. It does cost the filing fee and time, and if you are relying on the petition for an H-1B extension beyond the sixth year or for I-485 timing, a denial can disrupt your status planning. Weigh the stronger category against the one more likely to be approved, and consider whether filing both is the better hedge.

## How to decide

Start with an honest inventory. Count the EB-1A criteria you can prove with documents, not with assertions, and then ask whether an outsider reading the file would agree you are at the top of the field. Next, write two paragraphs describing a specific endeavor you will pursue in the United States and why it matters nationally; if that comes easily, the NIW is viable. Then look up your country of birth in the Visa Bulletin for both preferences.

If the EB-1A case is strong and EB-1 is current, file it. If the record is good but not exceptional, the NIW alone may be the right call. If you qualify for both and the second preference is backlogged, filing both gives you a priority date in each.
