Most H-1B petitions are subject to an annual cap and a lottery, but some employers are exempt and can file for a qualifying worker at any point in the year. If you are looking for H-1B cap exempt jobs, the first thing to understand is that exemption belongs to the employer, not to the job title or the worker.

## Who qualifies as an H-1B cap-exempt employer

The law carves out several categories:

- **Institutions of higher education.** Universities and colleges that meet the statutory definition.
- **Nonprofit organizations related to or affiliated with a university.** This can include teaching hospitals and research centers with a qualifying relationship. The affiliation has to be real and documented.
- **Nonprofit research organizations.** An organization primarily engaged in basic or applied research.
- **Governmental research organizations.** A government body primarily engaged in basic or applied research.

A general nonprofit that does not meet one of those definitions is not cap-exempt simply because it is a nonprofit. That is a common misunderstanding, and one that can sink a petition.

## How to find cap-exempt employers

There is no single official list of every cap-exempt employer. Practical ways to look:

- Search university and academic medical center career pages, which often state that they sponsor H-1B visas.
- Look at research institutes, public health bodies and government labs.
- Ask the employer's human resources team directly whether they have filed cap-exempt petitions before, and who prepares them.

A job posting that says "cap-exempt" is a starting point only. The employer should be able to explain which exemption category it relies on.

## Concurrent H-1B employment

A cap-exempt position can be used alongside a cap-subject job. Concurrent H-1B employment means you work for more than one employer, each with its own petition. In a common arrangement, a worker with a cap-exempt university appointment is later hired part-time or full-time by a cap-subject company, and the second employer files a concurrent petition. Because the worker has already been counted, the second petition may not be subject to the cap while the cap-exempt employment continues.

Details matter here. Each petition needs its own LCA and its own evidence that the job is a specialty occupation, and the cap-exempt job generally has to be ongoing for the second filing to rely on that status. If the cap-exempt job ends and you want to keep the other one, the situation changes.

## Pros and tradeoffs

Cap-exempt work lets you avoid the lottery and file at the time that suits you. Tradeoffs include:

- **Salaries** at academic and nonprofit institutions may be lower than in private industry.
- **Moving out later** from a cap-exempt role to a for-profit employer can trigger the cap question again if the worker was never counted.
- **Green card sponsorship** practices vary by institution, so ask early.

## Before you accept an offer

Ask the employer which exemption it claims and whether it has filed successfully before. If your plan depends on concurrent employment or a later move to a cap-subject employer, speak to an immigration attorney first, since the order of filings can affect whether you are counted against the cap.
