A K-1 visa holder is admitted to the United States for exactly 90 days, and the law expects a marriage to the petitioner inside that window. If you do not marry within 90 days on a K-1, three things happen at once: your lawful status ends on day 91, you begin accruing unlawful presence, and you lose every path to a green card from inside the United States except one, marriage to the person who petitioned for you. The deadline cannot be extended, and no agency will grant more time for a postponed wedding. What follows depends entirely on why the marriage did not happen and what you do next.

## Why the K-1 90-day deadline is absolute

The K-1 is a single-purpose visa. Congress created it for one event, a marriage to a specific U.S. citizen, and wrote the limits into the statute. Section 214(d) of the Immigration and Nationality Act requires the marriage within 90 days of admission. Section 248 bars a K-1 holder from changing to any other nonimmigrant status, so there is no switch to a student or work visa. Section 245(d) bars adjustment of status on any basis other than the marriage to the petitioner. Our [K-1 fiancé visa](/practice-area/key-services/k-1-fiance-visa) practice explains these limits before the fiancé travels, because they shape every decision after arrival.

Those three rules together are why a K-1 holder who does not marry the petitioner has no status to fall back on and no green card to apply for here, however strong the alternative basis might be.

## If you still plan to marry the petitioner, just later

This is the manageable case. A couple that marries each other after the 90 days have passed can still file Form I-485, because adjustment rests on the marriage to the petitioner and USCIS does not treat the late wedding as disqualifying. The costs are real but limited: the fiancé is out of status from day 91 until the I-485 is filed, is accruing unlawful presence during that gap, and cannot work. The I-485 filing places the spouse in a period of authorized stay and stops the unlawful presence clock.

Explain the delay in the filing. A venue problem, a family illness or a marriage license backlog is understandable; a long unexplained gap invites questions about the relationship. Marry at a courthouse if the ceremony is what is holding things up; the civil certificate is all the case needs. The adjustment package itself is covered in [how K-1 adjustment of status works](/k-1-adjustment-of-status-after-marriage).

## If the engagement is over

A K-1 holder who will not marry the petitioner is expected to leave the United States. There is no alternative status, and USCIS cannot adjust them through an employer, a different spouse, or a family member. If you later marry a different U.S. citizen, that marriage can support an immigrant visa only through consular processing abroad, not adjustment here. Departing promptly matters because of the unlawful presence bars: leaving after more than 180 days past the 90-day admission triggers a three-year bar on returning, and leaving after a year or more triggers a ten-year bar.

Those bars can sometimes be waived with Form I-601 or, for unlawful presence alone in a later family case, Form I-601A, on a showing of extreme hardship to a U.S. citizen or permanent resident spouse or parent. The waiver is explained in [how the I-601A provisional waiver works](/i-601a-provisional-waiver-explained). The far simpler plan is to leave before the 180 days accrue, which preserves a clean record for whatever visa comes next.

## If the petitioner refuses to marry you, or the relationship involved abuse

A fiancé stranded by a petitioner who changes their mind has the same status problem as anyone else. A fiancé who was abused by the petitioner may have other options, such as a U visa where a qualifying crime was reported to the police, or protections under the Violence Against Women Act where a marriage took place. Which of those applies depends on facts that have to be reviewed quickly, before the time out of status accumulates, and this situation needs counsel at once.

## What not to do

Do not enter a marriage to the petitioner that neither of you intends, to rescue the status. A finding that a marriage was entered to evade immigration law permanently bars approval of any later family petition for the beneficiary under section 204(c).

Do not file an application USCIS cannot grant. An I-485 based on a marriage to someone other than the petitioner, or an I-539 to change status, will be denied and the filing becomes part of the record.

Do not stay quietly past the 90 days with no plan. Every month adds to the unlawful presence total and shrinks the options. If you are in the United States on a K-1 and the wedding is in doubt, the time to get advice is before day 90, not after.

For couples who are marrying but worry about the timing, remember that the deadline applies to the marriage, not to the green card filing. Marry within the window, then prepare the I-485. For couples not yet in the K-1 process who are uncertain about the 90-day constraint, marrying abroad and pursuing a [marriage-based green card](/practice-area/key-services/marriage-based-green-card) through a consulate removes the deadline altogether; the eligibility rules for the fiancé route are in [K-1 visa requirements](/k-1-visa-requirements-who-qualifies).
