Every dollar you give away during life is a dollar that is not in your estate at death. That simple arithmetic is the basis of lifetime gifting, and for families whose assets approach the federal exemption, or the much lower New York and District of Columbia estate tax thresholds, it is the most reliable way to reduce what the estate will owe. The rules reward some gifts and penalize others, so the strategy is in choosing what to give, to whom, and in what form.

## Annual exclusion gifts

Federal law lets you give each recipient a set amount every year without any gift tax, any return, and any use of your lifetime exemption. The amount is indexed for inflation and changes periodically, so check the current figure rather than relying on an old number. A married couple can combine their exclusions and give double to each recipient. There is no limit on the number of recipients. Over ten or fifteen years, a couple with three children and their spouses can move a substantial sum out of the estate with no filing at all. Gifts above the annual exclusion are not taxed immediately; they are reported on Form 709 and reduce the lifetime exemption available at death. ## Unlimited direct payments for tuition and medical care

Payments made directly to an educational institution for tuition, or directly to a medical provider or insurer for care, are excluded from gift tax entirely and do not count against the annual exclusion. The payment must go to the school or provider, not to the student or patient. Grandparents paying a grandchild's university tuition straight to the bursar can remove large amounts from their estate each year on top of the annual exclusion gifts. Room, board, and books do not qualify under this rule, though they can be covered by annual exclusion gifts or a 529 plan.

## Larger gifts against the lifetime exemption

Gifts beyond the annual exclusion use up part of your unified lifetime exemption, the same figure that shelters your estate at death. The exemption is high enough that most families never pay federal gift tax, and giving early has an advantage that is easy to miss: the asset's future growth happens outside your estate. A gift of shares in a growing business, or of real estate expected to appreciate, removes today's value and all of tomorrow's. The exemption amount is set by statute and has changed several times; because Congress can lower it, some families choose to use a large portion while it is available.

## The catch: carryover basis

Property you give away during life keeps your cost basis in the recipient's hands. Property you leave at death receives a new basis equal to its date-of-death value, wiping out the built-in capital gain. For a highly appreciated asset, this can reverse the math. Giving a stock position bought decades ago saves estate tax on its value but hands the recipient a capital gains bill when they sell; leaving it in the estate may cost estate tax but erases the gain. The general rule is to give cash and high-basis assets during life, and hold low-basis assets until death unless estate tax exposure is severe. This is where gifting strategy and income tax strategy have to be modeled together, not decided from a rule of thumb.

## Gifts to a spouse

Gifts between spouses who are both U.S. citizens are unlimited and tax-free. Gifts to a spouse who is not a citizen are limited to a special annual amount, larger than the ordinary exclusion but capped. For international families this limit shapes the whole plan, as our post on [estate planning for non-citizens](/estate-planning-for-non-citizens) explains.

## Lifetime gifting in trust and other structured gifts

A gift does not have to be outright. Gifts to an irrevocable trust for children or grandchildren can qualify for the annual exclusion if the beneficiaries have a temporary right to withdraw the contribution, a mechanism known as a Crummey power. An irrevocable life insurance trust uses annual gifts to pay premiums on a policy the trust owns, keeping the death benefit out of the estate. Contributions to a 529 education plan can be front-loaded with five years of annual exclusion gifts in a single year by election on Form 709. Each structure trades simplicity for control over how and when the recipient receives the money.

## State rules and the Medicaid look-back

Virginia has no estate tax and no gift tax, so for Virginia residents only the federal rules apply. New York and the District of Columbia have estate taxes with exemptions far below the federal level. Neither has a gift tax, which makes lifetime gifts especially effective for their residents, with one caution: as of this writing New York adds back certain gifts made within a few years of death when computing the taxable estate, so late gifts by a New York resident may not escape the state tax. Separately, any gift made within the sixty-month look-back period before a Medicaid application for long-term care creates a penalty period, so gifting and long-term care planning must be coordinated rather than run on separate tracks.

## Mistakes that undo the benefit

The most common mistakes are retaining a benefit in the gifted asset, such as giving a house to children while continuing to live in it rent-free, which pulls the house back into the estate; giving away assets you later need; naming a minor as recipient without a custodial account or trust; and failing to file Form 709 for gifts above the exclusion, which leaves the lifetime exemption untracked. The gifts also have to be reconciled with the rest of the plan, since an unequal lifetime gift to one child should be addressed in the will to avoid a dispute, and the beneficiary designations discussed in our post on [beneficiary designations and your will](/beneficiary-designations-override-will) need to reflect any change in who is receiving what.
