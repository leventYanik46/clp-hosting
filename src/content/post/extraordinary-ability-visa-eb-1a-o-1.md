Extraordinary ability visas are for people who have risen to the top of their field and can prove it. The two main options are the EB-1A, a green card, and the O-1, a temporary work visa. Both rely on evidence of sustained acclaim, but they work very differently once the evidence is in.

## What the two visas are

The **EB-1A** is an employment-based green card in the first preference category. You can file it yourself, with no employer sponsor and no PERM labor certification. Approval puts you on the path to permanent residence.

The **O-1** is a nonimmigrant visa that lets you work temporarily in the United States. The O-1A covers extraordinary ability in the sciences, education, business, or athletics. The O-1B covers extraordinary ability in the arts or extraordinary achievement in motion picture and television. An O-1 requires a U.S. employer or agent to file the petition for you.

## Who qualifies

Both categories ask for sustained national or international acclaim and recognition for achievements in your field. People who qualify include researchers, scientists, founders and executives, artists, athletes, and other professionals who stand out from their peers. A title or a degree is not enough. What counts is proof that others in your field recognize your work.

## How USCIS evaluates an EB-1A case

USCIS uses two steps. First, you show either a one-time major internationally recognized award, such as a Nobel Prize, or evidence that meets at least three of ten criteria:

- Nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards
- Membership in associations that require outstanding achievement
- Published material about you in major media or professional publications
- Serving as a judge of the work of others in your field
- Original contributions of major significance to your field
- Authorship of scholarly articles
- Display of your work at exhibitions or showcases
- A leading or critical role for organizations with a distinguished reputation
- A high salary or other significant remuneration compared with others in your field
- Commercial success in the performing arts

Meeting three criteria is only the first step. In the second step, called the final merits determination, USCIS looks at all of the evidence together to decide whether you truly have sustained acclaim and are among the small percentage at the top of your field. Many denied cases meet the criteria on paper but fail this second step.

## How USCIS evaluates an O-1 case

For O-1A, you show either a major internationally recognized award or at least three of eight criteria, which are similar to the EB-1A list, such as awards, membership, published material, judging, original contributions, authorship, a critical role, and high salary. O-1B has its own criteria for arts and film or television. An O-1 petition also needs a consultation, or advisory opinion, from a peer group or labor organization in your field, along with a contract or itinerary of the work you will do.

## Key differences

- **Status.** EB-1A is a green card. O-1 is temporary, typically granted for up to three years at first and extended in increments.
- **Who files.** EB-1A can be self-petitioned. O-1 requires a U.S. employer or agent.
- **Dual intent.** An O-1 holder may pursue a green card while in O-1 status without jeopardizing the visa. Many other temporary visas have stricter rules.
- **Standard.** Many practitioners consider the O-1 to be somewhat more flexible in practice, but that is not a rule, and both require real evidence.
- **Wait times.** An approved EB-1A petition may still wait for a visa number depending on your country of birth. O-1 does not face that backlog.
- **Family.** EB-1A allows a spouse and unmarried children under 21 to receive green cards with you. O-1 family members receive O-3 status, and the spouse generally cannot work.

## Choosing between them

Some people file for an O-1 first to begin working while they build an EB-1A or other green card case. Others go straight to EB-1A if their record is strong and they want permanent residence without relying on an employer. The right choice depends on how much evidence you have, whether you have a U.S. employer, how soon you need to start work, and where you were born.

## What strong evidence looks like

- Independent articles about you and your work, not self-published content
- Letters from recognized experts who explain your impact in specific terms
- Proof of citations, patents, funding, or adoption of your methods
- Records that show you judged or reviewed others' work
- Documents that show a leading role and compensation compared with peers in your field

The narrative matters as much as the pile of documents. A clear explanation of your contributions, supported by independent proof, carries more weight than volume.
