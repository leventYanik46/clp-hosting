There is no statute that says a green card holder may stay outside the United States for exactly six months. There are two thresholds that change how CBP treats you at the airport, and behind both of them sits a question about intent: did you leave temporarily, or did you move? A permanent resident who stays abroad for 170 days a year, every year, with no job or home here can lose the card; one who spends eleven months abroad caring for a dying parent, with a lease and a job waiting, usually keeps it. This post explains how long a green card holder can stay outside the U.S. in practice, what 180 days and one year mean, what CBP can and cannot do, and how to protect your status before a long trip.

## The two thresholds: 180 days and one year

Under INA 101(a)(13)(C), a returning permanent resident is not treated as an applicant for admission unless one of several things is true, and one of them is an absence of more than 180 continuous days. Stay under that mark and the officer's starting point is that you are a resident coming home. Cross it and the officer may treat you as someone seeking admission, place you in secondary inspection, and ask you to prove that your residence is still here.

The second threshold comes from the regulations. A green card is a valid entry document only for a resident returning from a temporary absence of less than one year. After a year abroad you need a re-entry permit obtained before you left or, failing that, an SB-1 returning resident visa from a consulate, which our post on [the SB-1 returning resident visa](/sb-1-returning-resident-visa) explains. The [travel and re-entry permits](/practice-area/key-services/travel-and-reentry-permits) page covers both documents.

## Abandonment is a question of intent

Days are evidence, not the test. Abandonment turns on whether you left with the intent to return after a temporary visit abroad, meaning a trip with a fixed purpose and a foreseeable end. Officers and immigration judges weigh the reason for the trip and its length, whether you kept a home here or sold it, whether you kept a job or took one abroad, where your spouse and children live, whether you filed U.S. tax returns as a resident, and whether you kept bank accounts, a driver's license, insurance and property. A pattern matters more than any single trip. A resident who returns every five months for two weeks and then leaves again is building a record of living abroad, and no single absence under 180 days will save that record.

## What CBP can do at the airport

An officer who suspects abandonment can question you, examine your phone and documents, and ask you to sign Form I-407, the record of abandonment of lawful permanent resident status. Signing is voluntary. You are entitled to refuse, keep your card, and ask to see an immigration judge. If you refuse, CBP may admit you anyway, or it may issue a Notice to Appear and let you in pending a hearing. Only an immigration judge can find that you abandoned residence, and in that hearing the government bears the burden of proving abandonment by clear, unequivocal and convincing evidence. Residents who sign the I-407 under pressure give that protection away in a few seconds. If you have been handed the form, ask for time, and contact our [removal defense](/practice-area/key-services/removal-defense) team before the hearing.

## The tax trap

Filing a U.S. tax return as a nonresident alien, or claiming nonresident treaty benefits to avoid U.S. tax on foreign income, is treated as evidence that you consider yourself a resident elsewhere. The immigration rules and the tax rules both expect a permanent resident to file as a resident on worldwide income. Residents living abroad for work should talk to a tax professional about the foreign earned income exclusion and foreign tax credits rather than switching to a nonresident return.

## Protecting the green card before a long stay abroad

If the absence will approach or exceed a year, obtain a re-entry permit before leaving; our post on [getting a re-entry permit before a long trip](/re-entry-permit-before-long-trip) walks through Form I-131. If you are approaching eligibility for citizenship, remember that abandonment and continuous residence are separate tests, and a trip that is safe for the green card can still reset the naturalization clock, as our post on [continuous residence versus physical presence](/continuous-residence-vs-physical-presence) explains. Keep a U.S. address, a bank account with activity, a current driver's license, tax filings as a resident and, where possible, employment or a lease. Carry evidence of those ties when you return from any trip over a few months. An expired green card is a document problem, not a status problem, but renew it on Form I-90 before long travel because airlines may refuse boarding on an expired card.

## If you have already been away more than a year

Do not board a flight hoping for the best. The options are the SB-1 visa if the delay was beyond your control, a new immigrant petition through a relative or employer if it was not, or presenting at a port of entry with evidence and asking for admission, which can lead to a hearing where the burden is on the government. Each route has different odds and different costs, and the right choice depends on why you stayed. Residents considering citizenship after a long absence should also read our [citizenship and naturalization](/practice-area/key-services/citizenship-and-naturalization) page, because a return from abroad also sets the date from which a new residence period runs.
