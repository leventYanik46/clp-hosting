The bond hearing is a separate proceeding from the removal case, often held within days of a request, and it decides one thing: whether the detained person goes home while the case is pending or stays in custody for months. The immigration judge asks two questions. Is this person a danger to the community? Is this person likely to appear at future hearings? The detained person has the burden on both. Here is how an ICE detention bond hearing is scheduled, what the judge weighs, and what happens afterward.

## How an ICE detention bond hearing gets scheduled

When ICE makes its custody decision on Form I-286, the detained person can check the box requesting review by an immigration judge. If that box was missed, a written motion for a custody redetermination can be filed with the immigration court that has jurisdiction over the facility. Detained courts schedule these hearings quickly, often by video from the detention center. The bond hearing is on a separate record from the removal case; the judge does not decide the charges or any application for relief that day. Because a second request is only available when circumstances change materially, our [bond hearing practice](/practice-area/key-services/immigration-detention-and-bond) treats the first hearing as the only one and prepares accordingly.

## What the immigration judge decides

Under INA 236(a), a person arrested inside the United States and not subject to mandatory detention may be released on bond or on conditions. The governing Board of Immigration Appeals decision, Matter of Guerra, places the burden on the detained person to show that they are not a danger to persons or property and not a flight risk. The judge has broad discretion and may consider any evidence that bears on either question. Danger is examined first; a person found to be a danger is not eligible for bond regardless of flight risk. The factors the Board listed include a fixed address, length of residence, family ties, employment history, record of appearance at court proceedings, criminal record including its recency and seriousness, history of immigration violations, manner of entry, and whether the person has a realistic path to relief that gives them a reason to show up.

## The evidence that matters most

A persuasive bond packet is specific. The documents that move judges most often are:

- Proof of a stable address with a U.S. citizen or permanent resident sponsor, including the sponsor's identification and a lease or deed.
- Evidence of family ties in the United States, especially U.S. citizen children or a spouse, with birth and marriage certificates.
- Certified dispositions for every arrest and charge, with evidence of completed sentences, classes or treatment where relevant.
- Tax returns, pay stubs and an employer's letter showing work history and a job to return to.
- Evidence of eligibility for relief: an approved or pending Form I-130, a filed Form I-589 asylum application, or a clear basis for cancellation of removal.
- Character letters from employers, clergy, teachers and community members that describe specific conduct rather than general praise.

Criminal history deserves the most attention. A judge who sees an arrest with no disposition will assume the charge stands. Where a conviction raises immigration consequences, the interaction between the criminal record and the bond request is the subject of our post on [crimmigration](/what-is-crimmigration).

## What the hearing looks like

Bond hearings are short, often under thirty minutes. Government counsel presents the custody documents and any criminal record, and may argue for no bond or a high bond. The detained person's lawyer presents the packet and may call the sponsor or the detained person to testify briefly. The judge announces a decision from the bench: no bond, release on recognizance, or bond at a set amount. The statute sets a minimum, and judges commonly set amounts well above it where flight risk is a concern. No written decision is issued unless a party appeals, in which case the judge prepares a bond memorandum explaining the reasons.

## After the decision

If bond is set, a U.S. citizen or permanent resident obligor pays it through ICE, and release follows, usually within a day; the mechanics are covered in our post on [paying an immigration bond](/how-to-pay-immigration-bond). If bond is denied or set too high, either side may appeal to the Board within 30 days, and the detained person may file a new request if circumstances change, for example when a family petition is approved or a conviction is vacated. Our [BIA appeals and motions](/practice-area/key-services/bia-appeals-and-motions) team handles bond appeals alongside the habeas option discussed in our post on [what to do when bond is denied](/immigration-bond-denied-next-steps). The government may also appeal a grant of bond, and in some circumstances may invoke an automatic stay that keeps the person detained during the appeal.

## When a bond hearing is not available

The judge cannot set bond for everyone. People held under INA 236(c) because of listed criminal grounds are detained without bond and must instead contest whether the section applies in a Matter of Joseph hearing. People treated as applicants for admission under INA 235(b) are told the judge has no jurisdiction over custody, a category the government has interpreted broadly as of this writing. People with final removal orders are held under a different section with no bond hearing before the judge. In each of those situations the release question moves to ICE or to a federal court, which is why we assess the custody authority before requesting the hearing. The first days after arrest, before the hearing is set, are covered in our post on [getting someone out of ICE detention](/get-someone-out-of-ice-detention).
