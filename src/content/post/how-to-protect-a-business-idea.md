No, you cannot protect a business idea as such. The concept of a subscription service for pet food, a marketplace for used textbooks, or an app that matches tutors with students is free for anyone to copy the day you describe it. What the law protects are the specific things you build on top of the idea: the code and content you write, the name you sell under, the know-how you keep secret, and any invention that meets the patent standard. The practical question is which of those layers applies to your business and in what order to put them in place. Our [intellectual property law team](/practice-area/ip-law) usually answers that question in a single meeting, because the right layers follow from what the business actually does.

## Why ideas themselves are unprotected

Copyright covers original expression, never the idea behind it. The Copyright Act says so directly: protection does not extend to any idea, procedure, process, system, method of operation, concept, or principle. Trademark law protects source identifiers, not business models. Patent law protects inventions that are new, useful, and non-obvious, and a bare business concept rarely clears that bar. Trade secret law comes closest, but it protects information only while it is secret and only if it has value because it is secret.

That leaves a gap, and the gap is deliberate. Competition on ideas is how markets work. The protection you get is for execution, so the strategy is to execute in a way that creates protectable assets as early as possible.

## Layer one: keep it secret and use NDAs

Before launch, the idea and the details around it (the pricing model, the supplier list, the unlaunched feature set) can be trade secrets if you treat them that way. That means sharing them only under a signed non-disclosure agreement, limiting who inside the company has access, and marking documents as confidential. The test for what counts is explained in our post on [what qualifies as a trade secret](/what-qualifies-as-a-trade-secret).

An NDA is a contract, so it binds only the people who sign it. Investors routinely refuse to sign NDAs at the pitch stage, and pushing the point can end the conversation. The usual approach is to pitch the problem and the traction without the implementation detail, and save the detail for diligence, when an NDA is customary. For contractors, developers, and potential partners, an NDA before the first substantive conversation is standard and should never be skipped. CLP's [trade secret protection](/practice-area/key-services/trade-secret-protection) page outlines the policies that make those agreements enforceable.

## Layer two: copyright for what you write and build

The moment you write code, draft a website, record a video, or design a logo, copyright exists in that work. It stops others from copying the work itself, though not from building a competing product with their own code. Registration with the Copyright Office is optional but inexpensive, and it is required before suing over a U.S. work. It does not create the right, so we should be precise: what it adds is the ability to claim statutory damages and attorney's fees if you register before the infringement starts or within three months of first publication. Our post on [why registration matters even though copyright is automatic](/is-copyright-automatic-why-register) covers the timing.

Copyright is the layer that protects the most startups in practice, because competitors who want to move fast are tempted to copy interfaces, content, and code wholesale. Make sure the company owns it: contractor agreements need assignment language, or the freelancer who built your app owns the app.

## Layer three: a trademark for the name

The name and logo are often the most valuable asset a young company has, because they are what customers remember. Trademark rights in the United States arise from use in commerce, but a federal registration at the USPTO adds nationwide priority, a legal presumption of ownership, and the ability to stop imports and online counterfeits. Before you commit to a name, run a clearance search. Adopting a name that conflicts with an existing registration means a rebrand later, at the point when the brand is finally worth something. Our [trademark search and clearance](/practice-area/key-services/trademark-search-and-clearance) page explains what a proper search covers. File an intent-to-use application as soon as the name is chosen, before launch, to lock in priority.

## Layer four: a patent, if the invention qualifies

If the business involves a new technical solution (a novel algorithm applied to a specific problem, a device, a process, a formulation), a patent may be available. Patents give a right to exclude others for twenty years from filing, in exchange for full public disclosure. Two timing rules matter. Public disclosure, sale, or public use of the invention starts a one-year clock in the United States, after which a patent is barred, and most foreign countries allow no grace period at all. A provisional application is a lower-cost way to secure a filing date and gives twelve months to file the full application. Patent prosecution requires a registered patent practitioner, and we coordinate with patent counsel when a client's roadmap calls for it.

## Putting the layers in order to protect a business idea

For most early businesses, the sequence is: NDAs and internal confidentiality from day one; founder and contractor IP assignments at formation; a trademark clearance search and intent-to-use filing once the name is chosen; copyright registration of the core code and content around launch; and a patent evaluation only where there is a genuine technical invention and a budget for it. The idea stays unprotected throughout. The business built on it does not.
