The J-1 and H-1B both bring professionals, researchers, and physicians to the U.S., but they are built for different purposes. The J-1 is an exchange visitor visa for training, research, teaching, and cultural exchange. The H-1B is a work visa for specialty occupations. The biggest practical difference is the two-year home residency requirement that can attach to a J-1, which can block an H-1B or green card later.

## J-1 vs. H-1B at a glance

**Purpose.** J-1: exchange programs with a defined educational or training goal. H-1B: regular employment in a degree-level job.

**Sponsor.** J-1: a State Department designated program sponsor, often a university or exchange organization, issues Form DS-2019. H-1B: the employer files a petition with USCIS.

**Lottery.** J-1: none. H-1B: most private-sector employers must win the annual lottery. Universities and certain research institutions are cap-exempt.

**Length of stay.** Varies by J-1 category. Research scholars and professors can stay up to five years. Trainees and interns have much shorter limits. H-1B: up to six years, with extensions possible for green card applicants.

**Dual intent.** H-1B allows dual intent, so a green card case does not conflict with the visa. J-1 does not.

**Cost.** J-1 costs are mostly program fees and the SEVIS fee. H-1B petitions carry several employer-paid government fees, and for certain workers outside the U.S., the $100,000 payment under the September 2025 proclamation.

## The two-year home residency requirement

Some J-1 holders are subject to INA 212(e). If you are, you must spend two years in your home country after the program before you can:

- Change status to H-1B or L-1 inside the U.S.
- Get an H-1B, L-1, or K visa at a consulate
- Become a permanent resident

You may be subject to it if your program was funded by the U.S. or your government, if your skills appear on your country's Skills List, or if you came for graduate medical education or training. Check your DS-2019 and J-1 visa annotation, but the annotation is not final. An advisory opinion from the State Department can confirm.

If 212(e) applies, you will need to complete the two years or obtain a waiver before an H-1B or green card can move forward.

## Other J-1 limits

Research scholars and professors who complete a program generally face a two-year bar before returning in the same J-1 category, and a 12-month bar can apply to some short-term programs. These are separate from the 212(e) requirement.

## J-1 to green card

Without 212(e), a J-1 holder can pursue a green card through an employer, an EB-2 NIW, EB-1, or family. With 212(e), the waiver comes first. Physicians often use the Conrad 30 program or other interested government agency waivers, which require service in an underserved area.

## Which one fits?

- A university hiring a visiting researcher for a defined project: J-1 is often simpler
- A company hiring a permanent employee: H-1B
- A researcher planning to stay and pursue a green card: H-1B, or a J-1 not subject to 212(e)
- A J-1 already in the U.S. with no 212(e) issue: change of status to H-1B avoids the $100,000 payment when granted


## Frequently asked questions

### How do I know if I am subject to the two-year rule?

Look at your DS-2019 and visa annotation, and request an advisory opinion from the State Department if unclear. The annotation is a preliminary indication, not a final ruling.

### Can J-1 physicians get an H-1B?

Yes, usually after a waiver such as a Conrad 30 waiver, which often requires an H-1B role in an underserved area for three years.

### Can my J-2 spouse work?

J-2 spouses can apply for work authorization from USCIS. H-4 spouses cannot work unless they qualify for an H-4 EAD.

### Can a J-1 change to H-1B without leaving?

Yes, if the J-1 holder is not subject to the two-year home residency requirement, or has obtained a waiver. Changing status inside the U.S. avoids the $100,000 proclamation payment when the change is granted.
