On May 21, 2026, USCIS issued policy memorandum PM-602-0199, which describes adjustment of status as "a matter of discretion and administrative grace" and an "extraordinary" form of relief. A USCIS announcement the next day said consular processing abroad should be the ordinary route to a green card. The memo does not change the law or abolish adjustment of status, but it gives officers broader room to deny adjustment applications, even from people who meet every eligibility requirement.

## What the memo says

The core points of the memo are:

- Adjustment of status under section 245 of the Immigration and Nationality Act is discretionary, so meeting the eligibility requirements does not guarantee approval.
- Congress designed adjustment as an exception to consular processing, not a substitute for it.
- Officers should weigh all relevant factors in the totality of the circumstances.
- Choosing to adjust when consular processing is available can itself be weighed against the applicant in some cases.

Factors the memo and commentators identify as potentially adverse include failing to depart when the purpose of an admission or parole was accomplished, past immigration violations, and an apparent intent to avoid consular processing from the start, sometimes called "preconceived intent."

## Who is not affected

The memo recognizes that some categories are non-discretionary or have no consular alternative. These include adjustment for refugees and asylees under section 209 and categories such as the Cuban Adjustment Act, where the applicant cannot process abroad in the same way. In those cases, the memo's discretionary framing does not apply in the same way.

## Who is most affected

The memo reaches broadly, but practitioners expect the most scrutiny for:

- **Family-based applicants who entered on a visitor or student visa** and then married or applied soon after arrival.
- **Applicants with past status violations**, even ones adjustment law forgives for immediate relatives.
- **Employment-based applicants** whose dual-intent status, such as H-1B or L-1, is maintained. The memo states that maintaining lawful status is not enough by itself to warrant favorable discretion, though it does not eliminate employment-based adjustment.

## What applicants are seeing in practice

Reports since the memo show uneven implementation. Some interviews have included questions about why the applicant chose adjustment instead of consular processing. Some pending cases have received requests for evidence on discretionary factors. Others have been approved without any reference to the memo. Legal challenges were expected, so check for developments before relying on any specific reading.

## How to prepare an adjustment of status case now

**Document positive factors.** Family ties in the U.S., U.S. citizen children, long residence, employment, tax compliance, community involvement, and hardship that departure would cause are all relevant.

**Address the reason for adjusting.** If departing would trigger a bar to reentry, separate you from a U.S. citizen spouse or child, or interrupt employment, explain it with evidence.

**Be honest about your entry.** Inconsistencies between your entry purpose and later actions are a focus. Have a truthful explanation ready and supported by documents.

**Compare consular processing seriously.** For some applicants, especially those with no prior violations and no risk of bars, consular processing may now be more predictable. For others, departure would trigger the three-year or ten-year bar or face a long consular backlog, and adjustment remains the only practical route.

## Do not leave the U.S. without advice

Leaving to consular process can carry serious risks: unlawful presence bars, consular delays, travel restrictions affecting some nationalities, and administrative processing. The memo does not make those risks disappear.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does the memo apply to cases filed before May 2026?
The memo did not clearly exempt pending cases, and reports show some pending cases receiving requests for evidence on discretion. Plan as if it could apply.

### Is the memo being challenged in court?
Legal challenges were widely expected. Check for current rulings before making a decision based on the memo.

### Should I withdraw my adjustment application and consular process instead?
Not without advice. Withdrawing and leaving can trigger bars to return or long consular waits.

### Does it affect H-1B workers adjusting through their employers?
It can. The memo says maintaining lawful status is not enough by itself to warrant discretion, but it does not eliminate employment-based adjustment.

### What evidence shows favorable discretion?
Long residence, family ties, employment, tax compliance, community involvement, and hardship from departure are common positive factors.

### Are asylees affected?
Adjustment for refugees and asylees under section 209 follows a different, non-discretionary framework, so the memo's approach does not apply in the same way.
