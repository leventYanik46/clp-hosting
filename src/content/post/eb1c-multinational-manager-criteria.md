The EB-1C green card is for managers and executives who work for a multinational company, and it does not require a PERM labor certification. That makes it one of the faster employment-based routes, but it is also one of the most closely examined, because the two things USCIS has to believe are hard to prove on paper: that your role is truly managerial or executive, and that the companies are really related.

## The core EB-1C criteria

To qualify, four things have to be true:

1. **You worked abroad for a related company.** You must have been employed by the foreign entity for at least one continuous year in the three years before your entry to the United States, or before the petition if you are already here in another status.
2. **That job was managerial or executive.** Not just a senior title.
3. **A qualifying relationship exists.** The U.S. company and the foreign company must be a parent, subsidiary, branch or affiliate of each other, and that relationship must exist when the petition is filed.
4. **The U.S. job is also managerial or executive,** and the U.S. company has been doing business for at least a year.

The U.S. employer files the petition. This is not a self-petition.

## What counts as manager or executive

The regulations use specific definitions, and USCIS applies them closely.

- **A manager** typically directs a department, function or subdivision, supervises professional or managerial staff, or manages an essential function, and has authority over hiring and firing or personnel recommendations.
- **An executive** sets goals and policies, directs management, has wide discretion, and takes direction only from higher executives, the board, or shareholders.

Time spent on day-to-day operational tasks does not disqualify you, but it has to be a small part of the job. If the petition describes you doing the same work as your staff, an officer will question it.

## Common pitfalls

- **Vague job descriptions.** A description that says "oversees operations and strategy" with no detail on staff, decisions, or time allocation tends to draw an RFE.
- **Small companies.** A tiny U.S. office with few employees is not automatically disqualified, but USCIS wants to know who actually does the operational work if the beneficiary is a manager. Be ready to show how the business functions.
- **Managing a function without managing people.** This can qualify, but needs more evidence of the function's importance and your control over it.
- **Weak proof of the corporate relationship.** Ownership and control must be documented through stock certificates, share registers, tax returns and other records, not just asserted.
- **"Doing business" gaps.** The U.S. entity has to show regular, continuous activity, not just a registered address.
- **Specialized knowledge confusion.** People holding L-1B status often assume they qualify. EB-1C does not accept specialized knowledge on its own.

## Why the approval rate question comes with caveats

People often ask about the EB-1C approval rate. USCIS data can give a general picture, but results depend heavily on the quality of the evidence, the size and structure of the company, and the duties of the role. A strong filing for an established multinational faces a very different review than a thin filing for a new office.

## Preparing a strong petition

Gather organizational charts for both companies, detailed duty descriptions with approximate time allocations, ownership documents, and evidence of staff the beneficiary supervises. Letters from the foreign employer should be specific.
