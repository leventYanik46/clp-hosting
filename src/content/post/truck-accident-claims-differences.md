A truck accident claim is not a car accident claim with bigger numbers. The defendant is usually a company, not a driver. The standard of care is set by federal regulations, not just state traffic law. The most important evidence is electronic, held by the other side, and routinely overwritten within months. And the insurer on the other side often has an investigator at the scene before the injured person has left the hospital. Understanding these differences explains why the first weeks matter so much and why the strategy diverges from an ordinary crash case.

## More than one defendant

In a car crash there is usually one at-fault driver and one liability policy. In a commercial truck crash, responsibility can be spread across several parties: the driver, the motor carrier that employed or leased the driver, the owner of the tractor, the owner of the trailer, the company that loaded the cargo, the broker that arranged the shipment, and the shop that maintained the brakes. Each may have its own insurer and its own reasons to blame the others.

Identifying every potentially responsible party early matters for two reasons. The available insurance is the sum of their policies, and the deadline to sue applies to each of them separately. Our [truck accident team](/practice-area/key-services/car-truck-and-motorcycle-accidents) starts with the carrier's federal registration and the information on the crash report, then follows the lease, ownership, and shipping documents to find the rest.

## Federal rules set the standard of care

Interstate motor carriers and their drivers are governed by the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Regulations. Those rules limit how many hours a driver can be on duty and behind the wheel, require commercial driver licensing and medical certification, mandate drug and alcohol testing, require regular vehicle inspection and maintenance, and set standards for securing cargo. Most interstate carriers must use electronic logging devices to track hours of service.

A violation of these rules is powerful evidence of negligence. A driver who exceeded the hours-of-service limit, a carrier that put an unqualified driver on the road, or a company that skipped required inspections has breached a standard written specifically to prevent the kind of crash that happened. Proving that breach follows the same four-element framework we describe in our post on [how to prove negligence in a personal injury claim](/how-to-prove-negligence-injury-claim), but the evidence lives in the carrier's files, not on the roadside.

## Evidence that disappears unless someone demands it

The decisive evidence in a truck case is often electronic and short-lived.

- The engine control module records speed, braking, throttle, and other data from the seconds before a collision; it can be lost when the truck is repaired or returned to service.
- Electronic logging device records show hours of service, and federal rules require carriers to retain them for only six months.
- Dashcam and cab-facing camera footage is typically overwritten on a rolling basis.
- Dispatch records, bills of lading, GPS tracking, driver qualification files, and maintenance logs are kept by the carrier and may be purged on ordinary schedules.

A preservation letter, sent to the carrier and its insurer as soon as possible, puts them on notice to keep this material. If it is destroyed after notice, courts can impose sanctions and instruct juries to presume it was unfavorable. Without notice, its loss is simply a gap in your proof. Our post on [what to do in the first week after a car accident](/first-week-after-car-accident) covers the general steps; in a truck case, the preservation letter is the most important one.

## The insurer moves faster and harder

Commercial carriers are required to carry much higher minimum liability coverage than private drivers, and their insurers protect that exposure aggressively. Many carriers have rapid response teams of adjusters, investigators, and defense counsel who are dispatched to serious crashes within hours. They photograph the scene, interview witnesses, and sometimes approach the injured person or family with early contact and offers. Their job is to build the carrier's defense before the other side has started. The practical response is the same as in any claim, but with less room for error: do not give statements, do not sign anything, and do not accept early offers.

## Injuries, damages, and death

The physics of a crash between a passenger car and a vehicle weighing many times more produce catastrophic injuries at a much higher rate: spinal cord damage, traumatic brain injury, amputations, and burns. Damages in these cases involve lifetime medical costs, loss of earning capacity, and permanent changes to daily life, which usually require economists and life care planners to quantify. When the crash is fatal, the claim becomes a wrongful death action brought by the estate's personal representative, with its own beneficiaries and deadlines; our [wrongful death](/practice-area/key-services/wrongful-death) page explains how those cases proceed.

In Virginia and DC, contributory negligence still applies, and carriers routinely argue that the car driver cut in front of the truck, lingered in a blind spot, or stopped suddenly. The truck's own electronic data is often the best answer to that argument, which is another reason it must be preserved.

## What to do now

If you or a family member were hit by a commercial truck, get the crash report, write down everything you remember about the truck and its markings, keep every medical record, and get advice before anyone from the carrier's side contacts you. The preservation letter should go out this week, not after you have recovered.
