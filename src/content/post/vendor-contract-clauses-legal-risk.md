Most vendor contract risk is decided in about six clauses: limitation of liability, indemnification, insurance, data security, audit rights, and termination. The rest of the agreement matters, but those six determine who pays when the vendor's software goes down, its technician injures someone on your premises, or its systems leak your customers' data. Many businesses sign the vendor's standard paper because the price and service terms look right, then learn during the first serious problem that the vendor owes them a refund of one month's fees and nothing more.

This post covers what each clause does, what to ask for, and where vendors usually push back. Vendor terms are often the largest unexamined exposure we find in the [legal risk management and compliance reviews](/practice-area/key-services/risk-management-and-legal-compliance) we run for growing companies.

## Limitation of liability: the clause that matters most

A limitation of liability clause caps how much either party can recover, usually at the fees paid over a set period, and often excludes indirect or consequential damages such as lost profits. Vendors propose low caps because their pricing assumes limited exposure. What to check:

- Whether the cap is mutual or protects only the vendor.
- The size of the cap compared to the real harm a failure could cause you. A cap equal to three months of fees may be fine for an office supply contract and inadequate for the system that runs your payroll.
- Carve-outs, meaning claims that sit outside the cap. Common ones are breach of confidentiality, data security breaches, indemnification obligations, gross negligence or willful misconduct, and infringement. A vendor may accept a higher separate cap for data breaches rather than unlimited exposure, which is often a reasonable middle ground.
- Whether the consequential damages exclusion swallows your actual loss. If your main harm from a breach would be notification costs, regulatory fines, and lost customers, an exclusion of indirect damages may wipe out your recovery unless those categories are named as recoverable.

## Indemnification and insurance

Indemnification shifts the cost of third-party claims. If the vendor's product infringes someone's patent, or its employee injures a visitor, an indemnity clause makes the vendor defend and pay the claim. Check who is covered (you, your affiliates, your officers), what triggers the obligation (negligence, breach, infringement, violation of law), and how the defense works, including who picks the lawyers and who approves a settlement.

An indemnity is only worth what the vendor can pay, which is why insurance matters. Ask for proof of coverage that matches the risk: general liability, professional liability for service providers, cyber liability for anyone handling your data, and workers' compensation for vendors whose staff work on your premises. Ask to be named as an additional insured where it fits, and require notice if coverage is canceled.

## Data security, privacy, and audit rights

If a vendor stores, processes, or can reach personal data or confidential business information, the contract should set minimum security standards, limit use of your data to providing the service, require prompt notice of a security incident, and spell out who handles and pays for breach response. Every U.S. state has a data breach notification law, and the duty to notify affected people usually falls on the business that owns the data, not on the vendor that lost it. The contract is how you recover those costs.

Audit rights let you verify compliance through questionnaires, independent audit reports such as SOC 2, or on-site review for high-risk vendors. If you are in a regulated industry, check whether your regulator expects a specific level of vendor oversight, and make sure the contract gives you the access to provide it. Require the vendor to pass the same obligations down to its subcontractors.

## Termination and exit terms

The exit clause gets the least attention during negotiation and the most during a dispute.

### Termination rights
Can you terminate for convenience, or only for cause? How long is the cure period for a breach? Does the contract auto-renew, and how much notice must you give to stop it?

### Transition assistance
For any vendor that holds your data or runs a core function, require the vendor to return your data in a usable format and to cooperate with a handover for a defined period after termination.

### Governing law and disputes
Vendor paper often picks the vendor's home state law and courts, or requires arbitration. That can make even a strong claim expensive to pursue. Our post on [mediation versus arbitration for business disputes](/mediation-vs-arbitration-business-disputes) explains what those clauses mean in practice.

## How to prioritize your vendor contracts

You do not need to renegotiate every agreement. Rank vendors by what a failure would cost: access to sensitive data, importance to operations, contract value, and how hard the vendor would be to replace. Put your effort into the top tier and use a standard addendum for the rest. Before each renewal, review the top-tier contracts against your current risk, since a vendor that held a small slice of your data three years ago may now hold all of it.
