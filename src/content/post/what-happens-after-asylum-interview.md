After the asylum interview, USCIS has three ways to decide your case: a grant, a referral to immigration court, or a notice of intent to deny. Which of the last two you receive depends on whether you still hold lawful status, not on how weak the officer found the claim. The decision is almost always mailed rather than announced in the room, and the wait can be short or long depending on background checks and supervisory review. Our [affirmative asylum (Form I-589) team](/practice-area/key-services/affirmative-asylum-applications) prepares clients for all three outcomes before the interview, because the next step for each is different and some of them have deadlines measured in days.

## The wait after the asylum interview

The officer writes an assessment that a supervisor reviews, and the decision cannot issue until your fingerprint and name checks clear. If the officer intends to grant but the checks are pending, you may receive a recommended approval, which is a letter saying the case will be approved once the checks complete. A recommended approval is not a grant; you cannot file for a green card or an I-730 on the strength of it, though it does allow you to apply for work authorization.

While you wait, keep your address current with USCIS on Form AR-11 and tell the asylum office directly, because a decision mailed to an old address is still a decision. Do not travel outside the United States; departure without advance parole abandons the application. The 180-day clock that governs work authorization keeps running after the interview as long as you have not caused a delay, so an applicant who filed Form I-765 on day 150 can receive an EAD while the decision is pending.

## Outcome one: asylum is granted

A grant arrives as an approval letter and an I-94 record showing asylee status for you and for any spouse or child included on the application. Work authorization comes with the status; you can apply for an EAD card and a Social Security number. From the grant date, several clocks start, and the list below is the one we hand clients with the approval.

- Apply for a refugee travel document on Form I-131 before any trip abroad, and do not travel on your home country's passport.
- File Form I-730 within two years for a spouse or unmarried child under 21 who was not included in the grant.
- Count one year of physical presence, then file Form I-485 for a green card; our post on [when an asylee can apply for a green card](/when-can-asylee-apply-for-green-card) covers the counting.
- Keep the approval letter and I-94 safe; you will need them for every later filing, including the [green card through asylum](/practice-area/key-services/green-card-through-asylum) application and eventually naturalization.

## Outcome two: referral to immigration court

If you have no lawful status and the officer does not grant, USCIS does not deny the application. It refers the case to the immigration court by issuing a Notice to Appear, and the referral notice states briefly why the officer could not approve. A referral is not a finding that you lied. Many referrals rest on the one-year deadline, on a gap in corroboration, or on a legal question about the protected ground, and the judge decides all of it afresh.

In court, the same I-589 continues; you do not refile, though you can and should update it. The hearing is adversarial: a government attorney cross-examines you, and your interview testimony is part of the record the judge compares against. The judge also considers withholding of removal and protection under the Convention Against Torture, which USCIS could not grant. You will first attend a master calendar hearing to set the schedule and then an individual hearing on the merits, as described in our overview of [removal proceedings](/removal-proceedings). This is the point at which the case moves to our [removal defense attorneys](/practice-area/key-services/removal-defense), who often request the officer's interview notes and assessment through a FOIA request so the referral reasons can be answered directly.

## Outcome three: a notice of intent to deny

If you are still in valid status, as an F-1 student, an H-1B worker, or a TPS holder for example, USCIS cannot refer you to court because there is no basis to remove you. Instead it issues a notice of intent to deny, a NOID, that sets out the reasons and gives you 16 days to respond. The response is your one chance to add evidence or argument before a final decision, so it should address every reason the notice lists, with documents, and should be sent in a way that proves delivery.

After the response, USCIS either grants or issues a final denial. A final denial cannot be appealed. You keep whatever status you hold, and you may ask the asylum office to reopen or reconsider if you have new evidence or can show legal error. If you later fall out of status and are placed in removal proceedings, you can renew the asylum claim before the immigration judge, who is not bound by the earlier decision. If your status ends while the NOID is pending, USCIS converts the case to a referral.

## What the decision does to your work permit and your family

A referral does not cancel a pending or approved asylum-based EAD; the clock continues through the court case as long as you do not cause delays, and renewals continue while the case, and any appeal, remains pending. A grant replaces that EAD with one based on asylee status. A final denial after a NOID ends asylum-based work authorization, which matters most for applicants whose underlying status does not allow work.

Family abroad can be petitioned only after a grant. If the case is referred, the I-730 waits for the judge's decision, and if it is granted in court, the two-year window runs from the judge's order. Family members in the United States who were included on the application follow the same path you do, in whichever direction it goes.
