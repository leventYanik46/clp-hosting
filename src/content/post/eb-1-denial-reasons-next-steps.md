An EB-1 denial arrives as a written decision that lists the grounds, and that document is the most useful thing you own at that moment. It tells you whether the officer found the criteria unmet, found the criteria met but the final merits lacking, or found a structural defect such as a non-permanent job offer or an unqualified corporate relationship. Each of those calls for a different response. The firm's [EB-1 petition practice](/practice-area/key-services/eb-1-green-card) reviews denials of petitions prepared elsewhere as well as our own, and the decision path below is the one we use.

## Why EB-1 petitions get denied, by subcategory

### EB-1A

The most common ground is the final merits determination: the officer accepts three or more criteria and then concludes that the record as a whole does not show sustained national or international acclaim. The second is the criteria themselves: awards that are internal or student-level, memberships that require only a fee or a degree, press that mentions the beneficiary in passing, and contributions described as significant without evidence that anyone relied on them. Our post on [the EB-1A final merits determination](/eb-1a-final-merits-determination) explains the first ground in depth.

### EB-1B

The job offer fails more often than the researcher does. A position funded year to year with no expectation of continued employment, a postdoctoral appointment with an end date, or a private employer that cannot show three full-time researchers and documented accomplishments will sink the petition regardless of the citation record. Experience is the second issue: fewer than three years of post-degree teaching or research, or reliance on doctoral years without the regulatory exception.

### EB-1C

Duties and structure. USCIS denies when the beneficiary's foreign or U.S. role is primarily operational, when the staff supervised are not professionals or managers, when the qualifying relationship between the two companies is not documented through ownership and control evidence, or when the U.S. entity has not been doing business for a full year.

## Read the decision before choosing a remedy

The denial notice explains your appeal and motion rights and states the deadline. For most I-140 denials you have 30 days from the date of the decision to file Form I-290B, with three additional days if the decision was mailed. That window is short and fixed; the first task after a denial is to calendar it.

Then classify the problem. Was it a legal error, meaning the officer applied the wrong standard or ignored evidence that was in the file? Was it a factual gap, meaning the evidence that would have answered the point exists but was never submitted? Or was it a structural defect that no additional evidence can cure, such as a job offer that really is temporary? The answer points to the remedy.

## Your options after an EB-1 denial

- Motion to reconsider (Form I-290B). You argue that the decision was wrong on the law or policy as applied to the evidence already in the record. No new evidence is allowed. This fits a legal error, for example an officer who demanded that each criterion independently prove acclaim rather than reserving that question for the final merits stage.
- Motion to reopen (Form I-290B). You submit new facts and documents that were not before the officer. This fits a factual gap where strong evidence exists, such as citation data or independent letters that were omitted.
- Appeal to the Administrative Appeals Office (Form I-290B). The office that issued the denial reviews the appeal first and may treat it as a motion and reverse itself; otherwise the AAO reviews the record and the reasoning. Appeals take longer than motions and the AAO applies the law strictly, but a reasoned AAO decision can be valuable when the issue is one of interpretation.
- A new petition. Often the fastest and cleanest route when the record can be improved. Nothing prevents refiling. A denied petition establishes no priority date, so the new I-140 takes its own receipt date; a successful motion or appeal, by contrast, restores the original filing date. If the first petition was filed in the wrong subcategory, the new one can be filed in the right one, or in the EB-2 National Interest Waiver instead. Our [EB-2 NIW green card](/practice-area/key-services/eb-2-niw-green-card) page explains that alternative.

You may combine approaches, for example filing a motion to reopen and a new, stronger petition at the same time.

## Effects on status and on pending applications

A denied I-140 does not by itself end your nonimmigrant status, but it can affect what depends on it. An I-485 filed on the basis of the denied petition will be denied unless another approved petition with a current priority date can serve as the basis. A denied I-140 also cannot support an H-1B extension beyond the sixth year. If you are in O-1, H-1B or L-1 status with time remaining, the status continues and you can refile from inside the United States.

A denial also becomes part of your immigration history. Later officers will see it, so the new petition should address the earlier grounds directly rather than hoping they go unnoticed.

## Avoiding the denial in the first place

Most EB-1 denials are foreseeable at the Request for Evidence stage. Treat an RFE as a preview of the denial and answer every point with documents, not assertions; our post on [responding to an EB-1A RFE](/eb-1a-rfe-response) explains how. If the petition has instead gone silent for far longer than posted processing times, the problem is delay rather than denial, and a [mandamus action](/practice-area/key-services/immigration-mandamus) to compel a decision is the relevant tool.
