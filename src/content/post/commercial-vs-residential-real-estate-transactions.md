The deed looks the same and the closing table looks familiar, but almost everything in between is different. A residential purchase runs on standardized contract forms, federal consumer protections, and a lender that underwrites you. A commercial purchase runs on a negotiated contract, a due diligence period you define, and a lender that underwrites the property's income. The differences between commercial vs residential real estate transactions change how you prepare, what you sign, and where the risk sits.

## The contract itself

Residential contracts in the District and Northern Virginia are usually prepared by agents on regional association forms with limited room for negotiation beyond price, dates, and a handful of addenda. In New York, attorneys draft the residential contract from a bar association form, but the structure is still standardized. Commercial purchase agreements start from a letter of intent and are drafted from scratch for the deal: representations and warranties with survival periods, indemnities with caps and baskets, as-is clauses with negotiated carve-outs, assignment rights, and cooperation clauses for a tax-deferred exchange. Our note on [whether a letter of intent is binding](/is-a-letter-of-intent-binding) explains why the commercial process starts a step earlier.

## Due diligence and contingencies

A residential buyer protects themselves with contingencies: inspection, financing, appraisal, and title, each with a deadline and a defined remedy. A commercial buyer instead negotiates a due diligence period during which the buyer can terminate for any reason and recover the deposit, and uses that period for title and survey review, zoning confirmation, environmental assessment, lease and estoppel review, and a property condition report. When the period ends, the deposit typically becomes non-refundable. Our [commercial property due diligence checklist](/commercial-property-due-diligence-checklist) lists what belongs in that window. The practical difference is responsibility: the residential forms tell the buyer what to check, while the commercial buyer decides for themselves and lives with what they missed.

## Financing and consumer protections

Residential mortgage loans are governed by federal consumer rules. The lender must deliver a Loan Estimate and a Closing Disclosure on prescribed forms, the Closing Disclosure must arrive at least three business days before consummation, and the borrower is an individual whose income and credit are underwritten. Commercial loans have none of that. The lender issues a commitment letter, underwrites the property's debt service coverage and loan-to-value, requires an entity borrower with a personal guaranty or recourse carve-outs, and sends its own counsel to review title, leases, and organizational documents. One line matters for small investors: a building with up to four units is generally treated as residential for lending purposes, while five or more units is commercial multifamily with commercial loan terms.

Our [residential and commercial transaction counsel](/practice-area/key-services/guidance-in-commercial-and-residential-real-estate-transactions) handles both kinds of closings, and the shift from a consumer-protected loan to a negotiated commercial loan is where first-time investors most often underestimate the work.

## Parties, entities, and closing mechanics

Residential buyers and sellers are usually individuals, sometimes a trust. Commercial parties are almost always entities, which adds organizational documents, authority resolutions, and good standing certificates to the closing checklist, along with title endorsements (zoning, access, contiguity, and others) that residential policies rarely include. Commercial closings are often conducted in escrow, with documents delivered to the title company ahead of time and released when funds arrive, rather than at a single sit-down. If you are considering an entity for an investment purchase, our discussion of [buying property through an LLC](/buying-property-through-an-llc) covers the financing and tax consequences.

## Tenants and leases

A residential buyer usually takes possession of an empty house. A commercial buyer takes the property subject to existing leases, which means the leases are the asset. The buyer reviews the rent roll, obtains estoppel certificates from tenants confirming the lease terms and the absence of defaults, takes assignment of the security deposits, and sends notices directing tenants to pay the new owner. Tenant rights can also shape the sale: the District's tenant opportunity to purchase rules give tenants of rental buildings notice and purchase rights before a sale closes, and that process must be built into the timeline. Our [acquisitions, leasing, and development](/practice-area/key-services/property-acquisitions-leasing-development) practice reviews leases as part of every income-property purchase.

## Taxes and timing

Transfer and recordation taxes apply to both kinds of deals, and the rates vary by jurisdiction and sometimes by price or property type, so check the current schedule. Investors selling one property to buy another can defer gain through a like-kind exchange, which imposes fixed identification and closing deadlines that the purchase contract must accommodate. Residential sellers of a principal residence rely on a different exclusion. Both sides prorate taxes and operating income at closing, but a commercial closing also prorates rents, reconciles operating expense pass-throughs, and settles leasing commissions and tenant improvement obligations that run with the leases.

The common thread is that commercial deals replace protective rules with negotiated terms. That is an advantage for a buyer who prepares and a trap for one who assumes the residential safety net is still there.
