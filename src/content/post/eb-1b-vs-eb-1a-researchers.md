A researcher with a permanent offer from a university often has two first preference options on the table at once, and the choice between EB-1B vs EB-1A is rarely about which one you can win. It is about who controls the petition, how high the standard sits, and what happens if you change jobs before the green card is issued. The two categories share a form, a preference level and the Kazarian two-step analysis, and they diverge on almost everything else. Our [EB-1B practice for researchers and institutions](/practice-area/key-services/eb-1b-green-card) handles both, frequently together; this post sets out how we weigh them.

## EB-1B vs EB-1A: the standard

The EB-1A requires sustained national or international acclaim and a record showing you are among the small percentage at the very top of your field. You document at least three of ten criteria, and the final merits determination asks whether the whole record shows acclaim at that level.

The EB-1B requires international recognition as outstanding in a specific academic area, at least three years of teaching or research experience, and at least two of six criteria, followed by a final merits review against the outstanding standard. Outstanding is a lower bar than the very top of the field, and two criteria are fewer than three. For most academics, publications and peer review satisfy the EB-1B count immediately, while the EB-1A count requires something more: press, awards, a critical role or high pay. Our post on [who qualifies for the EB-1B](/eb-1b-outstanding-researcher-requirements) covers the elements in detail.

In short, the same record is more likely to be approved as an EB-1B than as an EB-1A. That is the EB-1B's main advantage, and it is a real one.

## Who files, and who owns the case

The EB-1A is a self-petition. You sign Form I-140 as petitioner, no job offer is required, and the approval belongs to you. If you move from one university to another, or leave academia for industry, the petition and the priority date travel with you, so long as you continue working in your field. Our post on [filing EB-1A without a job offer](/file-eb-1a-without-job-offer) explains that freedom.

The EB-1B is the employer's petition. The institution signs the I-140, must offer a permanent position, must prove its ability to pay the wage, and can withdraw the petition before approval. After approval, the petition still ties your green card to that position: a new employer cannot use it unless your Form I-485 has been pending at least 180 days and the new job is in the same or a similar occupation. If the I-140 has been approved for 180 days when the employer withdraws, you keep the priority date, but you still need a new petition to finish. For a researcher whose next move is uncertain, that dependence is the EB-1B's main cost.

There is a practical point as well. Some institutions have policies about which categories they will sponsor, how quickly they move, and who pays. An EB-1B depends on the department's willingness to sign and on the offer letter being drafted correctly; an EB-1A depends only on you.

## Position and timing constraints

The EB-1B requires a position that is tenured, tenure-track or of indefinite duration with an expectation of continued employment. A postdoctoral appointment with an end date does not qualify, so a postdoc choosing between the categories often has no EB-1B choice at all until a permanent offer arrives. The EB-1A has no position requirement; a postdoc with a strong record can file today.

At the green card stage the two are treated alike. Both are first preference, so the Visa Bulletin applies the same cutoff dates, and an approved I-140 in either category gives you the same place in line for the same country of birth.

## Risk, cost and the case for filing both

The EB-1A carries a higher denial risk because of the final merits standard. The EB-1B carries a structural risk: the job offer or the employer's research department can fail the regulatory test regardless of your record. Each petition requires its own filing fee, its own evidence set and its own brief, and premium processing is requested separately.

Many researchers file both. The institution files the EB-1B on the strength of the offer and the citation record, and the researcher files an EB-1A, or an EB-2 National Interest Waiver, as a petition that will survive a job change. If the EB-1B is approved first, the researcher proceeds on it; if the researcher later leaves, the self-petition is already in hand. Because the two petitions draw on the same publications, citations and letters, the marginal cost of the second is lower than filing it alone.

## How to decide

Choose the EB-1B alone when the offer is permanent, the institution is a willing and experienced sponsor, you expect to stay through the green card, and your record is outstanding but not yet at the EB-1A level. Choose the EB-1A alone when there is no permanent offer, when you may change employers soon, or when your record is strong enough that the higher standard is not a serious obstacle. Choose both when you can carry the cost and want the EB-1B's higher approval odds with the EB-1A's independence.
