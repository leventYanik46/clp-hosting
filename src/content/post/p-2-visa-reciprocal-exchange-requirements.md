The P-2 visa is the narrowest of the P categories, and the reason is in the name. It exists only for artists and entertainers who come to the United States under a reciprocal exchange program, meaning a U.S. artist of comparable skill goes abroad under the same agreement. If no exchange agreement exists, there is no P-2 case, however talented the performer. That one fact sorts most inquiries before anyone gathers a single exhibit. This post walks through the P-2 visa reciprocal exchange requirements so you can tell quickly whether the category fits or whether you should be looking at P-1, P-3, or O-1B instead.

## What a reciprocal exchange program means for a P-2 visa

The core of every P-2 petition is a formal, written exchange agreement between an organization in the United States and an organization in another country. Under that agreement, artists from each side perform in the other's country on similar terms. The best-known example is the program run by the American Federation of Musicians for musicians moving between the United States and Canada, but orchestras, theater companies, festivals, and arts councils can and do sign their own reciprocal agreements with foreign counterparts.

USCIS looks for similarity rather than symmetry. The regulations ask that the artists exchanged be comparable in caliber, that the terms and conditions of employment (including the length of the engagement) be similar, and that the numbers involved be roughly in balance. They do not require a one-for-one swap on identical dates. A U.S. quartet touring Germany in the spring and a German quartet touring the United States in the fall can satisfy the rule if the agreement ties the two engagements together.

What does not work is an agreement drafted after the fact to dress up an ordinary hire. Adjudicators read the document, compare it with the sponsor's statement, and expect the exchange to be real on both sides. ## Who can be the beneficiary

The P-2 classification covers artists and entertainers, individually or as a group. Musicians and actors are the most common beneficiaries because their unions run established exchange programs, but dancers, conductors, and other performing artists qualify if an exchange agreement covers them.

For a group, each member must be an artist with skills comparable to the U.S. artists going the other way. P-2 does not carry the P-1B rule that most of the group must have performed together for at least a year, which makes it a practical route for ensembles assembled for a specific exchange. Essential support personnel, such as a sound engineer or stage manager whose skills are integral to the performance and cannot readily be performed by a U.S. worker, can be included in a separate P-2S petition with their own consultation and a statement explaining why they are essential.

## The labor organization must be involved

Two separate requirements involve a U.S. labor organization, and they are easy to confuse.

First, like every P petition, a P-2 filing needs a written consultation, often called an advisory opinion, from an appropriate labor organization about the nature of the work and the beneficiary's qualifications. If no appropriate organization exists for the discipline, the petition must say so and USCIS decides the case on the remaining evidence.

Second, and unique to P-2, the petition must show that an appropriate U.S. labor organization was involved in negotiating the reciprocal exchange or has concurred with it. When the union itself is the sponsoring organization, as in the AFM program, this is straightforward. When an orchestra or festival signed its own agreement with a foreign counterpart, you will need a letter from the relevant union confirming it reviewed and agreed to the exchange. Getting that letter can take time, so request it before the petition is drafted, not after.

## Who files, and on what form

The beneficiary cannot file for a P-2 visa. The petitioner must be the U.S. sponsoring organization that is a party to the exchange agreement, a U.S. employer, or a U.S. agent acting for the employers involved. The petition is Form I-129 with the P classification supplement, filed with USCIS before any consular interview. The step-by-step sequence, from filing through visa stamping and admission, is laid out in our post on the [P-2 petition process](/p-2-visa-petition-process-steps).

An agent petition is common when a touring artist will perform for several venues or presenters. The agent must be authorized to act for all of them and must include an itinerary with dates and locations, plus the contracts or a summary of the terms with each.

## Situations the P-2 visa does not cover

Some performers who ask about P-2 belong in a different category. A band with international recognition but no exchange agreement is a P-1B case, and we compare the two routes in [P-2 vs P-1B for touring groups](/p-2-vs-p-1-visa-artists). A soloist with a record of distinction may fit better under the [O-1B extraordinary ability visa](/practice-area/key-services/o-1b-visa), which has no exchange requirement but a much heavier evidentiary burden. A traditional or folk ensemble should look at P-3.

Short appearances with no U.S. payment beyond expenses sometimes fit a visitor visa, but the limits are strict; see what you can and cannot do on a [B-1 or B-2 visa](/b1-b2-visas-in-the-united-states-what-you-can-and-cannot-do) before relying on it. Finally, P-2 is a temporary classification tied to the exchange engagement. You will be admitted for the period needed to complete it, up to one year at a time, and a consular officer can ask about your plans to return home when it ends.
