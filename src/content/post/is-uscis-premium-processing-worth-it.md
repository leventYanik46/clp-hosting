USCIS premium processing is worth paying for when speed changes something real in your case, such as a start date, a travel plan, or a status that is about to expire. It is not worth paying for if you simply want reassurance, because it only shortens the wait for a first decision. It does not make approval more likely.

## What premium processing does

For eligible petitions, an additional fee guarantees that USCIS will take an initial action within a fixed number of business days. The set timeframe depends on the form type and category, and USCIS publishes the current numbers on its premium processing page. The initial action can be an approval, a denial, a Request for Evidence or a Notice of Intent to Deny.

If USCIS misses its window, the agency refunds the premium fee and continues to process the case, but the case itself still has to be decided on the merits.

## What it does not do

- It does not raise your chances of approval.
- It does not prevent an RFE.
- It does not speed up steps outside USCIS, such as the Department of Labor stage, a visa interview at a consulate, or biometrics.
- It does not restart or reset a clock if USCIS issues an RFE. The timeframe begins again once USCIS receives your response.

## H-1B situations where it often helps

- **Change of employer.** Under portability you can usually begin work after USCIS receives the transfer petition, so premium processing is less about the start date and more about certainty. If a decision would affect a green card step or a travel plan, speed has value.
- **Change of status** from F-1, for example, where a future start date or an expiring authorization depends on a timely decision.
- **Extension** when your current approval is about to run out and you want a decision before travel.
- **Cap-subject cases** with an October 1 start date, where the employer wants a decision before the employee's current authorization ends.
- **Cases where an RFE is likely.** Because premium processing produces a first action sooner, an RFE can arrive early, and that leaves more time to respond.

## When you might skip it

- When your work authorization is secure and there is no travel on the horizon
- When your employer wants to control costs and the filing does not affect your plans
- When the petition has known weaknesses that you want fixed before USCIS reviews them. Speed does not substitute for a stronger filing.

## Who pays

Whether the employer or employee pays can depend on the circumstances, and the rules limit what an employer can require an employee to cover. Raise this with your employer's human resources team or attorney early. Some employers pay for premium processing as standard practice, and others offer it on request.

## Questions to ask

1. What changes for me if the decision arrives sooner?
2. Is there a travel date or status expiration the decision has to beat?
3. Will premium processing also affect any related filing, like a green card step?
4. What happens to the fee if USCIS misses the window?

If you cannot answer the first question with something concrete, premium processing may be an expense you can skip. If you can, it usually pays for itself.
