A family-based green card starts with two facts: who is petitioning for you and how you are related to them. Those facts decide whether you can file now or wait years for a visa number to open up.

## How a family-based green card works

A U.S. citizen or permanent resident (the petitioner) files Form I-130, Petition for Alien Relative, with USCIS. Approval of the I-130 proves the relationship. It does not give you a green card by itself. You then apply for permanent residence, either by filing Form I-485 inside the United States or by completing consular processing abroad. The petitioner also signs a financial promise to the government, the Form I-864 affidavit of support.

## Immediate relatives: no waiting line

The fastest family-based green card category is reserved for immediate relatives of U.S. citizens:

- Spouses of U.S. citizens
- Unmarried children under 21 of U.S. citizens
- Parents of U.S. citizens who are at least 21

There is no annual cap on this group, so a visa number is available as soon as the petition is approved. Processing times still vary by USCIS office and consulate, but you are not waiting behind a quota.

## Preference categories: the waiting line

Every other family relationship falls into a preference category with a limited number of visas each year:

- F1: unmarried sons and daughters (21 or older) of U.S. citizens
- F2A: spouses and unmarried children under 21 of permanent residents
- F2B: unmarried sons and daughters (21 or older) of permanent residents
- F3: married sons and daughters of U.S. citizens
- F4: brothers and sisters of U.S. citizens (the citizen must be at least 21)

Look at what is missing from that list. A permanent resident cannot petition for parents, married children, or siblings. Citizenship opens more doors than a green card does, which is one reason many families plan around naturalization.

## Priority dates and the Visa Bulletin

Your priority date is normally the day USCIS receives the I-130. The State Department publishes the Visa Bulletin every month, showing which priority dates are currently being processed in each category. You can move to the next step only when your date is current under the chart USCIS tells applicants to use that month.

Waits differ by category and by country of birth. Some countries, such as Mexico and the Philippines, often face longer lines in certain categories because demand exceeds the per-country limits. A sibling petition (F4) can take far longer than a petition for the spouse of a permanent resident (F2A). Check the bulletin for your own category and country instead of relying on someone else's timeline.

Life events can change the category. If a permanent resident petitioner naturalizes, an F2A child may become an immediate relative and skip the line. If a beneficiary marries, the category usually changes, and the wait can get longer.

## Adjustment of status or consular processing

If you are in the United States, you may be able to file Form I-485 and stay here while the case is pending. Immediate relatives who were inspected and admitted or paroled are in the strongest position, because some status problems are forgiven for them. People in preference categories are held to stricter rules about maintaining lawful status, so a past overstay or unauthorized work can end an adjustment case.

If you are abroad, the case moves to the National Visa Center. You pay fees, submit Form DS-260 and your civil documents, and attend a visa interview at a U.S. consulate. Some people who have stayed past their authorized period face a 3-year or 10-year bar on returning, so speak with an attorney before leaving the country if that describes you.

## The affidavit of support and other requirements

The petitioner must show household income at or above 125 percent of the federal poverty guidelines for their household size, or use assets or a joint sponsor to make up the difference. Applicants also need a medical exam on Form I-693 from a USCIS-designated doctor, certified civil documents, and a record that clears the grounds of inadmissibility, such as certain criminal history or prior immigration fraud.

## Mistakes that slow family cases down

- Filing under the wrong category because the relationship was misunderstood
- Missing documents, such as a translated birth certificate or proof that a prior marriage ended
- Ignoring a change in marital status or age while the case is pending
- Letting the sponsor's income fall short without lining up a joint sponsor
- Failing to update an address with USCIS and missing a notice

A family-based case is easy to describe and easy to damage with small errors. A short review of your facts before you file can save months of delay.
