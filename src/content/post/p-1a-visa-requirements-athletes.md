A foreign athlete with a U.S. contract and a record at the international level can often qualify for a P-1A visa without meeting the much higher O-1A standard. The P-1A visa requirements for athletes come from INA 214(c)(4) and 8 CFR 214.2(p)(4): a tendered contract, plus proof through at least two of seven criteria that you, or your team, are internationally recognized. The petition also needs a labor consultation, and you must keep a residence abroad. Our [P-1 visa for athletes and entertainment groups](/practice-area/key-services/p-1-visa) page describes how we handle these cases; this article explains what the standard asks for.

## What internationally recognized means for a P-1A athlete

The regulations define internationally recognized as a high level of achievement, shown by skill and recognition substantially above that ordinarily encountered, so that the achievement is renowned, leading or well known in more than one country. Two parts of that definition matter. First, the recognition must cross a border: a star of one domestic league who is unknown elsewhere has a harder case. Second, the bar is lower than the O-1A, which requires sustained national or international acclaim and a place among the small percentage at the very top of the field. Many athletes who would struggle with the O-1A qualify comfortably for the P-1A. [P-1 or O-1: which visa fits athletes and performers?](/p-1-vs-o-1-athletes-performers) compares the two in detail.

The classification covers individual athletes and athletic teams. For a team, the recognition belongs to the team as a unit, and each member is covered by the team's standing. The purpose of the trip must be to compete in a specific athletic competition, which can be a single event, a tournament series or a full season.

## The contract requirement

Every P-1A petition must include a tendered contract with a major U.S. sports league or team, or a tendered contract in an individual sport commensurate with international recognition in that sport. Team athletes usually have the first kind. Tennis players, golfers, fighters, runners and other individual competitors usually have the second: an agreement with a promoter, an event organizer or a sponsoring organization at a level that reflects international standing.

You cannot file for yourself. The petitioner must be a U.S. employer, a U.S. sponsoring organization, a U.S. agent, or a foreign employer acting through a U.S. agent. Individual athletes with several events across the season often use an agent with an itinerary.

## The seven P-1A criteria: you need two

Beyond the contract, the petition must document at least two of the following:

- Significant participation in a prior season with a major U.S. sports league.
- Participation in international competition with a national team.
- Significant participation in a prior season for a U.S. college or university in intercollegiate competition.
- A written statement from an official of the governing body of the sport explaining how you or your team are internationally recognized.
- A written statement from a member of the sports media or a recognized expert in the sport explaining that recognition.
- Evidence that you or your team are ranked, if the sport has international rankings.
- Evidence of a significant honor or award in the sport.

Two strong criteria beat four thin ones. National team selection at the senior level, an international ranking with an explanation of how the ranking system works, and a letter from the national federation or international governing body form the core of most petitions. Letters should describe specific results (events, dates, placings) rather than general praise, and the writer's position should be documented. A letter from your own coach is not a governing body statement.

## Athletes covered on other grounds

Congress expanded the P-1A in 2006 to cover certain athletes who might not meet the international recognition standard on their own. The statute now reaches professional athletes employed by teams in qualifying professional associations and their affiliated minor league teams, certain players and coaches on U.S.-based teams in qualifying foreign amateur leagues, and athletes in theatrical ice skating productions. Each category has conditions written into INA 214(c)(4)(A), and USCIS applies them through its policy guidance. If your case rests on one of these categories, the petition should quote the statute and prove each condition.

## The consultation and foreign residence

The P regulations require a written consultation from an appropriate labor organization. For team sports that is usually the players' association for the league; for some individual sports there is no labor organization, and USCIS then decides on the record. The organization may simply state that it has no objection. Request it early and send the contract and event details with the request.

P-1 status also requires a residence abroad that you have no intention of abandoning. The consular officer will ask about it, so be ready to describe your home, family and future plans outside the United States.

## Where P-1A petitions go wrong

Common problems include a contract that is unsigned or still under negotiation, ranking evidence without an explanation of the ranking system, national team evidence only at youth level, support letters from people with no documented standing, and an event description too vague to show a specific competition. Timing matters as well: a mid-season trade or a new contract changes the filing picture, which [how long a P-1 visa lasts and what happens if you are traded](/p-1-visa-validity-athlete-trades) explains. Athletes whose record is truly elite can also look at the [O-1A visa for athletes](/practice-area/key-services/o-1a-visa), which has no foreign residence requirement.
