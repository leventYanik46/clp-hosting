The E-3 visa is reserved for Australian citizens coming to the U.S. to work in a specialty occupation. It covers the same kind of degree-level jobs as the H-1B, but without the lottery. An E-3 visa attorney will tell most clients the same thing: if you are Australian and have a qualifying job offer, the E-3 is usually faster and cheaper than an H-1B.

## Who qualifies

- You are an Australian citizen. Permanent residents of Australia who are not citizens do not qualify.
- You have a U.S. job offer in a specialty occupation, meaning the role requires at least a bachelor's degree or equivalent in a specific field.
- You hold that degree or its equivalent through education and experience.
- Your employer has a certified Labor Condition Application for the role.

Spouses do not need to be Australian. Children of any nationality can accompany as well.

## The annual cap rarely matters

The E-3 has its own annual limit of 10,500 visas for principal applicants. Historically that cap has not been reached, so there is no lottery and you can apply any time of year.

## Step one: the LCA

The employer files a Labor Condition Application with the Department of Labor, attesting it will pay at least the prevailing wage for the role and location. DOL usually certifies an LCA within about a week.

## Step two: the visa or the petition

Most E-3 applicants apply directly at a U.S. consulate. There is no USCIS petition required first. You file Form DS-160 and bring the certified LCA, a job offer letter, and your degree evidence to the interview.

Current State Department policy asks nonimmigrant visa applicants to interview in their country of nationality or residence, so for most applicants that means Sydney, Melbourne, or Perth. Interview waivers have been largely eliminated, so plan for an in-person appointment.

If you are already in the U.S. in another status, the employer can file Form I-129 to change you to E-3 status, though you will still need a visa stamp the next time you travel.

## Length of stay and renewals

E-3 status is granted in up to two-year increments, and there is no limit on the number of renewals as long as you keep a qualifying job. Each renewal needs a new LCA.

The E-3 does not allow immigrant intent in the same way the H-1B does. You must intend to depart when your status ends. That does not ban a green card, but filing one can create problems at the next E-3 visa renewal. Time it carefully.

## Spouse work rights

E-3 spouses are authorized to work incident to status. CBP annotates the spouse's I-94 to show this, and that I-94 can be used for employment. Check the annotation at entry so there is no delay in starting work.

## Changing employers

A new employer needs its own LCA and either a new consular application or an I-129 filed with USCIS. You can generally start with the new employer once the I-129 is filed if you are in valid E-3 status.

## E-3 vs. H-1B

- No lottery for the E-3
- No $100,000 proclamation payment, which applies to H-1B petitions
- Lower government fees in most cases
- Shorter increments but unlimited renewals
- Less flexible for green card planning


## Frequently asked questions

### Can I get an E-3 for a part-time job?

Yes. Part-time specialty occupation roles can qualify if the LCA and offer reflect the hours and wage.

### Can I switch from E-3 to H-1B later?

Yes. Many E-3 holders move to H-1B status before starting a green card case, because the H-1B allows dual intent. That usually requires a lottery selection unless the employer is cap-exempt.

### Do E-3 holders get a grace period?

Like other common work categories, E-3 workers can generally receive a discretionary grace period of up to 60 days after employment ends, within their authorized stay, to find a new job or change status.
