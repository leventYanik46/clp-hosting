When someone is detained by Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE), one of the first questions families ask is whether they can get out on bond. Immigration bond hearings are held in immigration court, separate from the removal case. Whether a person qualifies, and how much bond is set, depends on their history and the law.

## What court has bond hearings

Bond hearings are held before an immigration judge in the Executive Office for Immigration Review (EOIR). They are not criminal court proceedings. The detained person, or their attorney, asks the judge for a bond hearing.

Sometimes ICE sets an initial bond amount. If ICE sets a bond, the detained person can ask the judge to lower it. If ICE denies bond, the person can ask the judge for a hearing.

## Who qualifies for bond

**Not eligible (mandatory detention):**
- People with certain criminal convictions, such as aggravated felonies and some drug offenses
- Many people who were first placed in expedited removal, including those later found to have a credible fear of persecution
- People subject to certain national security grounds

**Recent changes:** A 2025 Board of Immigration Appeals decision treats many people who entered without inspection as ineligible for bond before an immigration judge. Federal courts have been divided over that interpretation, and the law in this area has been changing. Check the current rules in your jurisdiction.

## How bond is set

The judge considers:

1. **Danger to the community:** criminal history, violence, and other factors
2. **Flight risk:** whether the person is likely to appear for hearings

Factors that help:
- Family ties in the U.S., especially U.S. citizen children
- Stable residence and employment
- Long time in the U.S.
- Eligibility for relief from removal
- Community support letters

The minimum bond is $1,500. Amounts vary widely.

## Preparing for a bond hearing

Gather:
- Proof of identity
- Proof of residence and employment
- Letters from family, employers, and community members
- Evidence of eligibility for relief
- Certified criminal records, if any

## Paying the bond

Bond is paid to ICE. The person who pays is the obligor. Bond can be paid in full or through a bond company. If the person attends all hearings and complies with conditions, the bond is returned at the end of the case.

## If bond is denied

You can appeal to the BIA. You may also ask for a new bond hearing if circumstances change materially.

## Habeas corpus

If detention is unlawful or prolonged, a federal habeas corpus petition may be an option.

## Practical example

A man who has lived in the U.S. for 15 years is detained after a traffic stop. He has a U.S. citizen wife, two U.S. citizen children, a steady job, and no criminal record beyond the traffic citation. His attorney requests a bond hearing, files letters from his employer and church, proof of his home and tax filings, and evidence that he may qualify for cancellation of removal. Those facts address both flight risk and danger. Whether a bond hearing is available in the first place may depend on how he entered the U.S. and on the current law where he is detained.

## Conditions of release

Judges can set conditions besides bond, and ICE can add others, such as check-ins or electronic monitoring. Missing a check-in or a hearing can lead to bond forfeiture and new detention. Keep copies of every notice and update your address with both ICE and the court.

## Bond companies vs paying directly

Families can pay the full bond amount directly to ICE, which is returned at the end of the case if all conditions are met. Bond companies post the bond in exchange for a nonrefundable fee, usually a percentage of the bond, and may require collateral. A bond company can help when the family can't raise the full amount, but read the contract carefully, including conditions like monitoring devices and what happens if the person misses a check-in.

## After release

Release on bond doesn't end the removal case. The person must attend every hearing, comply with any reporting requirements, and keep the court and ICE informed of address changes. The bond hearing is often the first step in a longer case, and preparation for relief should start right away.
