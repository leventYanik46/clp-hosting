Yes, you can get an EB-1C green card without ever holding an L-1A visa. The EB-1C has no prior status requirement. What it requires is a qualifying relationship between a U.S. employer and a foreign employer, at least one year of managerial or executive work for the foreign entity within a three-year window, and a managerial or executive job offer in the United States. The L-1A is simply the most common path because its rules are almost the same. Managers who are still abroad, or who are in the United States on an H-1B, E-2 or another work status, can qualify too.

## Filing for an EB-1C from abroad

A manager or executive who still works for the foreign company can be sponsored directly. The U.S. affiliate files the I-140 while the employee stays abroad, and once the priority date is current, the employee completes consular processing: the National Visa Center stage, the DS-260, and the immigrant visa interview at the U.S. consulate in the home country. The employee then enters the United States as a permanent resident.

The three-year window is easiest to meet in this scenario, because the year of managerial work abroad is recent. The harder question is usually the U.S. side. The U.S. company must have been doing business for at least one year, and the offered job must be genuinely managerial or executive in a company that can support it. A U.S. subsidiary with two employees will draw the same scrutiny an L-1A petition would. Our [EB-1C multinational manager green card](/practice-area/key-services/eb-1c-green-card) practice often files from abroad for executives who prefer to wait for the green card rather than relocate twice.

## Filing while on H-1B, E-2 or another status

### H-1B

An H-1B holder working for the U.S. affiliate of a company the employee managed for abroad may qualify. If the employee came to the United States to work for the same company group, USCIS generally looks at the three years before that entry to find the qualifying year abroad. H-1B is dual intent, so filing the I-140 and I-485 does not threaten the status.

### E-2

E-2 managers and investors are common EB-1C candidates, especially founders who own both the foreign company and the U.S. company. Common ownership can create the affiliate relationship the EB-1C needs. Two cautions apply. The E-2 is not a dual intent visa, so filing for a green card can complicate E-2 renewals and travel; plan the timing. And the founder's U.S. role must be managerial or executive in a business that is staffed to support it. Our [E-2 treaty investor visa](/practice-area/key-services/e-2-visa) team coordinates both sides.

### Other statuses

The analysis gets more technical when the employee entered the United States for a reason unrelated to the company group, such as study. In that case, the three-year window may be measured back from the filing date, and the foreign managerial year may have fallen outside it. A short return to a managerial role abroad can sometimes rebuild eligibility, but the dates need careful review before anyone makes plans around them.

## What still has to be true

Whatever the employee's current status, the same core elements apply:

- The U.S. employer has been doing business for at least one year, regularly and continuously.
- The U.S. and foreign entities are related as parent, branch, subsidiary or affiliate, and the relationship still exists when the I-140 is filed.
- The employee worked for the foreign entity for at least one year in a managerial or executive role within the relevant three-year window.
- The U.S. job is managerial or executive, and the employer can pay the offered wage.

None of these depends on L-1A status. An approved L-1A helps only because it shows that USCIS has already accepted similar evidence once, and USCIS can still reach a different conclusion at the green card stage. Our step-by-step guide to the [EB-1C green card process](/eb-1c-green-card-process-steps) covers what happens after the I-140.

## When the L-1A is still the better first step

Some managers should take the L-1A first. If the U.S. company is newer than one year, the EB-1C is not yet available, and a [new office L-1A petition](/new-office-l-1a-petition-requirements) can bring the manager to the U.S. while the business matures. If the employee needs to be in the United States quickly and the Visa Bulletin shows a long wait for the employee's country, an L-1A provides a work status in the meantime. And if the U.S. operation is small, a year or two of L-1A history with real hiring can make the eventual EB-1C petition stronger.
