Since 2008, every R-1 has started the same way: the religious organization files a petition with USCIS, and only after approval can the worker apply for the visa or begin work. There is no consular-only route and no self-petition. The R-1 visa process also has a feature most other work visas lack, which is a physical inspection of the employer's premises that USCIS can conduct before deciding the case. Understanding where that fits in the sequence is the key to planning realistically.

## Step 1: The employer prepares the petition

The petitioner is the U.S. religious organization. It files Form I-129 with the R-1 Classification Supplement, which includes an employer attestation signed by an authorized official. The attestation covers the organization (its tax-exempt status, number of members, number of employees, and the number of R-1 and special immigrant religious worker petitions it has filed in the past five years), the position (title, duties, hours, compensation, and location), and the worker (denominational membership and qualifications). The supporting evidence, from the IRS determination letter to proof of the worker's two years of membership, is set out in [R-1 visa documents the employer must provide](/r-1-visa-documents-employer-checklist).

Before any of that is drafted, the organization should confirm that the position and the worker meet the four core tests, which our [R-1 visa](/practice-area/key-services/r-1-visa) practice does at the first meeting. A petition that fails on eligibility cannot be fixed by better paperwork.

## Step 2: Filing with USCIS and the premium processing question

The petition is filed with the current USCIS filing fee at the address designated for I-129 R petitions. Premium processing for R-1 petitions has historically come with a condition that catches first-time petitioners: USCIS has limited it to employers that have already passed a site inspection. Check the current Form I-907 instructions before counting on it, and if your congregation is filing its first R-1, plan on regular processing. Check the USCIS processing times page for the current range rather than relying on what a neighboring church experienced last year.

The petition can be filed while the worker is abroad or, if the worker is in the United States in another valid status, with a request to change status to R-1. A visitor who came to attend a religious conference and was then offered a position can sometimes change status, but the timing of the offer relative to entry will be scrutinized, and work cannot begin until the change is approved. Our [change and extension of status](/practice-area/key-services/change-and-extension-of-status) page explains the rules for in-country filings.

## Step 3: The site inspection in the R-1 visa process

USCIS may verify any petition through an on-site inspection of the employer's premises, and for R-1 petitions this is common, particularly for organizations that have not petitioned before. An officer visits the location where the worker will be employed, confirms that the organization exists and operates as described, may interview officials, and may ask to see the facilities, records, and evidence of the organization's religious activities. Inspections can happen before adjudication or after approval.

The practical preparation is simple: the address on the petition must be a real place of worship or religious work, someone who knows the petition should be available to answer questions, and the organization's records should match what was filed. An inspection that finds a mailbox, a locked building, or an official who cannot describe the position is a denial. A clean inspection also helps the organization's later filings, including any premium processing request.

## Step 4: Approval, RFE, or denial

USCIS approves the petition for the requested period, up to 30 months initially, or issues a request for evidence or a denial. RFEs on R-1 petitions most often ask for better proof of compensation, of the two-year membership, or of the religious nature of the duties. Respond on every point and by the deadline. A denial can be appealed or the petition refiled, but the worker cannot start in the meantime.

## Step 5: The consular interview or admission

A worker abroad takes the I-797 approval notice, completes the DS-160, pays the visa application fee, and attends an interview at a U.S. consulate. The officer will ask about the denomination, the position, and the worker's background, and may contact the employer. Spouses and unmarried children under 21 apply for R-2 visas at the same time. Canadian citizens are visa-exempt and present the approval notice at the port of entry instead.

A worker already in the United States whose change of status was approved does not need to travel; the approval notice carries a new I-94 with the R-1 period. Either way, the I-94 record governs the authorized stay, and the five-year clock described in [R-1 maximum stay and extensions](/r-1-visa-maximum-stay-extensions) starts running from admission in R-1 status.

## Where the time goes

Three stages drive the calendar: gathering the employer's evidence, the USCIS decision (longer without premium processing and when a site visit is pending), and the consular appointment. None of them can be compressed by filing a thinner petition. Organizations that need a minister in place by a specific date should start the process as early as the position is defined, and should have a plan for coverage if the timeline slips.
