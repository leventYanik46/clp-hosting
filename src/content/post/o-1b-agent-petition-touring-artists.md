An artist with twelve U.S. bookings across six venues cannot reasonably ask every venue to file its own O-1B. The workable answer is an O-1B agent petition: one Form I-129, filed by a U.S. agent, covering every engagement and supported by an itinerary and the terms of each booking. The rules sit in 8 CFR 214.2(o)(2)(iv)(E), and most Requests for Evidence on tour petitions trace back to one of them. The eligibility side of the case, meaning the criteria and the advisory opinion, is covered on our [O-1B artists and entertainers visa](/practice-area/key-services/o-1b-visa) page. This article is about the structure.

## Who can act as an agent for an O-1B petition

The regulation recognizes three kinds of U.S. agent, and the paperwork differs for each.

### The agent as your employer

A management company or booking agency can be your actual employer, paying you and placing you with venues. In that case the agent must provide the contract between the agent and you, stating the wage offered and the other terms and conditions of your employment.

### The agent representing you and several employers

A person or company in business as an agent may file as the representative of both the employers and you. This is the classic tour model. The petition must include a complete itinerary of the events, contracts between the employers and you, and an explanation of the terms and conditions of employment. The burden is on the agent to explain those terms and provide the documents.

### The agent authorized to act for an employer

A person or entity authorized by an employer to act for it, or in its place, may also file. A foreign employer, such as a European orchestra touring the United States, can petition through a U.S. agent this way, and the foreign employer then carries responsibility for complying with U.S. employer sanctions rules.

In each model the agent should be a real business with a U.S. presence, and the petition should prove it with formation records, a business address and a history of representing performers. A company formed for one artist can work, as [Can a startup founder get an O-1 visa?](/can-startup-founder-get-o-1-visa) explains for founders, but it invites closer review.

## What the itinerary must show

For a representing agent, the itinerary must list the dates of each service or engagement, the names and addresses of the actual employers, and the names and addresses of the establishments, venues or locations where you will perform. Cities alone are not enough. The petition should also explain the nature of the events, with beginning and ending dates.

The regulations define an event broadly: it can be a tour, an engagement or a group of related activities, and it may include short vacations, promotional appearances and stopovers that are incidental to the event. That definition lets a tour with rehearsal days, press days and recording sessions be treated as one activity. Real tours have gaps. Explain them (travel, rehearsal, writing time) rather than leaving the officer to guess.

## Contracts, deal memos and summaries of terms

The petition must include copies of written contracts with you or a summary of the terms of an oral agreement. In tour practice that often means a mix: signed performance agreements for anchor dates, deal memos or booking confirmations for others, and the management or agency agreement between you and the agent. Each one should show who engages you, for what dates, and on what pay or fee terms. When the only paper is an email, the agent should prepare a summary of terms that ties the email to the itinerary entry. Gaps between the contracts and the itinerary, such as a venue listed with no supporting confirmation, are the most frequent RFE subject in agent cases.

## Adding dates after approval, and when to amend

You will almost always add dates after the approval notice arrives. The O regulations allow the petitioner to add similar or comparable performances, engagements or appearances during the validity period without filing an amended petition. More festival dates on the same tour fit that rule. A material change in the terms of employment or in your eligibility does not, and requires an amended I-129 with the current USCIS filing fee. Examples include switching to a different kind of work, such as a long teaching residency in place of performing, or replacing the petitioner. Keep an updated itinerary and copies of new contracts with you when you travel, because the consular officer and the CBP officer may ask what you will be doing.

## Common problems with agent petitions

Agent cases tend to fail on structure, not talent. The agent cannot show it is in business as an agent. The itinerary lists cities and months rather than venues and dates. Employer contracts are missing for half the engagements. A foreign employer files through an agent without understanding its own obligations. The advisory opinion describes different work than the itinerary, which is why the itinerary should go to the union with the consultation request, as [who provides the O-1 advisory opinion](/o-1-advisory-opinion-who-provides-it) describes. At the consulate, refusals follow when the artist cannot describe the bookings in their own words.

The evidence of distinction still has to be there, and the [O-1B evidence checklist for musicians and actors](/o-1b-evidence-checklist-musicians-actors) covers it. If you tour as a band that performs as a unit, the [P-1 visa for entertainment groups](/practice-area/key-services/p-1-visa) uses the same agent and itinerary concepts with a group standard, and may be the better fit.
