When USCIS holds a case far longer than normal, you are not stuck waiting indefinitely. A writ of mandamus lawsuit asks a federal court to order the agency to act on your application. It does not tell USCIS what to decide, only that it has to decide. It can be effective, but it is not right for every delayed case, and it has real limits.

## What a mandamus lawsuit is

You file a complaint in a U.S. district court against the agency officials responsible for your case. The claim is usually based on a federal statute that lets a court compel an official to perform a duty owed to you, and on the Administrative Procedure Act, which allows a court to compel agency action that has been unlawfully withheld or unreasonably delayed. Cases are filed in federal court, not in immigration court.

## When it may fit

Typical situations include:

- A green card, naturalization or other application pending far beyond posted processing times
- A case stuck after an interview, with no decision
- Prolonged background or security checks, often called administrative processing
- A refusal to schedule an interview or biometrics
- A consular case delayed after the interview

Courts look at whether the delay is unreasonable under the circumstances. They consider factors such as whether Congress has set a timeline, whether human welfare is at stake, the effect of speeding up your case on others in line, and whether the agency acted in bad faith. Different courts apply the factors differently, and a delay that one judge finds unreasonable another might not.

## What it cannot do

- **It cannot force approval.** The court can order a decision, and the decision can still be a denial.
- **It generally cannot override discretionary decisions** that the law leaves to the agency.
- **It does not work when the delay is within the normal range.** Courts often dismiss cases where the wait is not unusual.
- **It can create friction.** Some applicants worry that suing could harm their case. In practice, filing is often followed by USCIS resolving the application, but there are no guarantees.

## What drives the cost

A mandamus lawsuit typically involves:

- **The court filing fee,** which is set by the federal courts
- **Attorney fees,** which vary with the complexity of the case and the amount of litigation
- **Service of process** on the government parties
- **Further costs** if the government files a motion to dismiss or the case goes beyond the initial stage

Many cases settle early when USCIS adjudicates the application. Ask any attorney for a written fee agreement that explains what is covered, including what happens if the case resolves quickly or continues.

## Alternatives to try first

1. **A case inquiry** with USCIS, after the posted processing time has passed
2. **An expedite request,** if you have urgent circumstances such as a medical need or financial loss
3. **A request for help from the CIS Ombudsman**
4. **A congressional inquiry** through your representative's office
5. **Checking for errors,** for example a missed notice or an incorrect address

Some of these cost nothing, and some resolve cases without a lawsuit.

## Deciding whether to sue

A lawyer can look at how long the delay has lasted compared with typical times, the reasons for delay and the impact on you, then advise whether the lawsuit is likely to help. Gather your receipt notices, any correspondence and a timeline of your case before the meeting.
