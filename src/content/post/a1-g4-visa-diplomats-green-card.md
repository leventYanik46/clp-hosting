A and G visas cover people who come to the U.S. to work for foreign governments or international organizations. In the Washington, DC area that means embassy staff, World Bank and IMF employees, and staff of many other organizations. The visas themselves are straightforward to obtain, but questions about family employment, leaving the job, and getting a green card are not.

## What is an A-1 visa?

A-1 status is for ambassadors, diplomats, consular officers, and their immediate family members, when traveling on official business of their government. **A-2** covers other foreign government officials and employees on official duties, plus family members. **A-3** covers personal employees and household staff of A-1 and A-2 holders.

A visas are issued based on a request from the foreign government, not through a USCIS petition. Status lasts as long as the person is recognized by the State Department in that role.

## What is a G-4 visa?

G-4 status is for officers and employees of designated international organizations, such as the World Bank, IMF, United Nations, Organization of American States, and Inter-American Development Bank, and for their immediate family. G-1 through G-3 cover representatives of member governments to those organizations. G-5 covers their household staff.

## Can family members work?

Dependents in A and G status can work in the U.S. only with permission. Spouses and certain children may apply for employment authorization through the State Department's Office of Foreign Missions or their organization, using Form I-566, depending on bilateral agreements or arrangements with the dependent's home country. Some countries have reciprocal work agreements with the U.S.; others do not.

Without authorization, a dependent should not work, even for a U.S. company that seems willing to hire.

## What happens when the job ends?

A and G status ends when the official role ends. Leaving a diplomatic post or international organization job usually requires prompt departure, a change of status to another category, or another basis to remain. Planning before the end date matters.

## Paths to a green card for A and G holders

### Standard routes

A and G holders can pursue family-based or employment-based green cards like anyone else. Adjustment of status inside the U.S. requires additional forms:

- **Form I-508**, waiving the rights, privileges, exemptions, and immunities the person holds in A or G status.
- **Form I-566**, certifying the person's A or G status with the State Department.

Diplomatic immunity must be waived before permanent residence can be granted.

### The G-4 special immigrant category

Long-serving G-4 employees and their families may qualify for a green card as **special immigrants** in the EB-4 category. Eligible groups include:

- Retired officers or employees of an international organization who resided and were physically present in the U.S. for a long period, generally at least 15 years in G-4 or related status, before retirement, and who apply within a set period after retirement.
- Their surviving spouses, under similar conditions.
- Unmarried sons and daughters who lived in the U.S. in G-4 status for a required period during childhood.

Timing rules in this category are strict. A retiree who misses the filing window may lose eligibility.

## Taxes and benefits

Many A and G holders are exempt from certain U.S. taxes on official income. Becoming a permanent resident changes tax status. Talk to a tax adviser before adjusting status.

## Planning ahead

A and G families should plan before a job ends or a child ages out. Moving from G-4 to H-1B, from A-1 to another status, or toward a G-4 special immigrant green card all depend on precise timing.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a G-4 holder change to H-1B?
Yes. A U.S. employer can file a change of status petition, subject to the H-1B lottery unless the employer is cap-exempt.

### Do G-4 dependents lose status at 21?
Generally yes, unless they qualify for another status. Children often change to F-1 student status before then.

### Is a G-4 holder subject to the two-year home residency rule?
No. That rule applies to certain J-1 exchange visitors, not to G-4 holders.

### Can an A-1 diplomat apply for asylum?
Yes, a person in A status can apply, but doing so has serious diplomatic and practical consequences that need careful advice.

### Do A and G holders need to renew visas?
Status lasts while the official role continues, but visa stamps for travel may need renewal through the State Department's processes.

### Can a G-4 spouse start a business?
Self-employment generally requires employment authorization. Check whether the bilateral arrangement allows it.

### What is Form I-508 for?
It waives the rights, privileges, exemptions, and immunities of A and G status. It is required before adjusting to permanent residence.
