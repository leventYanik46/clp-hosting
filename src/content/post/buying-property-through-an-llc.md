Investors ask this before almost every rental purchase, and the honest answer depends on how you will finance the deal and what you are trying to protect. Buying property through an LLC puts a legal wall between the property's liabilities (a tenant's injury claim, a contractor dispute, a code violation) and your personal assets. It also costs you access to conventional residential mortgages, adds filing obligations, and in some jurisdictions triggers transfer taxes if you move the property into the entity after you have already bought it. The order of operations matters as much as the decision.

## What the LLC protects, and what it does not

A properly maintained LLC limits a claimant to the assets inside the entity. If a tenant is injured on the stairs and wins a judgment larger than the insurance, the judgment reaches the building and its rent, not your home or savings. That protection holds only if you treat the LLC as separate: its own bank account, leases signed in its name, no personal expenses run through it, and insurance adequate to the risk. Courts set aside the shield when the entity is a shell with the owner's personal finances flowing through it.

The shield also has gaps. If you personally guarantee the loan, you are liable on the loan regardless of the LLC. If you personally caused the harm, say by doing the electrical work yourself, you are liable for your own negligence. Liability insurance, including an umbrella policy, remains the first line of defense, and the LLC is the second.

Our [residential and commercial transaction counsel](/practice-area/key-services/guidance-in-commercial-and-residential-real-estate-transactions) typically raises the entity question at the offer stage, because the buyer named in the contract should be the party that will take title, and switching later can require the seller's consent and the lender's re-underwriting.

## Financing is the biggest practical tradeoff

Conventional residential mortgages sold to Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac are made to individuals, not entities. An LLC buyer generally uses a commercial, portfolio, or debt-service-coverage loan, which tends to carry a higher rate, a shorter term, and a personal guaranty from the members. For a single condo or a small rental, that difference can outweigh the liability benefit.

The common workaround, buying in your own name with a conventional loan and then deeding the property to an LLC, carries its own risk. Nearly every residential mortgage has a due-on-sale clause allowing the lender to call the loan on a transfer. Federal law protects certain transfers, such as a borrower moving an owner-occupied home into a revocable trust of which the borrower is beneficiary, but a transfer to an LLC is not on that protected list. Some lenders consent in writing; ask before you record anything. Also check the owner's title policy: newer policies generally continue coverage when you deed the property to an entity you wholly own for no consideration, but older policies may not, and a lapse in coverage is easy to miss.

## Taxes, transfer costs, and lost exemptions

For federal income tax, a single-member LLC is disregarded, so rental income and expenses land on your own return exactly as they would without the entity. A multi-member LLC files a partnership return and issues each member a Schedule K-1. The LLC itself does not change the tax result; it changes who holds title.

Transfer costs are a different story. The District and New York impose transfer and recordation taxes on conveyances, and moving a property you already own into an LLC is a conveyance. Each jurisdiction has limited exemptions for transfers that merely change the form of ownership, but whether yours qualifies depends on the facts, so get that answered before you sign a deed. Owner-occupant benefits are also at stake: a homestead deduction or a primary-residence property tax program generally requires an individual owner living in the property, and an LLC-owned home can complicate the capital gains exclusion for a principal residence. For those reasons the LLC is a tool for investment property, not the house you live in.

## Formation and ongoing compliance

Form the LLC in the state where the property sits unless you have a reason not to; an out-of-state LLC must register as a foreign entity where it owns real estate, which doubles the annual filings. New York requires a newly formed LLC to publish notice of its formation, and the cost of that publication depends on the county. Every LLC needs an operating agreement, a registered agent, and annual reports or fees, and beneficial ownership reporting rules have changed more than once, so confirm the current requirement at formation. ## When the LLC makes sense

The entity earns its keep for multi-unit and commercial buildings, properties with employees or regular contractors, and any purchase with more than one investor. In the multi-investor case the operating agreement does the work a deed cannot: it sets capital contributions, profit splits, who decides on a sale, and how a member exits. Our comparison of [joint tenancy and tenancy in common](/joint-tenancy-vs-tenancy-in-common) explains what co-owners get without an entity, and why that is often not enough. Foreign investors have additional reasons to consider an entity, including privacy and estate planning, which we discuss in our guide to [buying a home without U.S. citizenship](/purchasing-a-home-without-citizenship).

The LLC makes less sense for a single rental bought with a conventional mortgage by an owner who carries solid liability and umbrella coverage. There the financing penalty is real and the added protection is marginal. Investors with several properties often use one LLC per property, or a series structure where state law allows it, so that a claim against one building cannot reach the others; that decision depends on the lender and on how much administration you are willing to carry.
