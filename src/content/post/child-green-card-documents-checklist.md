A child green card petition fails on paperwork far more often than on law. USCIS approves Form I-130 on two questions: are you a U.S. citizen or permanent resident, and does the child meet the Immigration and Nationality Act's definition of your child. Every document in the packet answers one of those questions, and the second stage (Form I-485 or the consular DS-260) adds a predictable list of its own. This checklist covers the child green card documents for each type of relationship, what to do when a record is missing, and what tends to draw a Request for Evidence. CLP's [child green card](/practice-area/key-services/child-green-card) team reviews these packets before filing; this is what we look for.

## Proof of your own status

Send a copy, never an original, of one of these: your U.S. passport biographic page, your naturalization or citizenship certificate, your U.S. birth certificate, or a Consular Report of Birth Abroad. Permanent residents send the front and back of the green card. If your name has changed since the document was issued, add the marriage certificate or court order that explains the change. Mismatched names across documents are the easiest avoidable delay in these cases.

## The relationship documents, by type of child

The core proof is the child's birth certificate, but what else you need depends on how the child is yours under section 101(b)(1) of the INA:

- Child born in wedlock: the child's birth certificate naming both parents, plus your marriage certificate to the other parent.
- Child born out of wedlock, mother petitioning: the birth certificate naming you is enough.
- Child born out of wedlock, father petitioning: the birth certificate, plus either proof of legitimation before the child turned 18 (a court order, a later marriage of the parents, or legitimation by operation of the local law) or proof of a bona fide parent-child relationship before the child turned 21, such as financial support records, school and medical records naming you, correspondence, photographs over time, and affidavits.
- Stepchild: your marriage certificate to the child's parent showing a wedding date before the child turned 18, proof that all prior marriages ended, and the child's birth certificate naming your spouse. See our post on [sponsoring a stepchild for a green card](/stepchild-green-card-requirements).
- Adopted child: the final adoption decree showing the adoption before age 16, proof of two years of legal custody (the decree or a custody order), and proof of two years living together (school records, leases, medical records). Children adopted from a Hague Convention country generally immigrate on Forms I-800A and I-800 instead of an I-130, and the document list is different.

## When the birth certificate is missing or unreliable

Many countries register births late or not at all, and USCIS knows which ones. The State Department's Reciprocity Schedule lists, country by country, which civil documents exist and what they look like. If the schedule says birth certificates are unavailable for the relevant place and period, you submit a statement of non-availability from the civil registry together with secondary evidence: a baptismal or religious record made near the time of birth, early school records, medical records, or a census entry. If no records exist, two affidavits from people with personal knowledge of the birth, explaining how they know, can fill the gap.

A birth certificate registered years after the birth is treated with suspicion even when genuine, so pair it with secondary evidence created at the time. When documents cannot settle a biological relationship, USCIS or the consulate may suggest DNA testing. It is voluntary, it must be done through an accredited laboratory with samples collected under the agency's procedures, and a result that matches usually ends the dispute.

## Translations and format

Every foreign-language document needs a full English translation and a signed statement from the translator certifying that the translation is complete and accurate and that the translator is competent to translate. Notarization is not required. Submit clear photocopies; USCIS may ask for originals at an interview, and the consulate will want to see them. Keep names, dates and places consistent across the whole set, and explain any variation in a short cover note rather than hoping nobody notices.

## The second stage: the I-485 or DS-260 packet

Once the I-130 is approved (or filed concurrently for an immediate relative child in the United States), the second filing needs:

- Form I-864 from you with your most recent federal tax return or IRS transcript, W-2s or 1099s, and proof of current income at or above 125 percent of the Federal Poverty Guidelines for your household size. Add a joint sponsor's I-864 and status proof if your income falls short.
- Form I-864W instead of the I-864 if the child is under 18 and will become a citizen automatically on admission as a permanent resident in your legal and physical custody under section 320 of the INA.
- The medical examination: Form I-693 from a USCIS civil surgeon for adjustment, or a panel physician's exam for a consular case, with the vaccination record.
- The child's valid passport and passport-style photographs.
- For adjustment: copies of the child's I-94 record, visa and entry stamp, and the I-130 approval or receipt notice. Our guide to [Form I-485, step by step](/what-is-form-i-485-how-to-complete-step-by-step) covers the full packet.
- For consular processing: the DS-260 confirmation page, police certificates for applicants 16 and older from each country where they lived long enough to need one, and the civil documents again, uploaded to the National Visa Center. Our [family reunification](/practice-area/key-services/family-reunification) page explains the NVC stage.

## What triggers a Request for Evidence

The same handful of gaps account for most RFEs in child cases: a father's petition with no legitimation or relationship evidence, a late-registered birth certificate standing alone, a stepchild petition missing a prior divorce decree, an adoption decree with no custody or residence proof, and an I-864 filed without the tax return. Each one adds months while USCIS waits for your response, and processing times already vary by office, so check the current USCIS processing times and build in margin.
