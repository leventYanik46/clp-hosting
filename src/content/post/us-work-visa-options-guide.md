People who contact a work visa lawyer usually ask the same first question: which visa should I apply for? The answer depends less on the job title and more on your situation. Are you being hired by a U.S. company, transferred by your current employer, investing your own money, or recognized as a leader in your field? Each situation points to a different set of options.

## If a U.S. employer wants to hire you

**H-1B.** The default work visa for professional jobs that require a bachelor's degree in a specific field. Most employers must register in the annual lottery in March. Cap-exempt employers, such as universities and affiliated research organizations, can file year-round.

**TN (Canadian and Mexican citizens).** Created under NAFTA and continued under the USMCA, the TN covers a defined list of professions, such as engineers, accountants, and scientists. No lottery, renewable indefinitely in three-year periods, but it doesn't allow immigrant intent.

**E-3 (Australian citizens).** Similar to the H-1B in requirements, with its own annual quota that is rarely exhausted and renewable two-year periods.

**H-1B1 (Chilean and Singaporean citizens).** A small, separate quota modeled on the H-1B.

**H-2A and H-2B.** For temporary or seasonal agricultural and non-agricultural work.

## If your current employer is transferring you

**L-1A** for managers and executives and **L-1B** for employees with specialized knowledge. You must have worked for the related foreign company for at least one continuous year in the past three years. No lottery, and the L-1A offers a relatively direct path to a green card through the EB-1C category.

## If you're investing your own money

**E-2 treaty investor** for nationals of treaty countries who invest a substantial amount in a U.S. business they will develop and direct. Renewable as long as the business operates.

**E-1 treaty trader** for businesses engaged in substantial trade between the U.S. and the treaty country.

**EB-5** isn't a work visa but a green card through investment, and it is open to all nationalities.

## If you have an exceptional record

**O-1A** for extraordinary ability in sciences, business, education, or athletics, and **O-1B** for the arts and entertainment. No lottery, and founders can use their own company or an agent as the petitioner.

**P-1** for internationally recognized athletes and entertainment groups.

## If you're coming for training or exchange

**H-3** for structured training programs not available in your home country. **J-1** for exchange visitors, including interns, researchers, and physicians. **Q-1** for international cultural exchange programs. Each has limits on productive work.

## If you're a student or recent graduate

**F-1 OPT** allows up to 12 months of work after graduation, with a 24-month STEM extension for qualifying degrees. Many graduates use OPT while they wait for H-1B selection.

## Questions to help narrow your options

1. **What is your nationality?** It determines eligibility for TN, E-3, H-1B1, and E visas.
2. **Do you have a U.S. employer, or will you work for yourself?** Self-employment narrows the list to E-2, O-1, and a few others.
3. **Have you worked abroad for a related company for a year?** If so, the L-1 may be available.
4. **How strong is your record?** Awards, publications, press, and high salary point toward the O-1.
5. **Do you want a green card eventually?** Some visas, like the H-1B and L-1, allow dual intent. Others, like the TN and E-3, require you to intend to leave, which complicates green card planning.
6. **How quickly do you need to start?** Lottery-based visas take months. Others can be approved within weeks.

## Why the first choice matters

The visa you start on shapes your long-term options. A TN professional who later wants a green card has to plan carefully around immigrant intent. An H-1B worker builds toward a six-year limit. An E-2 investor needs a separate strategy for permanent residence. Picking a category with your five-year plan in mind can save years of rework.

## Getting it right

There is often more than one workable answer. The best choice balances speed, cost, your evidence, and your goals for permanent residence.

## How green card plans change the choice

If you expect to apply for a green card within a few years, put that at the center of your visa choice. H-1B and L-1 allow dual intent, so a pending green card case doesn't undermine the temporary visa. The O-1 is treated similarly in practice. TN, E-3, H-1B1, and most visitor and student categories require you to intend to leave at the end of your stay, and a green card filing can create problems at your next visa interview or border crossing. E-2 investors fall somewhere in between: they must state an intent to depart when status ends, but a later green card filing is common and workable with planning.

## Cost and timing at a glance

Visas that require a USCIS petition (H-1B, L-1, O-1, E-3 through USCIS, H-3, R-1, Q-1) cost more upfront because of filing fees and preparation time, but premium processing can bring a decision within weeks. Treaty and consular-only routes (E-2 at many consulates, TN for Canadians at the border) can be faster and cheaper, but you depend on a single interview or inspection for the outcome. Neither model is better in the abstract. What matters is which one matches your start date and your tolerance for risk.
