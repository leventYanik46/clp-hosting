Humanitarian parole lets the Department of Homeland Security allow a person into the United States temporarily, without a visa, for an urgent humanitarian reason or a significant public benefit. That is the entire legal basis, one sentence in section 212(d)(5) of the Immigration and Nationality Act, and everything about parole follows from it. Parole is not a visa, not an immigration status, and not a path to a green card on its own. For families it is a bridge: a way to bring a relative here for a medical emergency, a funeral, or a crisis while the immigrant visa case runs its course. CLP's [family reunification](/practice-area/key-services/family-reunification) practice files parole requests when they fit and tells families plainly when they do not.

## What counts as an urgent humanitarian reason

USCIS decides each request on its own facts and grants parole sparingly. The reasons that succeed share a pattern: something time-sensitive is happening to a specific person, and no visa can get them here in time or at all. Examples include a relative who needs medical treatment that is unavailable in their country, a family member who must care for a seriously ill or dying relative in the United States, attendance at a funeral or at a family member's final days, and protection of a vulnerable person facing a specific and documented danger. Significant public benefit covers situations such as a witness needed in a legal proceeding.

What does not qualify, in the government's view, is the ordinary pain of a long visa wait. A pending Form I-130 and a priority date years away is not, by itself, an urgent humanitarian reason, and USCIS says expressly that parole should not be used to bypass normal visa processing. A parole request that reads like an immigrant visa application in a hurry will be denied. One that documents an emergency, explains why a visitor visa was refused or is unrealistic, and asks for a defined period has a chance.

## How to request humanitarian parole: Forms I-131 and I-134

The request is made on Form I-131, Application for Travel Document, one for each person seeking parole. The applicant can be the beneficiary abroad or a sponsor in the United States filing on their behalf. Each request is accompanied by Form I-134, Declaration of Financial Support, from a sponsor who shows the income or assets to support the beneficiary so that they will not need public benefits, together with the sponsor's proof of status and income.

The evidence carries the case: medical records and a physician's letter describing the treatment and why it is unavailable abroad, a death certificate or hospice letter, proof of the relationship, copies of any visa refusal, a detailed statement explaining the emergency and the length of stay needed, and a copy of the beneficiary's passport. Pay the current USCIS filing fee or request a fee waiver on Form I-912. For life-threatening situations, ask for expedited handling in a cover letter and explain the timeline.

If USCIS approves, it notifies the U.S. embassy or consulate, which issues a travel document (a boarding foil) so the beneficiary can board a flight. The final parole decision is made by Customs and Border Protection at the port of entry, and the period of parole is set to match the purpose, typically no longer than a year.

## What parole gives you, and what it does not

- Parole is a lawful entry. Someone who was paroled has been inspected and paroled for purposes of adjustment of status, which can matter later for an immediate relative of a U.S. citizen who becomes eligible to file Form I-485; our guide to [Form I-485, step by step](/what-is-form-i-485-how-to-complete-step-by-step) explains that eligibility.
- Parole is not a status. A parolee cannot change to a nonimmigrant status from inside the United States on Form I-539, as our [change and extension of status](/practice-area/key-services/change-and-extension-of-status) page explains, and parole alone does not lead to a green card.
- Work authorization is not automatic. A parolee may apply for an Employment Authorization Document on Form I-765 under the parole category.
- Parole ends on the date set by CBP, or earlier if the parolee departs. Staying past the end date starts the clock on unlawful presence. A parolee who needs more time applies for re-parole on a new Form I-131 before the period expires.

## The family reunification parole programs

Separate from individual requests, the government has at times run categorical parole processes for beneficiaries of approved I-130 petitions, letting them wait for their priority dates inside the United States. The Cuban and Haitian Family Reunification Parole programs have existed for years, and in 2023 invitation-based processes opened for nationals of Colombia, El Salvador, Guatemala, Honduras and Ecuador. In 2025, several of these processes were paused or ended. As of this writing, availability changes quickly, so confirm the current status of any program on the USCIS site before relying on it, and keep the immigrant visa case moving in parallel; our post on [consular processing step by step](/consular-processing-immigrant-visa-steps) covers that case. A different program, parole in place, allows certain relatives of U.S. military members already in the United States to be paroled without leaving; it has its own rules.

## Alternatives when parole does not fit

A B-2 visitor visa remains the standard route for a short emergency visit, even for someone with a pending immigrant petition, as long as the petition is disclosed and the applicant can show they will return. For a relative whose immigrant visa case is already at the National Visa Center, a documented medical emergency or a child about to age out can support an expedite request to the NVC or the consulate, which is often faster than a parole request. A U.S. citizen living abroad in exceptional circumstances can ask the consulate to accept the I-130 directly. For an engaged couple, the [K-1 fiancé visa](/practice-area/key-services/k-1-fiance-visa) is a defined route with a 90-day marriage window rather than a discretionary one. For the overall map of family routes, see our post on [understanding family-based immigration](/understanding-family-based-immigration-attorney).
