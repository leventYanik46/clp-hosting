A concurrent H-1B lets you work for two or more employers at the same time, each under its own H-1B petition. Many H-1B workers use it to teach part time at a university, consult for a second company, or take a clinical role alongside a research position. It is fully legal, but each employer has to file separately, and the rules change if your first job is cap-exempt.

## How a concurrent H-1B works

The second employer files its own Form I-129 with its own Labor Condition Application, requesting concurrent employment. Your first H-1B stays in place. You can usually start working for the second employer once its petition is filed, under H-1B portability, as long as you are maintaining status.

Each employer must:

- Pay at least the required wage for its own role and hours
- Keep its own public access file
- Meet its own LCA obligations for the worksite

Part-time concurrent roles are allowed. The LCA and petition simply list part-time hours.

## Is the second petition subject to the cap?

Usually not. If you have already been counted against the H-1B cap, through a past lottery selection, a second employer can file a concurrent petition at any time of year without entering the lottery.

## The cap-exempt trap

The picture changes if your only H-1B so far has been with a cap-exempt employer, such as a university, an affiliated nonprofit, or a nonprofit or government research organization. Cap-exempt H-1Bs do not count against the cap.

In that case, a concurrent petition from a cap-subject employer can be approved without a lottery, but only because you are still working for the cap-exempt employer. Under the 2025 H-1B modernization rule and earlier practice:

- If you stop working for the cap-exempt employer, the cap-subject employment is no longer exempt
- The cap-subject employer would then need a lottery selection for you to continue

This is the source of the "concurrent h1b cap exempt hours" question. There is no fixed minimum number of hours at the cap-exempt job, but the employment must be real and ongoing. A token arrangement designed only to avoid the lottery invites scrutiny.

## Practical considerations

**Total hours.** There is no legal cap on combined hours, but USCIS may question whether you can realistically perform both jobs as described.

**Benefits and wages.** Each employer must pay you for the hours on its LCA, including benefits on the same basis as U.S. workers in similar roles.

**Travel.** If you travel abroad, you need a valid H-1B visa stamp. One stamp from either employer is generally enough to return, as long as you carry approval notices for both.

**Green card sponsorship.** Either employer can sponsor you. Coordinate so the job described in PERM matches the role you will hold.

## Fees and the $100,000 payment

The second employer pays its own filing fees. Under USCIS guidance, the $100,000 proclamation payment generally does not apply to a concurrent petition for a worker inside the U.S. whose request is granted.

## When concurrent H-1B makes sense

- Academics teaching at more than one institution
- Physicians splitting time between a hospital and a private practice
- Engineers consulting for a startup alongside a full-time role, with both employers' consent


## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need permission from my first employer?

Immigration law does not require it, but your employment contract might restrict outside work. Check your agreement and avoid conflicts of interest.

### Can a concurrent H-1B be for self-employment?

Under the 2025 modernization rule, a beneficiary with a controlling interest in the petitioning company can qualify for H-1B status, with shorter validity periods. This takes careful structuring.

### Does a concurrent petition affect my green card?

Not directly. But the employer sponsoring your green card should know about the second role so the PERM and I-140 describe your work accurately.

### Can I keep the concurrent job if I lose my main job?

The concurrent petition stays valid on its own if it was not tied to cap exemption from the first employer. If your status relied on the cap-exempt job, losing that job can affect the second.
