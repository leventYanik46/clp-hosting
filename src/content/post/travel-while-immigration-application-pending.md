The answer depends entirely on which form is pending. Leave the country with a pending Form I-539 and USCIS treats the application as abandoned. Leave with a pending Form N-400 and nothing happens to the application, as long as you come back for the interview. Leave with a pending Form I-485 and no advance parole, and the green card case is over. People travel while an immigration application is pending every day without harm, and people lose years of work by boarding the wrong flight. It is the question our [citizenship, status and travel](/practice-area/key-services/citizenship-status-and-travel) team hears most often before a holiday, so this post goes form by form so you can check before you book.

## Pending Form I-539: departure abandons the request

Form I-539 is the application to extend or change nonimmigrant status for visitors, students, exchange visitors and dependents. USCIS treats a departure from the United States while it is pending as abandonment of the request, for extensions and changes alike. The practical trap is the I-94 date. If your authorized stay has already passed while the application was pending, you were protected only by the pending filing; once you leave, that protection ends and you will need a new visa, in the new category, to return. Dependents who filed their own I-539 lose their application if they travel, even if the principal stays. Our [change and extension of status](/practice-area/key-services/change-and-extension-of-status) page explains the filing rules, and this is the application where the no-travel rule is strictest.

## Pending Form I-129: the petition survives, the status change does not

When an employer files Form I-129 asking USCIS to change your status to H-1B, L-1, O-1 or another work category, the petition and the change of status request are two separate pieces. If you leave while it is pending, USCIS can still approve the petition, but it will approve it for consular notification rather than granting the status change, and you will apply for the visa at a consulate and enter in the new category. Workers whose employer filed a same-employer extension are in a different position: travel on a valid visa with the receipt notice and prior approval is generally possible, but ask before booking, because the answer changes with the category and the timing. Sequencing these filings around trips is part of what we do for [status and travel](/practice-area/key-services/citizenship-status-and-travel) clients.

## Pending Form I-485: advance parole or valid H or L status

An adjustment of status application is abandoned by any departure unless you hold an approved advance parole document or are maintaining valid H-1B, L-1, or dependent status and returning to resume it. Advance parole is requested on Form I-131, often together with the I-485, and USCIS frequently issues it on a combined card with employment authorization. Even with the document, travel is risky for applicants with past unlawful presence, a prior removal order, or a criminal record, and CBP inspects you on return. Our post on [advance parole for pending green card applicants](/advance-parole-pending-green-card) covers the protections and the limits, and our guide to [Form I-485](/what-is-form-i-485-how-to-complete-step-by-step) explains where the I-131 fits in the filing.

### Pending Form I-131: it depends which document

The I-131 covers three travel documents and the travel rule differs for each. An initial advance parole request is abandoned if you leave before it is approved, so wait for the card. A re-entry permit application works the other way: you must file it from inside the United States and attend biometrics here, but once the fingerprints are taken you may leave and have the permit delivered to a U.S. address or a consulate abroad. A refugee travel document should be in hand before departure. Our post on [getting a re-entry permit before a long trip](/re-entry-permit-before-long-trip) describes the sequence, and the [travel and re-entry permits](/practice-area/key-services/travel-and-reentry-permits) page covers all three documents.

## Pending Form N-400: travel is allowed, with three conditions

A permanent resident with a pending naturalization application may travel. The conditions are that you keep each trip short enough to protect continuous residence, that you disclose every absence at the interview, and that you are physically in the United States for biometrics, the interview and the oath ceremony. USCIS schedules these by mail and online notice, so keep your address current and avoid long trips while a notice could arrive. A trip of six months or more during the pending period invites a continuous residence denial, no matter how strong the application was when filed.

## Pending asylum or removal proceedings

An asylum applicant who leaves without advance parole is presumed to have abandoned the application, and a trip to the home country undermines the claim itself. Anyone in removal proceedings should treat departure as a last resort: leaving executes an outstanding removal order, counts as withdrawal of a pending appeal, and can leave you unable to return for years. These cases need a review before any booking.

## Before you travel with a pending application: a checklist

Identify every receipt notice you hold. For each one, ask whether the form is on the no-travel list (I-539, initial I-131 advance parole, I-485 without parole, pending asylum), the ask-first list (I-129), or the travel-with-care list (N-400, re-entry permit after biometrics). Then check your I-94 date, your visa validity for the return trip, and whether any past unlawful presence or arrest makes a departure dangerous regardless of the pending form.
