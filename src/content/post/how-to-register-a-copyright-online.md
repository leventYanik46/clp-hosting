A copyright registration has three parts: a completed application, a deposit copy of the work, and the filing fee. The effective date of registration is the day the Copyright Office receives all three in acceptable form, not the day the certificate arrives, so a complete submission made today protects you from today even though examination takes longer. Most of the mistakes we see come from the choices inside the application rather than from the upload itself. This post walks through how to register a copyright online in the order the system presents it, and flags the fields that cause refusals or weak certificates. For help with the filing itself, our [copyright registration service](/practice-area/key-services/copyright-registration) handles the application end to end.

## Step one: pick the right online copyright application

The Copyright Office's online system, known as eCO, offers several application paths, and choosing the wrong one wastes the fee.

The Standard Application works for any single work and is the default. The Single Application is a lower-cost option limited to one work by one author who is also the sole owner, where the work is not a work made for hire and does not contain material by anyone else. A company registering code written by employees cannot use it, because the company is the author through the work-for-hire doctrine, and the Office treats that as outside the Single Application's limits.

Group applications cover many works in one filing. As of this writing, options include a group of unpublished works (up to ten), a group of published or unpublished photographs (up to 750), and a group of short online literary works such as blog posts, each within a word range and all published within a three-month period. These are the efficient route for photographers, writers, and publishers who need to keep the three-month post-publication window covered without filing weekly.

## Step two: describe the work accurately

The form asks for the type of work (literary, visual arts, performing arts, sound recording, motion picture), the title, and the year of completion. The year of completion is the year the version you are depositing was finished, not the year the project started.

Publication status is next, and it is the field most often answered wrong. A work is published when copies are distributed to the public by sale, rental, lease, lending, or an offer to distribute to a group for further distribution. Posting on your own website is a gray area the Office has not fully resolved; many applicants treat online posting as publication, and the Office accepts the applicant's reasonable determination. The answer matters because published works have different deposit rules and because the three-month window for statutory damages runs from first publication. If published, you supply the date and the nation of first publication.

## Step three: identify the author and the claimant

The author is the person who created the work, or the employer if it is a work made for hire. Answer the work-for-hire question with care. Saying yes for a contractor's work when no qualifying written agreement exists creates a certificate with a false statement, which a defendant will use against you. The distinction is explained in our post on [work made for hire versus assignment](/work-for-hire-vs-copyright-assignment).

The claimant is the owner at the time of filing. If the claimant is not the author, the form requires a transfer statement, usually "by written agreement." Have the signed assignment in your files before you say so. Anonymous and pseudonymous authorship can be indicated if the author prefers not to appear in the public record.

## Step four: limit the claim where needed

If the work contains material that is not yours or is already registered, the limitation of claim section is where you say so. You exclude the preexisting material (public domain content, licensed stock images, open-source code, a previously published or registered version) and describe the new material you are claiming (new text, revised code, added artwork). Skipping this section for a revised work can lead to a refusal or to a certificate that overclaims. A registration for version 2.0 of a program covers only what changed since version 1.0 if 1.0 was registered separately.

## Step five: upload the deposit

For unpublished works and works published only in electronic form, the deposit is uploaded as a file in an accepted format. For works published in physical form, the Office may require physical copies of the best edition to be mailed, and the system generates a shipping slip for that purpose. Software has its own deposit rules, including options to redact trade secret portions of the source code, which our post on [copyright registration for software](/copyright-registration-for-software) covers in detail. The deposit should match the work described in the application; a mismatch is a common reason for correspondence from the examiner.

## Step six: pay, certify, and wait

Pay the current filing fee listed on the Copyright Office fee schedule by card or deposit account, certify that the statements are true, and submit. You receive an email confirmation with a case number; that confirmation, not the certificate, establishes your effective date. The examiner may send correspondence asking for clarification, which has a response deadline and should be answered promptly. If the claim is approved, a certificate is mailed. If it is refused, you have a window to request reconsideration, a process described in our post on [why the Copyright Office refuses some registrations](/why-copyright-office-refuses-registration).

Processing times vary with the application type and whether correspondence is needed. The Office publishes current estimates, and special handling is available for a higher fee when litigation or a customs recordation is pending.

## After the certificate issues

Check every field on the certificate against what you intended. Errors can be corrected through a supplementary registration, which adds to the record rather than replacing it. Record any later assignment with the Office so the public record follows the ownership.
