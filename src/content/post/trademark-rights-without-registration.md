In the United States, trademark rights come from use, not from a certificate. The day you start selling goods or services under a distinctive name, you acquire common-law rights in that name in the places where you actually do business and where customers know you. So the short answer to whether you have trademark rights without registration is yes. The longer answer is that those rights are narrow, hard to prove and easy to lose ground on, which is why our [trademark law services](/practice-area/key-services/trademark-law) page treats federal registration as the default for any brand that plans to grow.

## What trademark rights without registration actually cover

Common-law rights attach to the geographic area where you use the mark and the area into which your reputation has spread. A bakery selling under a name in Herndon, Virginia has rights in Herndon and probably in the surrounding region, but not in Seattle, where no one has heard of it. If another bakery adopts the same name in Seattle in good faith, both businesses can keep using it in their own territories, and neither can push the other out.

The rights also attach only to the goods and services you actually sell. Use on coffee does not reserve the name for clothing. And they depend on continuous use. Stop using the mark with no intent to resume, and the rights lapse; under the Lanham Act, three consecutive years of nonuse is prima facie evidence of abandonment.

Proof is where unregistered owners struggle. There is no public record of a common-law mark, so if a dispute arises you must show, with dated invoices, advertising, photographs and customer evidence, when you first used the mark, where, and on what. The other side will attack every gap in that record.

## What a federal trademark registration adds

Registration on the USPTO's Principal Register does not create the mark. It changes what you can do with it.

- Nationwide priority. Once the registration issues, your application filing date becomes a nationwide constructive-use date. Anyone who adopts a confusingly similar mark anywhere in the country after that date is junior to you, even if they never saw your mark.
- A legal presumption. The registration certificate is prima facie evidence that the mark is valid, that you own it and that you have the exclusive right to use it on the listed goods and services. You hand the court a certificate instead of proving your rights from scratch.
- The ® symbol, which only a registered mark may carry. The symbol puts the public on notice and preserves your ability to recover profits and damages from an infringer who had no other notice of your rights. Our post on [the TM symbol versus the ® symbol](/tm-vs-registered-trademark-symbol) explains when each is allowed.
- A place on the register. USPTO examining attorneys cite registered marks against later applications, so your registration can block a competitor's filing before the dispute ever reaches you.
- Access to U.S. Customs recordation and to marketplace brand registries, both of which require a registration number.
- Incontestability. After five years of continuous use following registration, you may file a Section 15 declaration that removes several of the grounds on which a competitor could later attack the registration.

## Can you sue over an unregistered trademark?

Yes. Section 43(a) of the Lanham Act lets the owner of an unregistered mark bring a federal claim for false designation of origin, and state unfair competition law offers similar claims. The catch is the burden. Without a registration you must establish that your mark is distinctive, that you used it first and that your rights reach the territory where the defendant operates. A registered owner starts the case with those points presumed, and a defendant who adopted the mark after your filing date cannot claim it acted in good faith.

## When registration is worth the cost

Registration earns its filing fee when your business sells in more than one state, sells online, plans to license or franchise, raises outside money, or operates in a market where a competitor could plausibly adopt the same name. Investors and acquirers ask for the registration certificate in diligence, and a missing one becomes a negotiating point. For a broader view of how trademarks sit alongside copyrights and patents, see our overview of [intellectual property law in the U.S.](/intellectual-property-law-in-the-u-s).

The case for staying unregistered is narrow: a single-location business with no plans to expand and a name so descriptive that the USPTO would refuse it anyway. Even then, a [trademark clearance search](/practice-area/key-services/trademark-search-and-clearance) is worth running so you know whether someone else already holds federal rights in the name you are using, because their registration can limit where you grow.

## How to protect the rights you already have

If you have used a mark for years without registering, you have not lost anything yet. Document your first-use dates and keep the evidence: dated photographs, invoices, advertisements and web captures. Use the TM symbol to signal your claim. Then file a use-based application through a [USPTO trademark registration](/practice-area/key-services/trademark-and-intellectual-property-registration) practice, which converts your existing priority into a nationwide record.

One more point for long-time users. If someone else registered the same mark after you began using it, your earlier use may support a petition to cancel their registration, but the clearest ground for that petition is available only within five years of their registration date. Waiting narrows your options.
