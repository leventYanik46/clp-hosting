An intent-to-use trademark application lets you file with the USPTO before you have sold a single product under the mark. You file under Section 1(b) of the Lanham Act on the strength of a bona fide intention to use the mark in commerce, the application is examined and published like any other, and the registration issues only after you prove use. The payoff is priority: once the mark registers, your rights date back to the filing date, ahead of anyone who adopted a similar mark while you were still building the product. Our [USPTO trademark registration](/practice-area/key-services/trademark-and-intellectual-property-registration) service files most pre-launch applications on this basis, and the process has deadlines that catch applicants who treat the filing as the finish line.

## What bona fide intent to use actually means

The statute requires a real, documented intention to use the mark on the listed goods or services, not a wish to reserve a word in case it becomes useful. An application filed to warehouse a name, or to cover forty products the applicant has no plan to make, can be attacked for lack of bona fide intent and voided, usually by an opponent in a TTAB proceeding who demands the applicant's business records.

Keep evidence from the start: a business plan that mentions the mark, product development files, supplier correspondence, mock-ups and marketing drafts. The evidence does not go in the application, but it should exist on the filing date. Limit the identification of goods and services to what you genuinely intend to offer.

## The intent-to-use trademark application step by step

- File the Section 1(b) application with the mark, the owner, the identification of goods and services, and the filing fee for each class.
- The examining attorney reviews it for the same substantive and procedural issues as a use-based application. Any office action must be answered on time, or the application is abandoned.
- If approved, the mark is published for opposition, and third parties have thirty days to oppose or request more time.
- When no opposition succeeds, the USPTO issues a Notice of Allowance. This is not a registration; it is permission to prove use.
- You file a Statement of Use with a specimen and dates of first use, pay the fee, and the USPTO examines the specimen.
- When the Statement of Use is accepted, the registration issues.

The gap between the Notice of Allowance and acceptance of the Statement of Use is where most intent-to-use applications are lost.

## Statement of Use versus Amendment to Allege Use

If you begin using the mark before the application is approved for publication, you may file an Amendment to Allege Use, which converts the application to a use basis and lets it register directly after publication. If you begin use after the Notice of Allowance, you file a Statement of Use. Between approval for publication and the Notice of Allowance there is a window in which neither filing is accepted; use that begins then is simply reported in the Statement of Use later.

Both filings require a specimen showing the mark in actual use on the listed goods or services, and both allow you to delete goods you are not yet using but never to add any. A specimen that does not show real use draws a refusal, and our post on [fixing a trademark specimen refusal](/trademark-specimen-refusal-how-to-fix) explains what the USPTO accepts.

## Deadlines after the Notice of Allowance

The Statement of Use is due within six months of the Notice of Allowance. If you are not using the mark yet, you may file a Request for Extension of Time before that deadline, which buys another six months. Each extension carries a fee and a verified statement that you still have a bona fide intention to use the mark, and from the second extension onward a statement of your ongoing efforts, such as product development or marketing preparation. Up to five extensions are available, for a maximum of thirty-six months from the Notice of Allowance.

Miss the deadline with no extension on file and the application is abandoned. A petition to revive is possible within two months of the notice of abandonment if the delay was unintentional, but after that window closes the only path is a new application with a new, later filing date. The three years are generous; the six-month checkpoints inside them are not.

## Intent-to-use versus a use-based application

File on a use basis under Section 1(a) when you are already selling the goods or rendering the services across state lines. It is simpler, it skips the Statement of Use stage and it registers sooner. File on an intent-to-use basis when launch is ahead, when a competitor could plausibly adopt the name first, or when you want the application pending while the product is finalized.

Two intent-to-use rules affect planning. First, the application cannot be assigned before an allegation of use except to a successor of the applicant's business, so file in the entity that will actually use the mark; our post on [who should own the trademark](/who-should-own-trademark-founder-or-company) explains why that choice is permanent. Second, the name should be cleared before filing just as it would be for a use-based application, through a [trademark search and clearance](/practice-area/key-services/trademark-search-and-clearance) review, because a refusal for likelihood of confusion costs the same either way and the [office action response](/practice-area/key-services/trademark-office-action-responses) will be due on the same short clock.
