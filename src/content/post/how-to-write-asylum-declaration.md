Form I-589 gives you a few lines to explain why you fear return. The asylum declaration is where you actually tell it: a signed, dated statement, usually five to fifteen pages, that the asylum officer or immigration judge reads before they see you and that your testimony is measured against afterward. A good declaration makes the interview a confirmation. A bad one creates the inconsistencies that end cases. Our [I-589 asylum application team](/practice-area/key-services/affirmative-asylum-applications) spends more hours on the declaration than on any other document in the file, and this post explains how it is built: what it must do, how to structure it, how much detail belongs in it, and how to handle the parts you cannot prove.

## What an asylum declaration has to do

The declaration carries the legal elements of the claim in narrative form. It must establish who you are and where you come from. It must describe the harm you suffered or the threats you received with enough specificity that a reader can picture each event. It must connect that harm to a protected ground, race, religion, nationality, political opinion, or a [particular social group](/particular-social-group-asylum-explained), and to a persecutor the government sponsors, tolerates, or cannot control. It must explain why you cannot return and why relocating within your own country is not a safe answer. If you filed more than a year after arrival, it must also explain why. Every one of those elements should be findable in the document by someone reading quickly.

## A structure that works

Chronological order, first person, plain language, short numbered paragraphs. Headings help the reader and help you at the interview, when the officer asks about an event and you can find it. The sections we use are: your background (family, education, religion, ethnicity, political activity, work); each incident of harm or threat in its own section, with the date, place, who was present, what was said, what was done, any injuries, and what you did afterward, including whether you went to the police and what they did; why and when you left, and the route; what has happened since you left, including threats to family and any contact from the persecutor; what you fear now and why moving to another city would not protect you; and, if relevant, why the application was filed when it was.

Write what happened, not what it means. A sentence saying you were persecuted on account of your political opinion is a legal conclusion that belongs in your attorney's brief. A sentence saying that three men in the uniform of the municipal police came to your shop the morning after the march and said that people who carry that flag do not get to keep their businesses is evidence.

## How much detail

The right level is specific where memory is specific and honest where it is not. Describe what you can see when you remember an event: the time of day, the vehicle, the words used, the sequence. Where you cannot, say so, for example that it was in the spring of 2021 and you do not recall the exact date. Never invent precision. A made-up date that conflicts with a hospital record or a passport stamp does more damage than an admitted gap, because it tells the officer the rest may be invented too.

Leave out what belongs elsewhere. Country conditions, news articles, and statistics go in the evidence index with a short explanation of relevance; the declaration should mention them only where they touch your own story. Avoid adjectives and avoid repetition. A declaration that reads like a template, with the same phrases an officer has seen in other files, invites suspicion that it was not written from your memory.

## Consistency with everything else in the record

The officer or judge will compare the declaration with the I-589 answers, with every visa application you ever filed, with the record of a credible fear interview if you had one, with police and medical records, with your spouse's or child's statements, and with your public social media. Every discrepancy needs to be reconciled inside the declaration, before anyone asks. If your visitor visa application said you had no fear and were traveling for tourism, explain why you said that. If you told a border officer a shorter version, say what you left out and why.

If the declaration is written in your language, file it with a certified English translation and the translator's certificate. If it was drafted in English with your attorney's help, have it read back to you in your language before you sign, and sign and date every version. You will be asked at the interview whether the contents are true and whether you understood them.

## Corroboration, and the evidence you cannot get

The REAL ID Act allows an adjudicator to require corroborating evidence for any fact unless you show that you cannot reasonably obtain it. The declaration should therefore pair each major event with a document where one exists, and explain the absence where one does not: police in your town do not issue reports, or requesting the record would expose a relative. Useful corroboration includes:

- Medical and hospital records, with translations, matched to the dates in the narrative.
- Threatening messages, with screenshots that show the sender, date, and platform, and translations.
- Witness statements from people who saw events or saw you afterward, identifying who they are, how they know, and attaching a copy of their identification.
- Membership cards, baptism or conversion certificates, party records, press credentials, photographs at events.
- Arrest records, court summonses, or news coverage naming you or your family.
- A psychological evaluation that explains trauma-related memory gaps, and an expert country-conditions declaration where nexus or social distinction is contested.

## The mistakes that cost cases

The declaration is unsigned, undated, or missing a translation certificate. It was written by a notario or a document preparer and reused across clients. It overstates, so that the applicant retreats from it under questioning. It omits an event that then surfaces at the interview. It was never updated after filing, so new threats, a new child, or a changed address appear nowhere in the record. Each of these is avoidable with one more review before filing and one more before the [interview itself](/asylum-interview-what-to-expect).
