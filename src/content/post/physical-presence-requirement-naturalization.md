Two requirements trip up many naturalization applicants: physical presence and continuous residence. They sound similar but measure different things. The physical presence requirement for naturalization counts the actual days you've been in the U.S. Continuous residence looks at whether you've maintained your U.S. home without long breaks.

## The physical presence requirement

You must have been physically present in the U.S. for at least half of the required residence period:

- **Five-year rule:** at least 30 months of physical presence in the last five years
- **Three-year rule (spouses of U.S. citizens):** at least 18 months of physical presence in the last three years

Every day outside the U.S. counts against physical presence, even a short trip.

## The continuous residence requirement

Continuous residence means you've lived in the U.S. as a permanent resident without breaking your residence:

- **Five years** for most applicants
- **Three years** for spouses of U.S. citizens

Short trips don't break continuous residence. Long trips can:

- **Trips of more than six months but less than one year:** create a presumption that you broke continuous residence. You can rebut it by showing you kept your job, home, and ties in the U.S.
- **Trips of one year or more:** generally break continuous residence, unless you filed Form N-470 before leaving to preserve residence for certain qualifying employment abroad.

## How they differ

| | Physical presence | Continuous residence |
|---|---|---|
| What it measures | Days actually in the U.S. | Whether your residence stayed in the U.S. |
| Effect of short trips | Each day counts against you | Generally no effect |
| Effect of long trips | Each day counts against you | Can break residence |

You must meet both.

## How to count your days

1. **List every trip** outside the U.S. during the statutory period.
2. **Count the days** for each trip. USCIS generally counts the departure and return dates as days in the U.S.
3. **Add the days** and subtract from the total period.
4. **Compare** with the 30-month or 18-month requirement.

Use your passport stamps, travel records, and CBP's travel history tool (I-94 website) to confirm dates. The N-400 asks for every trip of more than 24 hours.

## State residence requirement

You must also have lived for at least three months in the state or USCIS district where you file.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing physical presence with continuous residence
- Forgetting short trips
- Filing too early after a long trip
- Not having evidence to rebut the presumption for trips of six months to a year

## If you've had long trips

If you've had trips of six months or more, gather evidence of your ties: tax returns filed as a resident, a U.S. lease or mortgage, a job you kept, and family in the U.S. You may also want to wait until enough time has passed since the trip.

## Practical example

An applicant became a permanent resident in June 2021 and wants to file under the five-year rule in 2026. In that period she took eight trips abroad totaling 410 days, including one trip of seven months in 2023. Her physical presence is fine: about 1,416 days in the U.S. is well over 30 months. Her continuous residence is the issue. The seven-month trip creates a presumption that she broke residence. She can rebut it by showing she kept her job, her lease, and her tax filings as a U.S. resident during the trip. If she can't, she may need to wait until enough time has passed since her return to file.

## Why your N-400 answers need to match the records

USCIS can compare the trips you list with CBP entry and exit records. Missing trips, even short ones, raise credibility questions at the interview. Take the time to reconstruct your travel accurately before filing.

## Special cases

Some applicants have different rules:

- **Military members** can naturalize with reduced or no residence and physical presence requirements, depending on service.
- **Spouses of U.S. citizens employed abroad** by certain employers may naturalize without the usual residence requirements.
- **Employees of certain U.S. organizations abroad** may preserve continuous residence by filing Form N-470 before a long assignment.

If any of these apply to you, check the specific requirements before counting days the usual way.

## Planning future travel

If you plan to naturalize soon, avoid trips of more than six months until after your oath. Even a well-documented long trip creates a presumption you must rebut, and it's simpler to avoid the issue than to defend it at the interview.
