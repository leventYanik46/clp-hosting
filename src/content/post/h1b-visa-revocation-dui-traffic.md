A routine traffic ticket, such as speeding or a parking violation, almost never affects an H-1B. A DUI or DWI arrest is different. The State Department can revoke your H-1B visa stamp after a DUI arrest, even before any conviction. That does not automatically end your H-1B status in the U.S., but it changes how and whether you can travel.

## Visa vs. status: the distinction that matters

- **The visa** is the stamp in your passport. It is a travel document that lets you request entry at the border.
- **Your status** is the permission to stay and work, shown on your I-94 and approval notice.

If the State Department revokes the visa while you are inside the U.S., you can usually remain and keep working in valid H-1B status. You simply cannot use that visa to reenter after a trip abroad. You would need to apply for a new visa at a consulate.

## Why DUI arrests trigger revocation

Since 2015, the State Department has had a policy of prudentially revoking visas when it learns of a DUI arrest within the past five years that it did not know about when the visa was issued. The concern is medical: alcohol-related arrests may suggest a health-related ground of inadmissibility. Revocation is a precaution and does not mean a finding of wrongdoing.

You will usually receive an email or letter from the consulate that issued your visa. Do not ignore it.

## What happens at your next visa application

When you reapply for an H-1B visa, the consulate will typically refer you to a panel physician for a medical exam that focuses on alcohol use. The doctor's findings go to the consular officer. Bring court records showing the final outcome of the arrest, whether a dismissal, reduction, or conviction, and any treatment or education programs you completed.

Plan for added time. The medical referral can extend a stamping trip by weeks.

## Traffic tickets and other minor offenses

Minor traffic infractions that are not criminal offenses generally do not need to be disclosed on visa forms as arrests, but read each question carefully. Reckless driving, driving without a license, or leaving the scene of an accident may be criminal in some states and should be reviewed.

## Effect on your H-1B extension or green card

USCIS asks about arrests and convictions on many forms, including the I-485. A single DUI conviction without aggravating factors is not usually a bar to a green card, but multiple DUIs, DUIs with injuries, or driving on a suspended license can create larger problems. Always disclose arrests accurately; failing to disclose can be worse than the arrest itself.

## What to do if you are arrested

1. Hire a criminal defense attorney and tell them you are on an H-1B.
2. Talk to your immigration attorney before entering any plea.
3. Keep every court document, including the final disposition.
4. Do not travel internationally until you understand whether your visa has been or will be revoked.
5. Watch your email for a notice from the consulate.


## Frequently asked questions

### Will a DUI affect my green card?

A single DUI is not usually a bar, but it must be disclosed and can lead to questions about alcohol use during the medical exam. Multiple DUIs or aggravating factors raise more serious problems.

### Do I need to tell my employer?

Immigration law does not require it, but if travel or a new visa will be delayed, your employer may need to know for planning.

### Does a dismissed DUI still trigger revocation?

It can. Revocation is based on the arrest, not the conviction. A dismissal helps at the next visa interview, but the medical referral may still happen.
