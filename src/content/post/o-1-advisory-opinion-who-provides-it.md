USCIS will not approve an O-1 petition without a written advisory opinion, or proof that no organization exists to write one. The requirement sits in 8 CFR 214.2(o)(5), and it trips up petitioners who assume a strong CV is enough. The O-1 advisory opinion, also called the consultation, is a letter from an appropriate peer group, labor organization or expert that describes the work you will do and comments on your qualifications. It is advisory, not binding, so a lukewarm letter does not end the case. A missing letter, on the other hand, draws a Request for Evidence or a denial every time.

## What the O-1 advisory opinion must say

The regulation asks the consulting organization to address two things: the nature of the work to be done and your qualifications for it. A useful letter therefore identifies the organization and the writer's authority to speak for it, describes your achievements in the field, summarizes the proposed U.S. engagement, and states whether the position calls for a person of extraordinary ability or, for the arts, distinction. It should be on letterhead and signed. It is not a recommendation letter. Recommendation letters come from people who know your work and form part of your evidence; the consultation is a procedural requirement that USCIS treats as the field's own view of the petition. Many organizations issue a short "no objection" letter rather than a glowing one, and that satisfies the rule.

## Who provides it for scientists, executives and athletes

For O-1A beneficiaries the appropriate source is a peer group or labor organization in your field. The regulation says a peer group may include a person or persons with expertise, so where no formal body exists, a letter from a senior figure with documented standing (a department chair, the president of a professional society, a recognized industry expert) can serve. Our [O-1A visa for scientists, executives and athletes](/practice-area/key-services/o-1a-visa) page lists the criteria the letter should speak to. Athletes in sports with a players' association should expect that association to be the labor organization. Business executives and engineers usually have no union, so the petition relies on a peer group or expert letter and explains why that source is appropriate. The writer should be independent of the petitioner where possible; a letter from your future supervisor reads as an employer statement, not a consultation.

## Union rules for artists and for film and television

O-1B cases are more structured because the arts are unionized. For the arts, the consultation comes from a labor organization such as Actors' Equity for stage performers, SAG-AFTRA for screen and recorded media, or the American Federation of Musicians for musicians, or from a peer group where no union covers the discipline. For motion picture and television work the rule is stricter: the petition must include opinions from both the appropriate union and a management organization in the field. Our [O-1B visa](/practice-area/key-services/o-1b-visa) page covers which standard applies, and [O-1B for film and television work](/o-1b-film-television-standard) explains the double-consultation rule in detail. Unions typically charge a consultation fee and publish their own turnaround windows, so request the letter early. The regulations also allow USCIS to waive the consultation for an artist returning to perform similar services within two years of a previous consultation; the petitioner must ask for the waiver and attach a copy of the earlier opinion. That waiver is written for the arts, so do not count on it for a film or television case.

## What happens if no appropriate peer group exists

If no appropriate peer group or labor organization exists, USCIS decides the petition on the evidence in the record. The burden is on the petitioner to show that none exists, so document the search: name the organizations you considered, explain why each is inappropriate (wrong discipline, no consultation function, no U.S. presence), and include any written refusals. This situation is common for emerging technologies, niche sciences and business roles. Do not simply omit the letter and hope. An officer who finds a plausible organization you did not approach will issue an RFE, and the response window is short.

## Negative, late or unhelpful opinions

Because the opinion is advisory, USCIS weighs it alongside the rest of the petition. A negative letter is not fatal, but you must answer it. Unions sometimes object on labor-market grounds, arguing that U.S. workers are available; that is not the O-1 test, and the petition should say so while pointing to the criteria you meet. If a union says it lacks enough information, supplement the request rather than filing a weak letter. If an organization refuses to respond, the petition can explain the attempts made; USCIS may contact the organization directly, which adds time. Keep copies of every request and reply. The criteria themselves are the subject of [O-1 visa: proving extraordinary ability to USCIS](/o-1-visa-extraordinary-ability), and the consultation is the last document to secure before the petition goes out.

## Timing the request inside the petition plan

Request the consultation after the brief and exhibits are drafted, so the organization sees the same case USCIS will see, but before the filing date, since the petition is incomplete without it. For agent petitions with a multi-city itinerary, send the itinerary with the request; unions want to know where and when you will perform. Where premium processing is used, USCIS acts within a short fixed window, so a consultation error surfaces fast and must be corrected within the RFE period. Build two to four weeks into the plan for the letter alone, more if the organization is slow or asks questions.
