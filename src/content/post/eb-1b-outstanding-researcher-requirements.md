The EB-1B is the first preference green card for outstanding professors and researchers, and it is the one EB-1 subcategory that most working academics can realistically reach. The EB-1B requirements, set out in INA 203(b)(1)(B) and 8 CFR 204.5(i), come down to three elements and a criteria count: you must be recognized internationally as outstanding in a specific academic area, you must have at least three years of teaching or research experience in that area, an employer must offer you a qualifying permanent position, and the petition must document at least two of six listed criteria. Our [EB-1B outstanding researcher green card](/practice-area/key-services/eb-1b-green-card) page summarizes the category; this post explains what each requirement means in practice and where cases fall short.

## EB-1B requirements: international recognition as outstanding

The standard is international recognition as outstanding in a specific academic area. It is lower than the EB-1A's very top of the field, but it has two parts that are tested separately. Recognition must be international: citations, collaborations, invitations and letters from researchers outside the United States and outside your home country carry particular weight, and a record confined to one institution or one national community will not do. And it must be in a specific academic area, which means the petition has to define the area and show that the recognition relates to it. A researcher who has moved between fields should expect the officer to ask which field the petition is about.

USCIS applies the same two-step analysis used in EB-1A cases: first, whether two criteria are met, and second, a final merits determination asking whether the record as a whole shows international recognition as outstanding. Most researchers clear step one through publications and peer review. The case is decided at step two, on citation data, journal standing and independent letters.

## Three years of teaching or research experience

The three years must be in the academic area, and they are counted after the degree in most cases. Under 8 CFR 204.5(i)(3)(ii), experience gained while working toward an advanced degree counts only if the degree was awarded and either you had full responsibility for the classes you taught or the research toward the degree has been recognized within the field as outstanding. For most researchers, that means doctoral years do not count unless the dissertation research itself has attracted recognition, which some cases can show through citations and awards, and postdoctoral, industry and faculty positions after the degree do count. Document the three years with employment verification letters that state dates, titles and duties.

## A qualifying permanent job offer

The employer must offer one of three positions: a tenured or tenure-track teaching position at a university or institution of higher education; a comparable permanent research position at such an institution; or a comparable research position at a department, division or institute of a private employer that employs at least three persons full time in research activities and has documented accomplishments in an academic field. Permanent means tenured, tenure-track or of indefinite duration with an expectation of continued employment.

This requirement is where EB-1B petitions most often fail, because many research positions are funded on fixed-term grants or structured as postdoctoral appointments with end dates. Our post on [what counts as a permanent job offer for EB-1B](/eb-1b-permanent-job-offer) works through the common position types. The employer also signs Form I-140 and must prove its ability to pay the offered wage; you cannot file an EB-1B for yourself.

## Two of six criteria

The six criteria at 8 CFR 204.5(i)(3)(i) are: major prizes or awards for outstanding achievement; membership in associations that require outstanding achievement; published material by others about your work; participation as a judge of the work of others; original scientific or scholarly research contributions; and authorship of scholarly books or articles in journals with international circulation. Two are required, and comparable evidence is allowed where a criterion does not fit. Our post on [the six EB-1B criteria and the evidence for each](/eb-1b-criteria-evidence) covers documentation; the short version is that authorship and judging are the common two, while contributions and published material about you are what carry the final merits stage.

## Who fits the EB-1B profile

The researcher who qualifies most comfortably has a PhD, several years of post-degree research, a publication record cited by groups in several countries, regular peer review, and an offer of a research scientist, assistant professor or equivalent position with no end date from an institution that will sponsor. The researcher who struggles has a strong record but a postdoctoral title, or an industry offer from a company whose research group is small or whose output is not published. In the first case the EB-1B is the natural choice; in the second, an [EB-1A self-petition](/practice-area/key-services/eb-1a-green-card) or an EB-2 National Interest Waiver may be the better route, often filed alongside the EB-1B.

Status matters too. Researchers on [J-1 exchange visitor](/practice-area/key-services/j-1-visa) status who are subject to the two-year home residence requirement can have an EB-1B approved but cannot adjust status until the requirement is satisfied or waived, so the waiver belongs in the plan from the start.
