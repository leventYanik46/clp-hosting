Two clocks run in every Special Immigrant Juvenile Status case, and they do not agree. Federal law lets a young person file the I-360 petition any time before turning 21. The state court that must first issue the required findings may lose authority over the young person at 18, depending on the court and the type of case. A 19-year-old who qualifies under federal law can be shut out because no court in the state will take the case. Understanding how SIJS age out rules work is the difference between a case that gets filed and one that never can be.

## The federal SIJS age limit: under 21 on the filing date

Under 8 CFR 204.11, the petitioner must be under 21 and unmarried when USCIS receives Form I-360. Congress fixed the age question at filing in 2008, so a petitioner who files at 20 and turns 21 while USCIS is deciding does not lose eligibility. The same protection carries through to the green card: turning 21 while waiting for an EB-4 visa number does not end the case. Marriage is different. The young person must stay unmarried until USCIS decides the petition, and a marriage during that window ends eligibility with no waiver available.

The filing date also becomes the priority date for the visa number. In a category with a backlog, filing in March rather than June can mean a materially shorter wait years later. Our [SIJS representation](/practice-area/key-services/special-immigrant-juvenile-status) is built around getting the state order and the federal petition done in the shortest sequence the facts allow.

## The state court clock: jurisdiction can end at 18

The harder deadline is almost always in state court. The federal statute requires a juvenile court order, and a juvenile court can only act while it has jurisdiction over the young person under state law. In many states, and in many types of proceedings, that jurisdiction ends on the eighteenth birthday. Some states have extended it for SIJS purposes. New York, for example, allows the Family Court to appoint a guardian for a young person who is over 18 but under 21 when the young person consents. In the District of Columbia and Virginia, the answer depends on the type of case and the court, and in some proceedings the practical cutoff is 18.

Age is measured when the state case is filed in some courts and when the order is entered in others. If a court requires the young person to be under 18 when the petition is filed, a case filed the week before the birthday may be enough. If it requires the order itself to be entered before 18, there is often no time for service by publication on an absent parent. We check the rule for the specific court before promising a family anything.

## What happens when the order comes after jurisdiction ended

The regulation requires that the state court order be in effect when the I-360 is filed and through the decision, with an exception when the court's jurisdiction ended because of the young person's age or because the case closed in a permanent placement such as adoption or permanent guardianship. That exception protects an order that was properly entered at 17 even though the guardianship terminated at 18. It does not help a young person who never got an order because no court would hear the case. For those clients the question becomes whether another court or another proceeding still has authority, or whether a different form of relief fits better.

## Other deadlines inside an SIJS case

Age is the headline deadline, but a few others shape the case. USCIS is directed to decide the I-360 within 180 days, which gives families a benchmark for when to follow up. A Request for Evidence sets a fixed response date, and if the answer requires an amended state court order, the state court's calendar must fit inside it. Each sibling needs their own state order and their own I-360, because SIJS has no derivative beneficiaries. And the SIJS recipient's later eligibility for the green card, covered in our post on [the SIJS green card after I-360 approval](/sijs-green-card-adjustment-of-status), depends on the priority date set by the original filing.

## A working timeline for a 17-year-old

For a client who is 17 and living with a relative in Northern Virginia, the plan usually looks like this. In the first week, we gather the birth certificate, school and medical records, and any messages or letters that show the parent's conduct, and we identify the parent's last known address abroad. In the second week, we file the guardianship or custody petition with a proposed order containing the three findings and the state statute behind each, and we start service on the parents. Service by mail or publication then controls the pace, often several weeks. At the hearing, the guardian and the young person testify, and we ask the judge to sign the proposed order with the facts recited. The I-360 is filed within days of the signed order. Our post on [the three findings the court order must contain](/sijs-state-court-order-findings) describes what we are asking the judge to sign.

## When the start is late

A young person who is already 18 in a court that stops at 18 has not necessarily run out of options. Some may have an [asylum claim](/practice-area/key-services/affirmative-asylum-applications) if they fear persecution at home, a family petition through a U.S. citizen or permanent resident relative, or a U visa if they were the victim of a crime in the United States. A young person already in immigration court may have defenses the judge can grant. The eligibility rules for the SIJS route itself are set out in [who qualifies for Special Immigrant Juvenile Status](/who-qualifies-for-sijs). What no one can do is restart the state court clock, which is why we treat every call about a 17-year-old as a same-week matter.
