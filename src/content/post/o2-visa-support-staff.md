An O-2 visa lets a person come to the U.S. to assist an O-1 artist or athlete in a specific event or performance. It is built for the people the principal truly cannot work without: a longtime camera operator on a documentary, a coach for an Olympic athlete, a sound engineer for a touring musician. An O-2 is not a general visa for an O-1's team or staff.

## Who can get an O-2 visa

To qualify, the O-2 applicant must:

- Accompany an O-1 artist or athlete for a specific event or performance
- Be an integral part of the O-1's actual performance
- Have critical skills and experience with the O-1 that are not general in nature and cannot be performed by a U.S. worker
- Keep a foreign residence they have no intention of abandoning

For O-1A beneficiaries, O-2 support is limited to athletic events. Scientists, business executives, and educators on O-1A status generally cannot bring O-2 staff.

For film and television production, the O-2 must have skills and experience with the O-1 that are critical either because of a pre-existing longstanding working relationship or because significant parts of the production will take place both inside and outside the U.S.

## What USCIS wants to see

The petition, filed on Form I-129 separately from the O-1 petition, should show:

- The O-1's approval or pending petition and itinerary
- The O-2's specific role in the events
- A history of working with the O-1, through credits, contracts, or statements
- Why the O-2's skills are critical and not easily replaced
- A consultation letter from a labor organization, and for film and television, from a management organization as well

Weak O-2 petitions usually describe the role in general terms ("assistant," "manager," "crew") without showing why this particular person is irreplaceable.

## Duration and limits

O-2 status is granted for the time needed to support the O-1's events, up to three years at first, with extensions in one-year increments. O-2 status depends on the O-1's status. If the O-1 ends, the O-2 ends too.

O-2 holders cannot work for anyone else in the U.S. or perform services outside their role with the O-1. Their dependents can come in O-3 status but cannot work.

## O-2 vs. other options

If a support person has strong credentials of their own, an O-1 in their own right may be more flexible. For tours of culturally unique groups, the P classifications may fit better. For a crew member who will stay long term, an H-1B or other work visa may be needed.

## Filing tips

- File the O-1 and O-2 petitions together so they can be reviewed at the same time
- Match the itinerary dates in both petitions
- Document the working history with specifics: productions, tours, seasons, and outcomes
- Prepare the O-2 for the consular interview, which now generally must take place in person in the applicant's country of nationality or residence


## Frequently asked questions

### Can an O-2 work for another artist?

No. O-2 status is tied to supporting the specific O-1 beneficiary named in the petition.

### Is there a cap on O-2 visas?

No. Like the O-1, the O-2 has no annual limit.

### Can O-2 staff change to another status later?

Possibly, but the O-2's foreign residence requirement means a green card filing may conflict with O-2 renewals. Plan any change with counsel.

### How is an O-2 different from a P-1S?

Both cover support staff. The P-1S supports P-1 athletes and entertainment groups, while the O-2 supports O-1 artists and athletes. The support visa follows the principal's classification.
