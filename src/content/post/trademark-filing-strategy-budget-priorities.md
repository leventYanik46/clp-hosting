A company with one brand name, three product names, a logo, a tagline and plans for two foreign markets is looking at a dozen possible filings. Few early-stage budgets cover all of them, and the ones that try usually overspend on the tagline and underspend on the one mark that carries the revenue. A trademark filing strategy is the ranking that decides which trademarks to register first, in which classes and countries, and what can wait a year. It is the first deliverable of the [IP portfolio strategy](/practice-area/key-services/ip-portfolio-management) service at Capitol Law Partners, and the ranking rules below are the ones we apply.

## Start with the house mark, then the names that carry revenue

The house mark, the name customers use for the company itself, comes first. It appears on every product, every contract and every invoice, and losing it would mean rebranding the whole business. The flagship product name comes second if it is marketed separately from the house mark and customers ask for it by name. Secondary product names, sub-brands and taglines follow, in order of how much revenue each one identifies and how hard it would be to rename.

Before any filing, check that the name can be registered at all. A merely descriptive name, one that simply describes the goods or a feature of them, will not register on the Principal Register without proof of acquired distinctiveness, and spending the budget on a doomed application is the most common early mistake. Our [trademark search and clearance](/practice-area/key-services/trademark-search-and-clearance) review tests both registrability and conflicts before the fee is paid.

## Word mark before logo

File the word mark in standard characters first. A standard character registration covers the word in any font, color or stylization, so it protects the name however the design evolves. A logo registration protects that specific design, and logos change every few years; a refreshed logo may no longer match the registered one, which creates problems at the next maintenance filing. Register the logo separately when it is distinctive on its own, is used without the word, or is the element competitors are most likely to imitate. If the budget allows only one filing, it is the word mark.

## Choose classes by what you sell, not what you might sell

The USPTO fee is charged per class, and every class you add costs money twice: at filing and at every maintenance filing afterward. Claim the classes that cover goods and services you sell now or will sell within the window an intent-to-use application allows. A software company that might someday sell branded hats does not need Class 25 today. Overclaiming also creates audit exposure, because a maintenance declaration that lists goods you never sold can draw a post-registration audit and, in a dispute, a fraud or expungement challenge.

Within each class, describe the goods precisely. A narrow, accurate identification is cheaper to maintain and easier to defend than a long list that invites scrutiny.

## Use intent-to-use filings to lock priority early

You do not need to be selling to file. An intent-to-use application under Section 1(b) gives you a filing date that serves as your nationwide priority date once the mark registers, provided you have a bona fide intent to use the mark, which should be documented with business plans, prototypes or marketing budgets. After the notice of allowance, you have six months to file a statement of use, extendable in six-month steps up to three years, each with a fee. For a product launching in the next year, an intent-to-use filing is the cheapest insurance against a competitor adopting the same name first. For a product that is three years from launch, it is probably premature.

## When to file abroad and where

Outside the United States, rights usually go to the first party to file, not the first to use. So the question is not whether you have sales abroad yet but where a loss would hurt: the countries where you will sell within two years, the country where your products are manufactured, and markets where counterfeiting of your product category is common. File in those before launch, distributor agreements or trade shows there. The Paris Convention gives six months from your U.S. filing date to file abroad with the same priority, which lets you spread the foreign cost across two budget periods while keeping the early date. We compare the Madrid Protocol route with direct national filings in [international trademark registration: Madrid or direct filing](/madrid-protocol-vs-direct-national-filing).

## Budget tiers and the costs people forget

A workable plan has three tiers. Tier one, filed now: the house mark in standard characters in the core class, plus foreign filings in the one or two markets where a loss would be worst. Tier two, filed within the next year: the flagship product name, the logo, and the next class or market. Tier three, watched but not filed: taglines, minor product names and speculative markets, with a trigger to move them up if revenue or risk changes.

Then add the costs that do not appear on the filing receipt. Maintenance filings at years five to six and nine to ten, with fees per class. Responses to office actions, which a meaningful share of applications draw. A trademark watch to catch conflicting applications while you can still oppose them. And a reserve for enforcement, because a registration you cannot afford to assert is worth less than it looks. A docket that carries all those dates is described in our post on [IP docketing](/ip-docketing-deadlines-explained), and a broader overview of the categories of protection is in [intellectual property law in the U.S.](/intellectual-property-law-in-the-u-s).

## Mistakes that waste a limited budget

Registering a descriptive name instead of adopting a stronger one. Skipping clearance and discovering the conflict in an office action after the fee is spent. Filing in a founder's personal name rather than the company's. Letting a tagline consume a filing fee that the flagship product name needed. And filing nothing abroad until the first foreign distributor registers the mark for you, at which point the cost of recovering it dwarfs the cost of having filed.
