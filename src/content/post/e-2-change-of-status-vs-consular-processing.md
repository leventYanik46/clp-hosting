An E-2 change of status lets you switch into treaty investor status without leaving the United States, but it gives you status, not a visa. That single difference drives most of the decision. If you are already here in a valid nonimmigrant status, such as H-1B, F-1, L-1, or B-2, you can ask USCIS to change you to E-2 by filing Form I-129 with the E supplement. If you are abroad, or you want a visa you can travel on, you apply at a U.S. consulate with Form DS-160 and, at most posts, Form DS-156E. Both routes test the same requirements. They differ in who decides, how travel works, and what a refusal costs you.

## Who can file an E-2 change of status

You must be in valid status on the day USCIS receives the I-129. If your I-94 has already expired, or you worked without authorization, the change of status route is generally closed and the consulate becomes the only path.

Some people cannot change status at all. Visitors admitted under the Visa Waiver Program on ESTA are barred from changing to any other nonimmigrant status. So are people admitted in transit or crew status, and J-1 exchange visitors who are subject to the two-year home residency requirement and have not obtained a waiver.

Intent at entry matters too. A B-2 visitor may look at businesses, meet sellers, and talk to accountants. A visitor who arrives, signs a purchase agreement within days, and files for E-2 can face questions about whether the stated purpose of the trip was accurate. The fix is not to avoid the route, but to make sure the timeline in your file is honest and explainable.

The underlying case is identical either way. Your [E-2 treaty investor visa](/practice-area/key-services/e-2-visa) still needs treaty nationality for you and the company, a substantial investment that is committed and at risk, a real operating business that is not marginal, and a role in which you develop and direct the enterprise. If you are still sizing the investment, our post on the [E-2 minimum investment question](/e-2-visa-minimum-investment-2026) explains the proportionality test.

## How the two routes differ in practice

With a change of status, a USCIS officer reviews a paper record. There is no interview. If something is missing, the officer usually sends a Request for Evidence, which gives you a chance to fix the gap before a decision. Approval comes as a Form I-797 notice with a new I-94 that grants E-2 status for up to two years.

With consular processing, a State Department officer reviews your submission and then interviews you, often briefly. The officer decides at or shortly after the window. A refusal is immediate and there is no formal appeal, though you can reapply. Approval produces a visa whose validity depends on the reciprocity schedule for your country, and each entry on it gives you a two-year admission.

The evidence package is the same in substance: corporate documents, proof of ownership, bank records tracing the funds, the lease or purchase agreement, and a business plan. Some consulates have their own E visa units with formatting rules, page limits, or company registration steps. USCIS has none of that, but it reads the file without hearing you explain it.

## Travel is the catch

An approved change of status does not put a visa in your passport. The first time you leave the United States, you must apply for an E-2 visa at a consulate before you can come back in E-2 status. The consular officer is not bound by the USCIS approval and will decide the case fresh.

That creates a real risk for investors who travel. Picture an approved change of status, a family emergency abroad six months later, and a consular refusal while the business, the lease, and the staff are all in the United States. That scenario is the reason many investors who expect to travel within the first year choose the consulate from the start.

If you rarely travel, the calculation flips. You can operate under the I-797 approval, extend through USCIS in two-year increments with another I-129, and deal with the visa only when you actually need to leave.

## Timing, work, and cost factors

We do not quote processing times here because they move. For a change of status, the wait depends on USCIS workload and whether you pay for premium processing, which is available for E-2 petitions on Form I-129; check the current USCIS filing fee and premium processing fee before you file. For a consular case, the wait depends on the post's appointment backlog and on whether the case goes into administrative processing. Check USCIS processing times and the consulate's own website before you plan around a date.

One rule catches people off guard: you generally cannot start working in the E-2 business until USCIS approves the change. You can keep doing whatever your current status allows. An H-1B employee can keep working for the H-1B employer while the E-2 case is pending, but cannot run the new company day to day. If the business needs you in charge immediately, that gap belongs in the plan.

## Which route fits your situation

A change of status tends to fit when you:

- are in valid status that will not lapse before filing
- have no travel planned for the next several months
- face a long appointment wait at your home consulate
- want the chance to answer an RFE instead of a same-day refusal

Consular processing tends to fit when you are already abroad, travel often for the business, have a status history that would complicate a USCIS filing, or simply want a visa in hand. Spouses do well under either route: an E-2 spouse is employment authorized incident to status, and the I-94 should carry the E-2S annotation.
