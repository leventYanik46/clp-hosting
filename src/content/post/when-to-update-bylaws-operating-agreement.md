Most companies write their bylaws or operating agreement once, at formation, from a template, and never open the document again. Then a co-founder leaves, an investor comes in, a manager starts signing contracts nobody authorized, and the governing document still describes a company that no longer exists. The right time to update your bylaws or operating agreement is when the way the business actually makes decisions has drifted from the way the document says it should. Capitol Law Partners' [corporate governance and internal policies](/practice-area/key-services/corporate-governance-and-internal-policies) work often begins with exactly that gap: a document from year one and a business in year six.

## Why a stale governing document is a real problem

Bylaws (for a corporation) and an operating agreement (for an LLC) are the rules the owners agreed to follow. Courts, lenders, investors, and buyers read them literally. If the operating agreement says all members must approve a lease and the manager signed one alone, the lease may be valid against the landlord, but the manager has breached the agreement and a disgruntled member has a claim. If the bylaws require an annual shareholder meeting and none has been held in years, a dissident shareholder can use that failure in a dispute, and a buyer's diligence will flag it.

Where the document is silent, state default rules fill in, and the defaults are not always what the owners would have chosen. Virginia, DC, and New York each have their own LLC and corporation statutes, and the defaults on matters like voting by ownership percentage versus per capita, transfer of interests, and withdrawal rights differ. An outdated or silent document hands those questions to a statute the owners have never read.

## Events that should trigger a review

The pattern is consistent across industries. Update your bylaws or operating agreement when any of the following happens:

- Ownership changes. A new owner joins, a founder leaves, interests are transferred, or an estate inherits an interest. The document should reflect who owns what, how they vote, and what happens to their interest on death, disability, divorce, or departure.
- Outside investment. Investors bring their own requirements: a board seat, approval rights over major decisions, preferred economics, information rights, and anti-dilution protection. These must be written into the governing documents, not just the term sheet.
- A change in management. The founders who ran everything step back, a professional CEO is hired, or a manager-managed LLC starts behaving like a member-managed one. Delegations of authority, officer roles, and spending limits need to match reality.
- A 50/50 split with no tie-breaker. Two equal owners and no deadlock mechanism is a dissolution waiting to happen. Our post on [breaking a deadlock between 50/50 owners](/breaking-50-50-owner-deadlock) explains the tools, all of which work better if they are in the document before the dispute.
- A planned sale, financing, or audit. Buyers and lenders will read the documents. Fixing them during diligence is possible but looks careless and costs negotiating credibility.
- A change in law or tax status. Electing S corporation status, converting entity type, or a change in the state statute may require conforming amendments.

## What an update usually covers

Start with the decision-making map. Who can bind the company, for what, and up to what amount? Which decisions require the board or the managers, which require the owners, and at what vote threshold? Most disputes trace back to one of those questions being unanswered. Our post on [when a company needs a board resolution](/when-do-you-need-board-resolution) lists the decisions that typically belong at the board level.

Then the ownership mechanics: transfer restrictions, rights of first refusal, buy-sell provisions, valuation methods, and what happens when an owner stops working in the business. An operating agreement that lets a departing member keep a full economic interest forever, with no buyout, is a common and expensive oversight.

Then the procedural rules: meeting and notice requirements, written consents, quorum, and recordkeeping. A document that requires in-person meetings for a company whose owners live in three states should be amended to allow electronic meetings and written consents, which most state statutes now permit.

Finally, conflicts and compliance: a conflict of interest procedure, indemnification and advancement of expenses for directors and officers, and any industry-specific requirements. These often live in separate policies, but the governing document should authorize them.

## How to amend the document correctly

The document itself says how it can be amended, and that procedure controls. Bylaws can usually be amended by the board, the shareholders, or either, depending on the articles and the state statute. Operating agreements typically require a vote of members at a stated threshold, and some provisions, such as those affecting a particular member's economics, may require that member's consent. Follow the procedure, document the vote in a written consent or minutes, and keep the amended document with the company's records.

Check the articles or certificate of formation at the same time. Some provisions, like a corporation's authorized shares or, in some states, an LLC's management structure, live in the state filing and require an amendment filed with the state (the State Corporation Commission in Virginia, the Department of Licensing and Consumer Protection in DC, or the Department of State in New York). Our post on the [documents required to set up a company in America](/documents-required-to-set-up-a-company-in-america) describes what those formation filings contain.
