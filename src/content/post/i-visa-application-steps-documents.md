The I visa application goes straight to a U.S. embassy or consulate. There is no Form I-129, no USCIS petition, and no labor condition application; the employer's letter and your credentials do the work a petition would do in other categories. That makes the process faster than an H-1B or O-1, but it also means a single interview decides everything, with no RFE stage in between. Prepare the file as if an adjudicator will read every page, because the consular officer will.

## Step one: confirm you fit the category

Before filing anything, test the assignment against the three requirements: a foreign media employer with its home office abroad, a role essential to that employer's informational function, and content that is news, documentary, or educational rather than entertainment or advertising. Our post on [who qualifies for an I visa](/who-qualifies-i-visa-foreign-media) works through each. If the answer is uncertain on any of them, resolve it now; a refusal under Section 214(b) at the interview is recorded and must be disclosed in every future application.

## Step two: the DS-160 and the appointment

Complete Form DS-160 online, selecting the I visa classification. The form asks for your employer, your position, the purpose of travel, your intended length of stay, and prior U.S. travel. Answer the employment questions with the foreign media organization's details, not the U.S. bureau's, and describe the assignment in plain terms (for example, "correspondent covering U.S. economic news for a broadcaster based in Istanbul"). Inconsistency between the DS-160 and the employer letter is the most common avoidable problem we see, and our [I visa practice](/practice-area/key-services/i-visa) page describes how we reconcile the two before submission.

Pay the visa application fee at the current rate listed by the consulate, print the confirmation page, and schedule the interview. Some posts offer an interview waiver for renewals in the same category; check the specific consulate's rules rather than assuming.

## Step three: assemble the documents

Bring originals where you have them. The core set:

- A passport valid for at least six months beyond the intended stay
- The DS-160 confirmation page and fee receipt
- A letter from the employer on letterhead, signed by an editor or manager, stating your name, position, the purpose and expected duration of the assignment, and confirming that the organization's home office is outside the United States
- Your press credential or press card, and for members of an independent production company, the credential from a professional journalistic association
- For freelancers, the contract with the foreign media organization covering the period of the assignment
- Evidence of the organization's media activity: mastheads, broadcast schedules, or a link to its published work
- For event coverage, the accreditation or assignment confirmation

If a crew is traveling together, each member applies separately with a letter naming the full crew and each person's role. Technical staff should be described by function (camera, sound, satellite uplink) and the letter should state that the content is for broadcast abroad.

## Step four: the interview

Interviews are short. Expect questions about what you will cover, where, for how long, and who pays you. The officer is testing two things: that the work is journalistic and that you will leave when the assignment ends. The I visa does not carry the same presumption of immigrant intent as a B-2, but officers still look at the employment relationship and the organization's reality. Answer consistently with the letter. Do not describe side projects, personal content channels, or plans to seek U.S. employment; those are either irrelevant or harmful.

If the officer refuses the application, ask for the refusal letter and the section cited. A Section 214(b) refusal can be reapplied for with new evidence; a Section 221(g) hold means the officer wants more documents or administrative processing. Our general note on [what to do after a visa denial](/my-visa-application-was-denied-what-should-i-do) applies here, with the added point that I visa refusals are frequently cured by a better employer letter.

## Step five: validity, entry, and what the I-94 says

Visa validity and number of entries follow the reciprocity schedule for your nationality, which ranges from a few months to several years. The visa is only permission to travel to a port of entry. At the border, Customs and Border Protection admits I visa holders for the period of the assignment; as of this writing, that has been recorded on the I-94 as duration of status (D/S), though DHS has proposed replacing it with fixed admission periods for I, F, and J categories. Check your electronic I-94 after every entry and note whether it shows D/S or a date. If it shows a date, you will need to file Form I-539 before that date to stay longer, as our post on [changing employers or extending an I visa stay](/i-visa-change-employer-or-extension) explains.

## Dependents

Your spouse and unmarried children under 21 may apply for I visas as dependents at the same time, with marriage and birth certificates. They may study in the United States but may not work. If a spouse intends to work, he or she needs an independent basis such as an [H-1B specialty occupation petition](/practice-area/key-services/h-1b-visa) or an O-1, and an employer willing to file for it.
