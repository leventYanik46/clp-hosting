An E-2 business plan is evidence, not a pitch deck. The consular officer or USCIS adjudicator reads it to answer two questions the law asks about every treaty investor: is the money you put in enough to get this particular business running, and will the business grow beyond earning a living for you and your family? If the plan answers both with specific, checkable facts, it carries the case. If it reads like a template, it invites a refusal, even when the investment itself is sound.

## What the officer is trying to learn from your E-2 business plan

State Department guidance asks whether the enterprise has the present or future capacity to generate more than a minimal living for the investor and family, or to make a significant economic contribution, usually by creating jobs. For a new business, officers generally look for that capacity within five years of the start of operations. The plan is the only document that shows the future.

The officer also uses the plan to test the rest of the file. Does the budget match the amount you claim to have invested? Does the business described match the lease, the licenses, and the purchase agreement? Does your role look like someone who will develop and direct the company, or someone who will work the counter? Every requirement on our [E-2 visa requirements](/practice-area/key-services/e-2-visa) page shows up somewhere in the plan.

## Sections a strong plan includes

A plan that works at the consulate is organized so an officer can find answers fast, and it stays within any page limit the post sets. The core sections:

- An executive summary that states the business, the location, the total investment, the ownership split, and the five-year hiring target in one page.
- Company structure and ownership, showing that at least 50 percent is held by nationals of your treaty country.
- Your background and why it equips you to run this business.
- A local market analysis with named competitors, real pricing, and a reason customers will choose you.
- Operations: premises, suppliers, licenses, equipment, opening date.
- A staffing plan listing each position, its start date, and its wage.
- Five years of projected profit and loss and cash flow, with the assumptions written out.
- A use-of-funds breakdown showing where every dollar of the investment went or is committed.

Keep the writing plain. Officers are not grading prose. They want to see that you understand your own business.

## The numbers must match the bank records

The most common internal contradiction is between the use-of-funds section and the money trail. If the plan says you spent a certain amount on build-out, the file should contain the contractor invoices and the wires that paid them. If the plan says you bought equipment, there should be a bill of sale. An officer who finds a gap of any size will ask about it, and a vague answer at the window can sink an otherwise strong case.

The same goes for staffing. A plan that projects eight employees in year two but shows payroll costs that would cover three tells the officer the projections were not built from the ground up. Wages should match local market rates, and the payroll line in the profit and loss should equal the sum of the positions in the staffing plan.

For an acquisition, the plan starts from the seller's actual tax returns and financial statements. It should explain what you will change after closing and why revenue will grow. Inflated projections on an existing business are easy to spot because the history is right there.

## Marginality: the question the plan must answer

A business is marginal when it only supports the investor and family. That is the finding behind many E-2 refusals, and it is decided almost entirely from the plan. There is no fixed number of jobs the law requires, but a hiring plan that brings on U.S. workers over the first few years, with the payroll to back it, is the clearest way to show the business is more than a vehicle for your own salary.

Small service businesses face the hardest version of this test. A one-person consulting firm with no hires planned rarely clears it, whatever the investment. If your concept is small, the plan needs a credible growth path: a second location, a sales hire, a contract pipeline. Our post on the [E-2 minimum investment](/e-2-visa-minimum-investment-2026) explains why a modest investment can be fine when the plan shows the business will scale.

## Mistakes that weaken an E-2 plan

The plans that cause trouble tend to share a few features. A purchased template still names a different city or industry. The financial model was built by someone who never spoke to the investor. The plan describes the investor as an owner who will "oversee" while a manager runs everything, which undercuts the develop-and-direct requirement. Or the plan is so long that the key facts are buried.

The plan also has to match what you say at the interview. Officers often ask about projected revenue, first hires, or the main competitor. If your answers drift from the document, the officer may conclude the plan was written for the visa rather than for the business. Read it, know it, and be ready to explain any number in it.
