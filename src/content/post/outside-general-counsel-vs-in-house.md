For most small and midsize companies, outside general counsel is the better fit until the volume and type of legal work justify a full-time lawyer every day. An in-house general counsel gives you constant presence and deep knowledge of the business. An outside general counsel gives you a lawyer who knows your company plus access to specialists in tax, employment, immigration, IP, and litigation, without a full-time salary and benefits. The choice is not permanent, and many companies end up using both.

## What each model actually is

An in-house general counsel is an employee. They sit in leadership meetings, review contracts as they arrive, and know the history behind every major decision. They also need support. Most in-house lawyers still hire outside firms for litigation, transactions, and specialized areas, so the in-house salary is added to outside legal spend rather than replacing all of it.

An outside general counsel is a law firm or attorney engaged as the company's primary legal advisor on an ongoing basis, usually under a monthly arrangement with a defined scope. The difference from hiring a lawyer project by project is continuity: the same team learns your contracts, people, and risk tolerance, and handles questions as they come up. We describe the day-to-day work in our post on [what an outside general counsel does](/what-outside-general-counsel-does).

## Outside general counsel vs in-house: cost and coverage

The in-house cost is mostly fixed: salary, bonus, equity, benefits, and the tools and staff a lawyer needs. It makes sense when the lawyer will be busy every week with work that does not require a specialist.

The outside cost is variable or capped by agreement. You pay for the work and, under a retainer, for availability. The tradeoff is that an outside lawyer is not in the building, so you have to bring issues to them. Companies that get the most from outside counsel build the habit of a quick call before signing, hiring, or launching, not after.

Coverage differs too. One in-house lawyer, however capable, cannot be an expert in employment law, data privacy, securities, commercial leases, and immigration at the same time. An outside general counsel can coordinate specialists within the firm, so the company gets the right expertise on each issue without onboarding a new firm every time.

## Privilege and independence

Communications with in-house counsel can be privileged, but only when the lawyer is acting as a lawyer. In-house counsel who also hold business roles, such as approving deals or running operations, often find that courts treat their communications as business advice, which is not protected. An outside lawyer's role is usually clearer, and that clarity can help in a later dispute.

Independence is a structural point, not a criticism. An employee lawyer reports to the CEO they may need to push back on. Outside counsel can deliver unwelcome advice with less career risk. Many in-house lawyers are fiercely independent, but for compliance-sensitive businesses, the reporting line is worth thinking through.

## Signs you are ready for an in-house lawyer

Consider an in-house hire when several of these are true:

- A steady flow of contracts needs review every week, and turnaround time is slowing sales.
- Your industry is heavily regulated and requires daily judgment calls, as in health care, financial services, or government contracting.
- You are preparing for an IPO or already file reports with the SEC.
- Your outside legal bills are consistently high for work a generalist could handle.
- Leadership wants a lawyer at the table for every major decision, not on call.

Even then, the first in-house hire often works best alongside an outside firm that continues to handle litigation, specialized transactions, and overflow.

## How to decide

List your legal work over the past year: contracts, employment issues, disputes, regulatory questions, financing, and corporate housekeeping. Sort it by frequency and by how specialized it was. If most of it is periodic and varied, outside general counsel fits. If most of it is daily and repetitive, an in-house hire may pay for itself.
