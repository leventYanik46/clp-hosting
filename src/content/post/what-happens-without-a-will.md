When someone dies without a will, the state does not take the property, but it does take the decisions. A statute called the intestacy law names the heirs and their shares, a court appoints whoever administers the estate, and a judge, not the parent, chooses who raises any minor children. The outcome is often close to what the person would have wanted and sometimes nothing like it. This post explains what happens if you die without a will in Virginia, the District of Columbia, and New York, and which parts of your estate the rules do not reach.

## The intestacy statute decides who inherits when you die without a will

Each state sets a fixed order of heirs. The spouse and children come first, then parents, then siblings and their descendants, then more distant relatives. The shares differ by state in ways that surprise people.

In Virginia, a surviving spouse takes the entire estate unless the decedent left children or descendants who are not also the spouse's, in which case the spouse takes one-third and those descendants share two-thirds. In New York, a spouse with children takes a fixed statutory amount plus half of the balance, and the children share the rest. In the District of Columbia, the spouse takes a fractional share when the decedent is survived by descendants or parents, and the whole estate only when there are neither. A blended family in any of these jurisdictions can end up with the surviving spouse owning a house jointly with stepchildren.

The statute recognizes only legal relationships. An unmarried partner inherits nothing, regardless of how long the couple lived together. A stepchild who was never adopted inherits nothing. A child born outside marriage inherits from the father only if paternity is established under the state's rules. Friends, charities, and caregivers are not on the list. A will is the only way to provide for any of them, which is the point our [wills and trusts team](/practice-area/key-services/wills-trusts-probate-administration) makes to every client who assumes the default rules will handle it.

## A court appoints the administrator

With no will, no one has been nominated to run the estate. An interested person must petition the court to be appointed administrator, and state law sets the priority: usually the surviving spouse, then children or other heirs. If relatives disagree about who should serve, the court holds a hearing. The administrator usually must post a bond with surety, which a will can waive but which the court has only limited power to waive in an intestate estate. The estate then moves through the same sequence of notice, inventory, creditor claims, taxes, and distribution described in our post on [how the probate process works](/how-probate-works-step-by-step), with the intestacy shares substituted for the terms of a will. Intestate estates are rarely simpler than testate ones and are often harder, because the family has to agree on everything the will would have settled.

## A judge chooses the guardian for minor children

If both parents are gone, a court appoints a guardian for the children. The judge considers the best interests of the child and will hear from relatives who want the role, but with no written nomination from the parents there is no presumption in anyone's favor. Grandparents on both sides, aunts, and uncles can all petition, and the court may appoint someone the parents would never have chosen. The children's inheritance also comes under court supervision. A minor cannot receive property outright, so the court appoints a guardian of the estate or a conservator, who must account to the court until the child turns eighteen, at which point the child receives everything with no strings attached. A will can nominate the guardian and leave the inheritance in a trust that pays out at a more sensible age. The [legal guardianship](/practice-area/key-services/legal-guardianship) process still applies, but the parents' written choice carries real weight with the court.

## What intestacy does not control

Intestacy only governs probate assets: property in the decedent's sole name with no beneficiary designation. Life insurance, retirement accounts, payable-on-death and transfer-on-death accounts, and jointly owned property with right of survivorship pass to the named beneficiary or surviving owner under their own terms. For many people these are the largest assets, which means the intestacy statute may control the house and the car while the retirement account goes wherever an old beneficiary form sends it. The result can be lopsided: one child receives the account by designation and then also shares the probate estate equally with siblings.

## Small estates and partial intestacy

All three jurisdictions offer simplified procedures for small estates below a statutory value, allowing heirs to collect assets by affidavit or through an abbreviated court filing rather than full administration. The thresholds and procedures differ, so check the current rules before assuming an estate qualifies. Partial intestacy is also common: a will that disposes of some assets but omits a residuary clause, or names a beneficiary who died first with no alternate, leaves the uncovered property to pass under the intestacy statute. A poorly drafted will can therefore produce the same uncertainty as no will at all.

## What dying without a will actually costs

Dying without a will does not usually mean the family loses the estate. It means they lose control over who receives it, who manages it, who raises the children, and when the children get their money. It also tends to cost more: bond premiums, contested appointments, and the inefficiency of a family negotiating every decision through a court.
