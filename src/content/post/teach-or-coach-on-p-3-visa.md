Yes. The statute and regulations describe the P-3 beneficiary as an artist or entertainer coming to perform, teach, or coach under a program that is culturally unique. A master teacher of a traditional dance form can qualify for P-3 to run a university residency without ever giving a ticketed performance. Sponsors often overlook this because the category is marketed as a performer visa, but for cultural centers, universities, and community organizations that want instruction rather than a show, the ability to teach or coach on a P-3 visa is the reason the category fits at all.

## What an instructional P-3 program looks like

The program must still be culturally unique: the method, style, or medium being taught must belong to a particular country, ethnicity, religion, tribe, or other group, and the beneficiary must be an authentic practitioner of it. Typical examples we see include a workshop series on a regional percussion tradition at a conservatory, a semester-long residency teaching a classical dance form at a university, a summer intensive on a traditional textile or instrument-making craft at a folk school, and ongoing coaching of a community ensemble that maintains a diaspora musical tradition.

The program can be commercial or noncommercial. A paid intensive at a private studio and a free community class funded by a grant can both qualify. What matters is that every session under the petition is culturally unique instruction, which our [P-3 visa](/practice-area/key-services/p-3-visa) team confirms against the syllabus or curriculum before filing. The underlying standard is explained in [what counts as culturally unique](/what-counts-as-culturally-unique-p-3-visa).

## Who can sponsor a teacher or coach

The petitioner must be a U.S. employer, sponsoring organization, or agent. For instruction, the natural petitioner is the school, cultural institution, nonprofit, or studio hosting the program. A university department can file directly. A community organization without payroll experience can still petition, but it must be able to document the terms of the engagement and pay the beneficiary as agreed, and it should expect USCIS to look at whether the organization is real and able to carry out the program.

If the teacher will work for several institutions, one of them or a U.S. agent files with an itinerary covering each location and the contract or summary of terms for each. The filing sequence is the same as for a performing tour: union consultation, Form I-129 with the P supplement, USCIS decision, consular interview, and admission for the length of the program.

## Evidence that is specific to teaching

The core evidence does not change: expert letters attesting to the authenticity of the beneficiary's skill in teaching or coaching the form, published material about the tradition, and proof that every event is culturally unique. For an instructional program, three additions make the petition stronger.

First, a curriculum or syllabus describing what will be taught, session by session, with the traditional content identified. Second, evidence of the beneficiary's own training lineage and teaching history, because expert letters about teaching authenticity are more persuasive when they can point to who taught the beneficiary and whom the beneficiary has taught. Third, a letter from the host institution explaining why this instruction is culturally unique and why it needs this teacher. Expert letters should address teaching specifically; the regulation's language about presenting, coaching, or teaching exists so that the authenticity standard can be met in those terms. Our post on [P-3 evidence and expert letters](/p-3-visa-evidence-expert-letters) covers the structure.

## Limits to keep in mind

A P-3 teacher may only perform the instructional work described in the petition for the petitioner or the employers on the itinerary. Adding a new school mid-year requires an amended petition or a new one. The period of stay follows the program, up to one year at a time, with extensions available to continue it. A P-3 teacher who is later offered a permanent faculty position is in a different conversation: that may call for an H-1B through a cap-exempt university, an O-1B, or an immigrant petition, and the change should be planned rather than improvised. The [talent and extraordinary ability visa overview](/practice-area/key-services/talent-and-extraordinary-ability-visas) describes the longer-term options.

Two situations do not fit P-3 teaching. Instruction in an international art form, such as classical piano or ballet, fails the culturally unique test regardless of the teacher's eminence; that is an O-1B or H-1B question. And a short, unpaid guest lecture with only expense reimbursement may be permissible on a visitor visa, but regular, paid teaching is not, and the line is easy to cross. Our post on [what B-1 and B-2 visitors can and cannot do](/b1-b2-visas-in-the-united-states-what-you-can-and-cannot-do) sets out the limits.

## A note on family and support staff

A P-3 teacher's spouse and unmarried children under 21 may accompany them in P-4 status, which permits study but not employment. Essential support personnel, such as an accompanist or an assistant whose skills are integral to the instruction and not readily available in the United States, can be petitioned separately as P-3S with their own consultation and a statement of why they are essential.
