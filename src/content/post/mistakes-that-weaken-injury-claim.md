Most personal injury claim mistakes happen in the first two weeks, before anyone has talked to a lawyer, and most of them are made by people acting in good faith. You feel fine so you skip the doctor. The adjuster sounds friendly so you give a statement. You post a photo to reassure your family. Each one hands the insurer an argument it will use months later. This post lists the errors we see most often in Virginia, DC, and New York cases, what each one costs, and the simple alternative.

## Waiting to see a doctor, or stopping too soon

The insurer reads your medical records as a timeline. A gap between the injury and the first visit is read as evidence that the injury was minor or came from something else. A gap in the middle of treatment is read as evidence that you recovered. Adrenaline hides symptoms for a day or two, and many soft-tissue and concussion injuries get worse before they get better, so get examined within a day or two even if you feel mostly fine. Then follow the plan. If you cannot afford a visit, say so, because medical payments coverage, health insurance, and no-fault benefits in New York often cover early care. Our [personal injury team](/practice-area/personal-injury-law) cannot repair a treatment gap after the fact; only consistent care prevents it.

## Giving a recorded statement to the other side's insurer

The other driver's adjuster will call quickly, often within days, and ask for a recorded statement "to process the claim." You have no obligation to give one. The questions are designed to lock you into details before you know the full extent of your injuries and to find anything that suggests shared fault, which in Virginia and DC can bar recovery entirely. Your own insurer is different: your policy requires cooperation, and you should report the accident promptly. Even then, stick to facts you know and do not guess at speed, distance, or what you might have done differently.

## Posting about the accident or your recovery

Insurers and defense lawyers review public social media as a matter of routine, and in litigation they can request private content through discovery. A photo of you at a barbecue becomes proof that you are not in pain. A comment that you are "doing great" becomes a quote at deposition. The safe practice is to stop posting about your activities, your injury, and the case until it resolves, and to ask friends and family not to tag you. Do not delete existing posts after a claim is pending; deleting evidence creates a worse problem than the post.

## Signing before you understand what you are signing

Two documents cause the most damage. The first is a blanket medical authorization that lets the insurer pull your entire medical history, which it will then search for prior complaints. Authorizations should be limited to records relevant to the injury and a reasonable time window. The second is a release. An early settlement offer comes with a release that ends your claim permanently, including for injuries that have not yet been diagnosed. If a surgeon tells you a month later that you need a procedure, the release still stands. Do not sign a release until you have reached maximum medical improvement or your doctor can reliably project future treatment.

## Missing a deadline you did not know existed

The general statute of limitations is two years in Virginia and three years in DC and New York, but claims against government bodies require notice within months, New York no-fault benefits must be applied for within 30 days, and insurance policies have their own notice provisions. Negotiating with an adjuster does not pause any of these. Our post on [how long you have to file a personal injury claim](/injury-claim-deadlines-dc-virginia-new-york) walks through the deadlines that most often catch people.

## Letting evidence disappear

Surveillance footage is overwritten, often within days or weeks. Vehicles are repaired or totaled. Skid marks fade. Witnesses move. A preservation letter to the business, the trucking company, or the property owner, sent early, puts them on notice to keep the evidence and makes its destruction a problem for them rather than you. Keep your own evidence too: the damaged helmet, the shoes you slipped in, the photos on your phone, and a daily note on pain and limitations.

- Photograph the scene, the hazard, and your injuries as they change.
- Keep every bill, receipt, and explanation of benefits in one folder.
- Write down witness names and contact details the same day.
- Save texts and emails with insurers rather than relying on phone calls.

## Handling a car crash claim without knowing the coverage

Crash cases add a layer of insurance mistakes: assuming the at-fault driver's policy is the only source of recovery, overlooking your own uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage, or settling with the liability carrier without notifying your underinsured carrier first, which can forfeit that coverage. Our post on [how a car accident insurance claim works](/car-accident-insurance-claim-process) explains the sequence, and our [car, truck, and motorcycle accident](/practice-area/key-services/car-truck-and-motorcycle-accidents) page describes the coverage questions we review in every crash case.

None of these mistakes is fatal on its own, but each shifts the advantage to the insurer, and in a contributory negligence jurisdiction a small shift can be the whole case.
