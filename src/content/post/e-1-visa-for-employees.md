An E-1 visa for employees is available, and for many treaty trading companies it is the main reason to use the E-1 at all. Our [E-1 company and employee visa](/practice-area/key-services/e-1-visa) page covers the company side of the case. Once the company itself qualifies as a treaty trader, it can bring staff to its U.S. operation in E-1 status. Three conditions apply. The employee must have the same treaty nationality as the company. The employee must fill an executive or supervisory role, or have skills essential to the U.S. operation. And the employee cannot be a U.S. citizen or green card holder. Unlike the L-1, there is no requirement that the employee worked for the company abroad first.

## The employer has to qualify first

An employee's E-1 rests entirely on the employer's status. The company must be at least 50 percent owned by nationals of the treaty country, and those owners must live outside the United States or hold E status here. It must carry on substantial trade, and more than half of its international trade must run between the United States and the treaty country.

If the company has not yet been reviewed by a consulate, the first employee's application usually doubles as the company's own case. At posts that register E companies separately, the company goes first and the employees follow. Our overview of the [E-1 treaty trader visa requirements](/e-1-treaty-trader-visa-requirements) covers the company-level tests in detail.

## The nationality match

The employee must hold the same nationality as the company. A Japanese trading company can bring Japanese staff in E-1 status; it cannot use the E-1 for a Brazilian engineer, however senior, because the E-1 rests on the treaty between the United States and Japan. Third-country staff need a different route, such as the H-1B or, if they have worked for an affiliate abroad, the L-1.

Dual nationals can use whichever nationality matches the company. A green card holder cannot hold E-1 status at all, and a naturalized U.S. citizen does not need it. Note, too, that a company's nationality can shift over time. If owners naturalize or sell shares to non-treaty investors, every employee's E-1 status is at risk at the next renewal. We check ownership before each round of filings for that reason.

## Executive and supervisory roles

An executive role gives the employee broad control over the U.S. company or a major part of it, with authority to set policy and direct the operation. A supervisory role gives responsibility for a significant portion of the operations, usually through managing skilled staff or a key function.

Officers look past the job title. They consider where the position sits in the organizational chart, how many people report to it and what those people do, how much the position pays, and how much independent judgment it carries. A first-line supervisor of unskilled workers is not usually enough. A country manager, a regional sales director with a team, or a head of logistics running import operations usually is.

## Essential skills employees

An employee who is not an executive or supervisor can still qualify by having skills essential to the efficient operation of the U.S. enterprise. Officers weigh the degree of proven expertise, how unique the skills are, how long it takes to acquire them, how long it would take to train a U.S. worker to do the job, and the salary the skills command.

Essential skills cases often arise when a company opens or expands its U.S. operation and needs people who know its products, systems, or quality standards. In that start-up phase, the officer may view the employee's role as time-limited and expect U.S. staff to be trained to take over. Other roles stay essential long term, such as technicians who service proprietary equipment. Either way, the company's letter should explain concretely why a U.S. hire could not do the job now.

## How E-1 visas for employees are filed and renewed

The employee applies like any E-1 applicant: a DS-160, the DS-156E signed by the company, the company's evidence, and an employer letter describing the role. Employees already in the United States in valid status can instead change status through USCIS on Form I-129. Our guide on [how to apply for an E-1 visa](/how-to-apply-e-1-visa) walks through the sequence.

Each admission lasts two years and can be extended in two-year increments. The employee's spouse and unmarried children under 21 can come as dependents and do not need to share the nationality. The spouse is employment authorized incident to status.

E-1 status ties the employee to the treaty company. Working for an unrelated U.S. employer is not allowed. Substantive changes, such as a merger or a sale that alters the company's ownership, can require a new filing with USCIS before the employee continues in E-1 status, so build immigration review into any corporate transaction.

## E-1 employee or L-1 transferee

The E-1 has two real advantages over the [L-1A intracompany transfer visa](/practice-area/key-services/l-1a-visa): no prior year of employment abroad is required, and the company does not need a qualifying affiliate abroad. The L-1 has its own advantages: no treaty nationality requirement, and a clearer path to an EB-1C green card for managers. Our [L-1 transfer guide](/l-1-visa-guide-transfers) covers that route.
