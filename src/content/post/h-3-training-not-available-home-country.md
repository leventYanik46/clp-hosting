Training is "not available in the home country" for H-3 purposes when the petitioner can show, with evidence rather than assertion, that the specific skills, methods, or systems to be taught cannot be obtained in the trainee's own country, and that the trainee needs to be in the United States to learn them. USCIS does not accept that a company's training is unique simply because the company says so. It also does not accept that training is unavailable abroad when the trainee could learn the same thing at a university, a competitor, or the petitioner's own foreign affiliate. The question the officer asks is narrow: why here, and why not there?

## The four requirements, and where this one sits

An H-3 trainee petition on Form I-129 must satisfy four regulatory requirements. The training must not be available in the beneficiary's own country. The beneficiary must not be placed in a position that is in the normal operation of the business and in which U.S. citizens and resident workers are regularly employed. The beneficiary must not engage in productive employment unless it is incidental and necessary to the training. And the training must benefit the beneficiary in pursuing a career outside the United States.

The first and fourth requirements are linked. If the skill is useful only in the United States, USCIS will conclude the program is really a staffing device, and the regulation separately bars programs in fields where the knowledge is unlikely to be used abroad. Our [H-3 trainee visa](/practice-area/key-services/h-3-visa) page sets out how we build the petition around all four requirements at once, because an officer who finds the training is available abroad will usually also find the position is productive employment, and the two findings reinforce each other in a denial.

## Evidence that persuades

The strongest files show unavailability at three levels: the petitioner, the industry, and the country.

- Petitioner level: proof that the training involves proprietary systems, equipment, software, or methodologies that exist only at the U.S. facility. Include technical descriptions, internal manuals (redacted as needed), and an explanation of why the foreign affiliate or future employer abroad does not have them.
- Industry level: a statement, ideally from an independent expert or trade body, that the training is not offered by educational institutions or employers in the home country. Name the institutions that were considered and explain why each does not deliver it.
- Country level: market or regulatory facts showing the technology, practice, or standard has not yet been adopted there, which also explains why the trainee will be valuable at home afterward.

Letters from the trainee's foreign employer are valuable when they state that the employer will place the trainee in a defined role on return and that no local provider could deliver the training. If the petitioner is a U.S. company with a foreign subsidiary, the subsidiary's letter should say the same and identify the position. A vague commitment to "consider opportunities" does not meet the career-abroad requirement.

## What does not work

Several patterns produce Requests for Evidence or denials with regularity:

1. Training in generic business skills (sales technique, customer service, general management) that any large employer in the home country teaches.
2. Training for a trainee who already holds substantial expertise in the field. The regulation bars programs for someone who already possesses the training, so a ten-year engineer cannot be an H-3 trainee in engineering fundamentals.
3. Training in a field where the knowledge is unlikely to be used outside the United States, such as U.S.-specific regulatory compliance for a company with no foreign operations.
4. Programs that describe the training only in terms of the trainee's output, because that shows productive employment rather than instruction.
5. Petitions that are silent on why the foreign affiliate cannot run the program, when the petitioner has one.

A petitioner that cannot answer the "why not there" question honestly should consider whether the trainee fits a different category. For a comparison of the H-3 with the sponsor-based alternative, see our post on [H-3 versus J-1 trainee programs](/h-3-visa-vs-j-1-trainee), and for cases where the foreign affiliate will later send the person back as a manager, the [L-1 intracompany transfer guide](/l-1-visa-guide-transfers) describes how a year abroad after training can set up that petition.

## Medical and nursing training

Physicians cannot use the H-3 for graduate medical education or training; residencies and fellowships fall under the [J-1 exchange visitor visa](/practice-area/key-services/j-1-visa) and, in limited cases, the H-1B. Narrow exceptions exist for medical students on externships during school vacations and for foreign nurses, each with specific requirements that include evidence the nurse is fully qualified under home-country law and a statement from the petitioner about the need for the training. These petitions are scrutinized closely and should not be attempted on a general H-3 template. Our overview of [how healthcare professionals can work in the United States](/how-healthcare-professionals-can-work-in-the-u-s) covers the categories that fit clinical roles.

## Showing the career abroad

The fourth requirement is proved with the same evidence as the first, viewed from the other side. Show the job the trainee will hold at home, the employer who will provide it, and how each component of the training maps to that role. Where the trainee is self-employed or will join a family business, provide business registration documents and a plan. If the trainee has no identifiable career abroad, the petition will fail regardless of how good the training is, and the trainee may be better served by a category that does not require departure, which we discuss in [what happens after H-3 training ends](/after-h-3-training-ends-options).
