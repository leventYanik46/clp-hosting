An as-is clause in a real estate contract means the seller is delivering the property in its current condition and will not repair, credit, or warrant anything, and the buyer accepts that condition at closing. It does not mean the buyer has given up the right to inspect, and it does not shield a seller who conceals a defect or answers a disclosure form falsely. The clause allocates the risk of unknown problems to the buyer; it does not license hiding known ones.

## What the clause does

The clause does three things. It removes any obligation on the seller to make repairs or deliver the property in a particular condition beyond what the contract states elsewhere. It disclaims implied warranties about condition, fitness, or habitability that might otherwise attach, with the caveat that some warranties on new construction cannot be fully disclaimed. And it records that the buyer is relying on their own inspection rather than on anything the seller or the agent said. In exchange the buyer usually gets a lower price or a faster deal. Estate sales, bank-owned property, investor flips, and nearly all commercial sales use the clause, and our [real estate contracts](/practice-area/key-services/real-estate-contracts-and-negotiations) practice sees it in an increasing share of ordinary residential contracts as well.

## What it cannot do

No as-is clause protects a seller from a claim for fraud, intentional misrepresentation, or active concealment. A seller who knows the basement floods and paints over the water line, or who says "no" to a direct question about foundation movement after receiving an engineer's report, has made a misrepresentation that the clause does not cure. The same is true of statutory disclosure duties: the federal lead paint disclosure for pre-1978 housing, New York's Property Condition Disclosure Statement, the District's seller's disclosure form, and Virginia's narrower required disclosures all apply regardless of an as-is sale. Our summary of [what a seller must disclose](/seller-disclosure-requirements-home-sale) covers each jurisdiction. The practical rule for sellers is that the clause works best when paired with honest written disclosure: a buyer who was told about the roof and bought as-is anyway has no claim, while a buyer who was not told may.

## As-is with an inspection contingency

The clause and the inspection contingency coexist in most residential contracts. "As-is" describes the seller's obligations; the inspection contingency describes the buyer's exit. A buyer can inspect, learn that the furnace is at the end of its life, and terminate with the deposit, even though the seller never promised to fix it. What the buyer usually cannot do under a pure as-is contract is force a repair negotiation, since the seller has already said no. Many sellers will still entertain a credit to keep the deal, and our note on [negotiating repairs after an inspection](/negotiating-repairs-after-home-inspection) explains how to ask when the contract gives you no right to demand. A buyer who signs an as-is contract with the inspection contingency struck has given up both protections and should price the risk accordingly.

## Commercial as-is clauses: disclaimers, releases, and survival

In a commercial purchase agreement the as-is clause is a full section rather than a sentence. The buyer acknowledges that it is sophisticated, has had the opportunity to conduct its own due diligence, is not relying on any statement by the seller outside the express representations in the agreement, and accepts the property "as is, where is, and with all faults." A release follows, under which the buyer waives claims against the seller relating to the property's condition, often including environmental conditions, for all time. The counterweight is the set of express representations and warranties the seller does make, which survive closing for a negotiated period and are typically subject to a cap on liability and a minimum threshold before any claim can be brought. The negotiation is over what goes into that express list: title and authority, leases and rent roll, pending litigation, notices of violations, environmental reports in the seller's possession, and the seller's actual knowledge of defects. Our [commercial property due diligence checklist](/commercial-property-due-diligence-checklist) shows what the buyer should verify independently, since the clause makes that verification the buyer's only protection for everything not expressly represented.

## How to negotiate the clause

A buyer should keep the inspection or study period intact, add carve-outs from the release for fraud and for breach of the express representations, require the seller to deliver all reports, permits, and notices in its possession, and ask for a representation that the seller has no actual knowledge of undisclosed material defects. A seller should obtain a clear acknowledgment that the buyer had the opportunity to inspect, disclose known problems in writing so the clause can do its work, and resist knowledge representations that are broader than what the seller's principals can truthfully confirm. Both sides benefit from a defined survival period and a defined cap, because an open-ended release and an open-ended warranty are each likely to produce litigation rather than certainty.
