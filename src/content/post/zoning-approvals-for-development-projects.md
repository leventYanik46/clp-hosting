A lender will not fund and a contractor cannot pull permits until the project has the legal right to exist on that parcel, and that right comes from zoning. The first question for any development site is whether the intended use and density are permitted by right. If they are, the path is administrative: site plan review and building permits. If they are not, you need discretionary zoning approvals, and discretionary means public hearings, conditions, neighbors, and time. Knowing which path you are on before you sign the land contract is the difference between a schedule and a guess.

## Step one: what the zoning allows by right

Start with the zoning map and the ordinance text for the district. The use table tells you whether your use (residential, office, retail, industrial, mixed) is permitted, permitted with conditions, or prohibited. The dimensional standards set height, density or floor area ratio, setbacks, lot coverage, and parking. Overlay districts add rules on top: historic districts bring design review, and in the District a project in a historic district goes through the historic preservation review process before permits. A property with a nonconforming existing use may continue it but usually cannot expand it. A zoning verification letter from the jurisdiction confirms the classification in writing, and our [development and acquisitions](/practice-area/key-services/property-acquisitions-leasing-development) team obtains one during the study period on every project site, because the broker's description of what the land allows is not an approval.

## Special exceptions, special use permits, and variances

When the use is allowed only conditionally, the ordinance provides a mechanism for case-by-case approval. Virginia localities call it a special use permit or special exception, decided by the board of supervisors or the board of zoning appeals depending on the locality; the District's Board of Zoning Adjustment hears special exceptions; New York City's Board of Standards and Appeals hears special permits and variances. The applicant must show that the proposal meets the standards written in the ordinance, and the board can attach conditions on hours, traffic, buffering, and design.

A variance is different and harder. It asks permission to depart from a dimensional rule because of a hardship arising from the property itself, such as an irregular lot that cannot meet a setback. Self-created hardship and financial inconvenience generally do not qualify. Projects that need a variance to work should price in the real possibility of denial.

## Rezoning, proffers, and planned developments

If the use or density is simply not allowed, the fix is a rezoning (a map amendment) or a text amendment, both of which are legislative acts by the local governing body. Virginia practice allows the applicant to offer proffers, written conditions that become part of the zoning and bind the land, covering matters like road improvements, design, and contributions toward public facilities. The District's planned unit development process, before the Zoning Commission, allows greater density and flexibility in exchange for public benefits negotiated in the application. In New York City, zoning map and text changes run through the Uniform Land Use Review Procedure, with review by the community board, the borough president, the City Planning Commission, and the City Council on a fixed clock. Each of these processes is public, each invites opposition, and each produces conditions that will govern the project for its life.

## Site plan, subdivision, and building permits

Entitlement is not the end. A by-right or approved project still goes through site plan review, where engineers at the locality examine grading, stormwater management, utilities, traffic, and landscaping, and may require bonds for public improvements. If the parcel must be divided or combined, a subdivision plat is recorded first. Building permits follow, with plan review for structural, fire, mechanical, and accessibility compliance, and a certificate of occupancy comes only after final inspections. Lenders typically require the site plan approval and building permit as conditions to the construction loan.

## Tying the approvals to the land contract

The land purchase should be structured around the approval path. A buyer who needs a rezoning should negotiate a study period long enough to file and obtain it, extension rights with a defined price, and a termination right if the approval is denied or comes with unacceptable conditions. Some contracts adjust the purchase price to the approved density. The contract should also address who controls the application, who pays for consultants, and what happens to work product if the deal dies. Our [contract and negotiation](/practice-area/key-services/real-estate-contracts-and-negotiations) team drafts these entitlement contingencies into the agreement, and our note on [purchase contract contingencies](/real-estate-contract-contingencies) explains how such conditions interact with the deposit.

Two more points apply across jurisdictions. Approvals carry expiration dates, so track when a special exception or site plan lapses if construction does not start. And neighbors and other parties have appeal rights, which means an approval is not final until the appeal period runs; a construction lender will usually wait for that date. Build both into the schedule, alongside the rest of the [due diligence for the acquisition](/commercial-property-due-diligence-checklist), and the project's timeline becomes something you can defend to a lender and an investor.
