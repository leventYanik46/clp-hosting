You can stay in the United States on an L-1B visa for a maximum of five years. That total includes the initial approval and every extension, and it also counts any time you spent in H-1B or other H status. The L-1B five-year limit is shorter than the seven years available to L-1A managers and executives, so specialized knowledge employees need a plan for year five well before it arrives. The good news is that the clock is more flexible than it looks: time outside the United States can be added back, and some patterns of work do not count at all.

## How the L-1B clock is built

A first L-1B petition for an established U.S. office is approved for up to three years. If the U.S. entity is a new office, doing business for less than a year, the first approval is limited to one year. Extensions come in increments of up to two years, until the employee reaches the five-year total. A typical path is three years, then a two-year extension, but a new office path might be one year, then two, then two.

The extension is filed on Form I-129 with the L Supplement before the current status expires. A timely filed extension lets the employee keep working for the same employer while USCIS decides it, under the 240-day rule for pending extensions. Premium processing is available for L-1 extensions if the company needs a decision on a fixed timeline; check the USCIS website for the current fee and timeframe. ## What counts toward the five years

The regulation counts time spent in the United States in L status and in H status together. An engineer who spent two years on an H-1B with a U.S. employer and then moved to L-1B with a different company has three L-1B years left, not five. The reverse is also true: L-1B time reduces the remaining H-1B time.

Time spent outside the United States does not count, and it can be recaptured. Business trips, vacations and assignments abroad all qualify, as long as they are documented. Days outside the country are added back to the remaining balance on the next extension petition. Keep a travel log with passport stamps, I-94 records and boarding passes from the start; reconstructing five years of travel at the last minute is slow and error-prone.

## Exceptions to the limit

The limit does not apply to employees whose L-1 work in the United States is seasonal, intermittent, or for an aggregate of six months or less per year. It also does not apply to employees who live abroad and regularly commute to the United States to work part-time. A specialist based in Toronto who spends two months a year at the U.S. affiliate may never touch the five-year cap. The burden is on the employee and the employer to prove the pattern, usually with arrival and departure records, tax returns and payroll records from abroad.

## What happens at year five

When the five years are used up, the employee generally must live and be physically present outside the United States for one continuous year before qualifying again for L or H status. Brief visits for business or pleasure during that year do not reset the clock, but they also do not count toward the year. For most families, that one-year gap is a real disruption, which is why the plan has to start earlier.

The main options, in rough order of how early they need to start:

- File a green card case. L-1 is a dual intent status, so a PERM labor certification or an EB-2 national interest waiver petition does not undermine the L-1B. Our guide to [L-1B green card options](/l-1b-to-green-card-options) covers each route.
- Move to an L-1A role. An L-1B employee who is promoted to a managerial or executive position can file for L-1A. If the employee holds L-1A status for at least six months, the overall limit becomes seven years.
- Change to another status, such as an H-1B (subject to the cap and the shared H and L clock) or an O-1, which has no fixed maximum.
- Return abroad for the one-year period and come back on a new L-1 petition.

## Planning around the clock

The timing problem is real: a PERM-based green card often takes longer than the time an L-1B employee has left, especially for employees born in countries with long Visa Bulletin backlogs. Some benefits under AC21 help, such as extensions of H-1B status beyond six years based on a pending labor certification, but those extensions are tied to H-1B status and do not extend the L-1B limit. An employee who needs that protection may have to move to H-1B before the L-1B runs out, which requires a cap-subject registration or a cap-exempt employer.

If you are an employer, start the green card conversation at the first extension, not the second. If you are the employee, ask for the plan in writing.
