An asylee can apply for a green card after one year of physical presence in the United States following the grant of asylum. That sentence hides three questions people get wrong: which date starts the year, whether time spent abroad counts, and when a spouse or child files. It also hides a fourth, because the law sets no deadline for asylee adjustment, and many people read no deadline as no hurry. Our [green card through asylum](/practice-area/key-services/green-card-through-asylum) practice handles Form I-485 for principal and derivative asylees, and this post answers all four questions in the order they come up.

## The asylee green card one-year rule

The statute, INA 209(b), lets USCIS adjust the status of an asylee who has been physically present in the United States for at least one year after being granted asylum. The year starts on the date of the grant. For an affirmative case that is the date on the USCIS approval letter, not the date of a recommended approval. For a case won in immigration court it is the date of the judge's order; if the government appealed that order, ask counsel which date controls before you count, because the grant must be final.

Physical presence means days inside the country. A trip abroad does not reset the clock, but the days you were away do not count toward it, so an asylee who spent six weeks outside the United States during the first year becomes eligible six weeks later than the anniversary. Keep a list of every departure and return with dates; USCIS will ask for it on the I-485, and your own record is more reliable than a reconstruction from passport stamps.

File on or after the day the year is complete. An I-485 filed early is rejected or denied, and the filing fee is not refunded.

## The other requirements USCIS checks

The one-year rule is the gate, but four more conditions apply under INA 209(b) and 8 CFR 209.2. Your asylum must not have been terminated. You must continue to meet the definition of a refugee, or be the spouse or child of someone who does, which is why a return trip to your home country before adjustment is dangerous. You must not have firmly resettled in another country. And you must be admissible, or qualify for a waiver on Form I-602; asylees are exempt from the public charge, labor certification, and entry document grounds, and most other grounds can be waived, which our post on [inadmissibility waivers for asylees](/asylee-inadmissibility-waiver-i-602) explains.

## When derivatives file

Each derivative asylee files a separate Form I-485 after completing their own year. The counting differs by how the derivative received status.

- A spouse or child included on your I-589 and present at the grant counts from the same date you do.
- A relative who came from abroad on an approved Form I-730 counts from the date they were admitted to the United States as an asylee.
- A relative already in the United States whose I-730 was approved counts from the approval date.

Families therefore become eligible in stages, and there is no requirement that everyone file together. A derivative child who turns 21 after the grant does not lose derivative asylee status and can still adjust. Marriage or divorce after the grant raises a harder question, because the regulation asks whether the derivative continues to be the spouse or child of a refugee at the time of adjustment; USCIS has treated derivative status as continuing in many such cases, but the answer turns on timing, so get advice before filing rather than after a denial. A derivative whose relationship to you has ended, or who wants to petition for their own family, can request asylum as a principal, nunc pro tunc, and adjust on that basis.

## Why waiting is a mistake

Asylee status is secure only until something changes. USCIS can terminate asylum for fraud in the original application, for a fundamental change in country conditions, for firm resettlement, or for certain criminal and security grounds, and a terminated asylee can be placed in removal proceedings. A green card ends that exposure. Laws, forms, and fees change, almost never in the applicant's favor. Travel is easier with a green card than with a refugee travel document, as our post on [asylee travel before the green card](/asylee-travel-before-green-card) explains. And the citizenship clock is built on the green card: INA 209(b) backdates an asylee's permanent residence to one year before the approval date, so most asylees can apply for naturalization about four years after approval rather than five. Every year of delay in filing the I-485 is a year of delay in reaching [citizenship](/practice-area/key-services/citizenship-and-naturalization).

The I-730 window is the other clock that runs independently. If a spouse or child abroad was not included in your grant, the petition for them must be filed within two years of the grant, whether or not your green card application is pending.

## How the filing works

The application is Form I-485 with proof of the asylum grant, identity documents, evidence of one year of presence, a complete travel history, the Form I-693 medical examination, two photographs, and either the current USCIS fee or a fee waiver request on Form I-912, for which asylee applicants are eligible as of this writing. Many applicants add Form I-765 for a work card and Form I-131 for a refugee travel document in the same package. USCIS then schedules biometrics, may schedule an interview at the local field office, and issues a decision. The document list, including what to do when a birth certificate or passport is unavailable, is in our [asylee I-485 checklist](/asylee-i-485-documents-checklist). If you won asylum before an immigration judge rather than through an [affirmative application](/practice-area/key-services/affirmative-asylum-applications), the process is the same; only the proof of the grant differs.
