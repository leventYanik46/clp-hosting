A dead trademark in the USPTO database tells you one thing: the application was abandoned or the registration was cancelled or expired. It does not tell you that the owner closed the business, stopped using the name or gave up its rights. Because trademark rights in the United States come from use rather than from the register, a company can hold enforceable common-law rights in a name whose registration died years ago. Treating a dead record as a green light is one of the most common clearance mistakes, and it is one reason a [comprehensive trademark clearance search](/practice-area/key-services/trademark-search-and-clearance) looks past the status field to what the prior owner is actually doing.

## What a dead trademark means in the USPTO database

Every record in the USPTO's trademark search system is marked live or dead. An application goes dead when it is abandoned, usually because the applicant did not respond to an office action, did not file a Statement of Use after a Notice of Allowance, or expressly withdrew it. A registration goes dead when it is cancelled or expires, most often because the owner missed the Section 8 declaration of use due between the fifth and sixth year, or the combined declaration and renewal due every ten years. Registrations are also cancelled by the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board after a successful cancellation petition.

A dead record has one certain consequence: an examining attorney will not cite it against your application. Examiners cite only live registrations and pending applications. That is a real advantage, and it is the beginning of the analysis rather than the end.

## Why a dead record does not mean a free name

Three things can be true of a name whose record is dead. First, the owner may still be using it. Businesses miss maintenance deadlines constantly, and a company that forgot to file its Section 8 declaration still has every common-law right it had the day before. It can oppose your application during publication on the basis of its prior use, and it can sue if your use is likely to cause confusion in its market. Our post on [trademark rights without registration](/trademark-rights-without-registration) explains the scope of those rights.

Second, the death may be temporary. An application abandoned for failure to respond can be revived by petition within two months of the notice of abandonment, and a registrant who missed a maintenance deadline may still be inside the six-month grace period. A record that died last month is not a safe signal.

Third, the owner may already have filed again. Companies that let a registration lapse often file a new application for the same mark, and that new application is live and citable even though the old registration is dead. Search the owner's name, not just the mark.

## How to find out whether the prior owner still uses the mark

Start in the USPTO's records. The status and document history show why the record died and when, and whether the same owner has other live filings. Then leave the register. Visit the owner's website and social accounts, search marketplaces for current listings, check whether the state corporations office shows the entity as active, and look for recent press or reviews. A business that is still trading under the name will usually show up within an hour of searching. If the stakes justify it, a professional investigator can confirm whether the goods are still sold and where.

Measure nonuse against the statutory standard. Under the Lanham Act a mark is abandoned when its use has been discontinued with no intent to resume, and three consecutive years of nonuse is prima facie evidence of abandonment. A mark that has not been used for three years, by an owner that has stopped trading, is a strong candidate for adoption. A mark that disappeared from the register eighteen months ago, by an owner that still has a storefront, is not.

## When a dead mark is usable and how to take it over

If the prior owner is gone, the goods are no longer sold, and the nonuse comfortably exceeds three years, the mark is generally available to adopt. File your own application through a [USPTO trademark registration](/practice-area/key-services/trademark-and-intellectual-property-registration) practice and build your own rights from your own use. Do not try to buy the dead registration; a cancelled registration has nothing to assign. If some goodwill in the name survives and the former owner can be found, the cleaner route is to buy the mark and its goodwill by written assignment and then file a new application in your own name.

Two cautions. Residual goodwill can persist for a while after sales stop, especially for consumer products with loyal buyers, and a court can find that the public still associates the name with the old source. And if your intended use is close to the prior owner's former business, expect the owner, or a successor, to notice when your application publishes.

## Live registrations that look dead

The mirror image problem is a registration that is live on the register but dead in the marketplace. Examiners will cite it against you no matter how long ago the owner stopped using it. The remedies run in the other direction: a petition to cancel for abandonment, or an expungement or reexamination proceeding to remove goods the mark was never or no longer used on. Those tools belong to our [trademark enforcement and TTAB proceedings](/practice-area/key-services/trademark-enforcement-and-ttab-proceedings) practice, and they are often the way to clear a blocking registration that a search turns up. Either way, the status field is where the inquiry begins, not where it ends; our guide to [how to search for a trademark](/how-to-search-for-a-trademark) explains what to look at next.
