The P-2 vs P-1B question usually comes from a manager or presenter who knows a band or ensemble needs a work visa for a U.S. tour and has heard that P visas are for performers. Both are P visas, both use Form I-129, and both need a union consultation. After that they split. P-2 is built on a reciprocal exchange agreement; P-1B is built on the group's international recognition. Which one you can use depends less on how good the group is and more on which set of facts you can document.

## The deciding question: is there an exchange?

Start with P-2. If a U.S. organization has a written reciprocal exchange agreement with an organization in the group's home country, and U.S. artists of comparable skill are going the other way on similar terms, P-2 is usually the simpler route. The evidence is about the agreement and comparability, not about reviews, awards, or chart positions. Our [P-2 exchange visa](/practice-area/key-services/p-2-visa) page explains the agreement requirements in detail.

If there is no exchange, P-2 is off the table. No amount of talent substitutes for the agreement. That sends most touring groups to P-1B.

## What P-1B asks for instead

The [P-1 visa for internationally recognized groups](/practice-area/key-services/p-1-visa) requires that the entertainment group have been recognized internationally as outstanding in the discipline for a sustained and substantial period. USCIS tests that through evidence such as international awards, performances for distinguished organizations, commercial or critical success, recognition from experts, and high pay relative to others in the field. You need to satisfy several of those criteria, not just one, or show nomination for or receipt of a significant international award.

P-1B also has a membership rule that P-2 does not. At least 75 percent of the group's members must have had a sustained and substantial relationship with the group for at least one year, and the group itself must have been established and performing regularly for at least one year. There are limited exceptions, including for circus performers, but a newly assembled touring lineup can fail on this point alone. P-2 has no equivalent requirement because the exchange, not the group's history, is what justifies the classification.

## Comparing the practical burdens

- Evidence: P-2 turns on the exchange agreement, sponsor statement, and comparability proof. P-1B turns on a record of international recognition that must already exist.
- Who controls the outcome: P-2 depends on a willing U.S. sponsoring organization and union concurrence. P-1B depends on the group's own history.
- Group changes: the P-1B one-year rule penalizes new members. P-2 does not.
- Individuals: P-1B covers entertainers only as members of a group, with narrow exceptions. P-2 covers individual artists as well as groups.
- Duration: both are admitted for the time needed to complete the engagement, up to one year at a time for entertainers, with extensions available. Longer initial periods exist for P-1 athletes, but that does not help musicians or actors.

## When neither is right

A soloist with major recognition and no exchange may be an O-1B case. The standard is distinction in the arts, and the evidence looks like a P-1B package for one person; our post on [proving extraordinary ability for an O-1](/how-to-prove-extraordinary-ability-o-1) shows what that record looks like. A traditional or folk group performing a culturally unique program should look at [P-3](/practice-area/key-services/p-3-visa), which replaces international recognition with expert testimony about authenticity. And a group with no U.S. payment beyond expenses performing at a noncommercial event may fit a visitor admission, though the limits are tight and easy to breach.

## A practical way to decide

Ask three questions in order. First, is there a reciprocal exchange agreement, or can one realistically be put in place with a willing U.S. partner and union concurrence before the tour? If yes, P-2. Second, has the group existed for a year with a stable lineup and a documented international reputation? If yes, P-1B. Third, if the answer to both is no, is the program culturally unique, or is there an individual whose record supports O-1B?

The order matters because P-2 is the least evidence-heavy route when it is available, and sponsors sometimes overlook it in favor of a harder P-1B filing. The P-2 side of that decision, including who qualifies and who can file, is set out in [who qualifies for a P-2 visa](/p-2-visa-reciprocal-exchange-requirements). One more consideration: a P-1B approval travels with the group's reputation and can be renewed for future tours without a partner organization, while every P-2 engagement depends on an exchange partner being willing and able to send U.S. artists abroad. For a group that expects to tour the United States regularly, building the P-1B record over time is worth the effort even if P-2 is the right answer for the first trip.
