A program qualifies for an M-1 visa when two things are true: the school is certified by the Student and Exchange Visitor Program (SEVP) to enroll M-1 students, and the program is vocational or nonacademic and meets the regulatory definition of a full course of study. That second test has numbers attached. At a community college or a postsecondary vocational or business school, you need at least 12 semester or quarter hours per term. In a program measured in clock hours, you need at least 18 hours per week if the instruction is mostly classroom, or 22 hours per week if it is mostly shop or laboratory work. A school that issues you a Form I-20 for M-1 status is certifying that its program meets these thresholds.

## The SEVP certification test

No matter how good the school, if it is not SEVP-certified it cannot issue a Form I-20 and you cannot get a student visa to attend it. Certification is school-specific and status-specific: a school may be certified for F-1 only, M-1 only, or both. A culinary institute that is certified only for F-1 will give you an F-1 I-20 even though cooking is a trade. The DHS Study in the States school search lists every certified school and which status each is approved for; check it before paying a deposit. Our [M-1 vocational student visa](/practice-area/key-services/m-1-visa) page describes the pre-enrollment review we do for exactly this question.

Within a certified school, the program itself must be vocational or nonacademic. Typical M-1 programs include:

- Flight training leading to a private, commercial, or instrument rating
- Automotive, diesel, aviation maintenance, HVAC, welding, and electrical trades
- Cosmetology, barbering, and esthetics
- Culinary arts certificates at trade schools
- Medical assisting, phlebotomy, dental assisting, and similar allied health certificates
- Technical certificates in fields such as CNC machining or commercial truck driving

Programs that are academic in nature, including English language training, are F-1 programs even when short. A student who wants an intensive English course followed by a trade program will usually hold F-1 for the language portion and then change to M-1 or obtain a new I-20, a sequence we review closely because it affects both the visa interview and the later practical training budget.

## Full course of study, in numbers

Consular officers and USCIS rely on the DSO's certification, but the numbers still matter to you because they define what you must maintain to stay in status. The regulation sets the floor:

1. Community or junior college: at least 12 semester or quarter hours, unless fewer are needed in the final term.
2. Postsecondary vocational or business school: at least 12 semester or quarter hours, or the equivalent if the school does not use credits.
3. Vocational or nonacademic curriculum in clock hours: at least 18 clock hours per week when the dominant part is classroom instruction, or 22 clock hours per week when the dominant part is shop or laboratory work.
4. Vocational or nonacademic high school curriculum: the minimum the school requires for normal progress toward a diploma.

A program advertised as "part time, evenings" cannot support M-1 status. Neither can a self-paced online certificate; M-1 students may not count online or distance education classes toward the full course of study at all, so a program taught online does not qualify. If you need a lighter schedule for medical reasons, the [M-1 extension and reduced course load rules](/m-1-transfer-and-extension-rules) describe the one narrow exception.

## Flight schools and TSA clearance

Flight training carries an extra layer. Before a school may begin flight instruction for a foreign national, the student must complete the Transportation Security Administration's Alien Flight Student Program process: a security threat assessment with fingerprints, submitted through the school, for each training category (such as initial private pilot, instrument, or multi-engine). Training cannot begin until TSA approves. Plan for that step alongside the visa, because an approved visa with a pending TSA assessment still means sitting on the ground.

Students should also confirm the school is FAA-certified under Part 141 and SEVP-certified for M-1; some flight schools operate under Part 61 and cannot issue an I-20 at all. A student who enters on a visitor visa or the Visa Waiver Program to take "a few lessons" is violating status if the training is a course of study, and will not be able to change to M-1 afterward. The consequences resemble those described in our [F-1 visa refusal guide](/after-your-visa-was-refused-f-1-visa-process): prior status violations follow you into the next interview.

## When F-1 is the better fit

Some programs can be offered under either status, and the choice has consequences the admissions office may not explain. F-1 allows on-campus work, Curricular Practical Training, and 12 months of post-completion OPT with a possible STEM extension; M-1 allows no work during the program and at most six months of training afterward, as laid out in our post on [M-1 practical training rules](/m-1-visa-practical-training-rules). F-1 also permits transfers and extensions without a USCIS filing and allows a later change to another student program. M-1 cannot be changed to F-1 from inside the United States.

If the same credential is available as an associate degree at a community college certified for F-1, that route usually serves the student better. The M-1 is the right fit when the program is short, terminal, and tied to a specific trade the student will practice at home.
