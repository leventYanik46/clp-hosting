Form I-539 is the application you file yourself, without an employer, to extend your stay or change to another nonimmigrant category while you are in the United States. It is used by B-1 and B-2 visitors, by students and exchange visitors in some situations, and by the spouses and children of nearly every work visa holder. The form itself is short. The decision turns on whether it was filed before your I-94 date and on the evidence attached to it, which is where most requests for evidence and denials begin. This post covers who files Form I-539, the deadline, a document checklist, and the mistakes that produce RFEs.

## I-539 or I-129: which form is yours

The rule is simple: if a category requires an employer or sponsor petition, the employer files Form I-129 to change or extend your status; if it does not, you file Form I-539. B-1 and B-2 visitors use the I-539 for extensions and for changes to F-1 or M-1. F-1 students use it for reinstatement and for changes to another category such as B-2 or H-4. J-1 exchange visitors use it in limited cases, subject to the two-year home residency rule. Dependents use it in every work category: H-4, L-2, O-3, E-2 and TN dependents, and F-2, J-2 and M-2 family members. Each spouse and child included in one application is listed on a Form I-539A supplement, and all co-applicants must be seeking the same status. Our [change and extension of status](/practice-area/key-services/change-and-extension-of-status) practice files both forms and pairs an I-539 with the employer's I-129 when a family moves together.

## The deadline is the I-94 date

File before the admit-until date on Form I-94, not the date on your visa. A timely filed, non-frivolous application places you in a period of authorized stay while USCIS decides, even after the I-94 date passes, and unlawful presence does not accrue during that time. A late filing is excused only if the delay was due to extraordinary circumstances beyond your control, the delay was reasonable, you have not otherwise violated status, you remain a bona fide nonimmigrant, and you are not in removal proceedings. Those five conditions are all required, so file early. Our post on [I-94 expiration versus visa expiration](/i-94-expiration-vs-visa-expiration) explains how to retrieve the record and spot errors in it.

## Evidence checklist

Build the package so the officer can confirm three things without an RFE: that you held status when you filed, that you can support yourself, and that the new or extended stay has a credible purpose and end date.

- Passport biographic page for each applicant, valid through the requested period, plus a copy of the visa and the current I-94.
- Every prior Form I-797 approval notice for your current status, and for dependents, the principal's current approval or receipt notice and recent pay statements.
- Proof of the family relationship for dependents: marriage certificate and birth certificates, with certified English translations.
- Proof you maintained status: for students, Forms I-20 or DS-2019 and transcripts; for visitors, nothing suggesting employment; for dependents, the principal's continued employment.
- Financial evidence: bank statements covering several months, a sponsor's letter with the sponsor's own evidence, or a scholarship letter. A lump sum deposited the week before filing needs an explanation of its source.
- A written statement in your own words explaining why you need more time or the new category, your plans, and your intent to depart at the end.
- For a B-2 extension, an itinerary, proof of a return ticket or the funds to buy one, and medical letters if treatment is the reason.
- For a change to F-1 or M-1, the new I-20 and the SEVIS I-901 fee receipt; for J-1, the DS-2019.

## Filing mechanics: online, fees, biometrics, premium processing

Many I-539 categories can be filed online through a USCIS account, which generates an immediate receipt; others still go by mail to a lockbox. Pay the current USCIS filing fee shown on the form page on the day you file, since the amount changes. As of this writing USCIS has exempted most I-539 applicants from the biometrics appointment, but check the current notice because the exemption is a policy choice. Premium processing on Form I-907 is available for applicants in the F, M and J classifications and commits USCIS to act within 30 business days; it does not change the standards, and USCIS may extend it to other dependent categories, so confirm availability before filing.

## After filing: receipt, RFE, decision

Keep the receipt notice; it is your proof of a pending application if you are asked about your status. Answer any request for evidence by its deadline with everything requested, because a second RFE is rare. Do not leave the country while the application is pending; departure abandons it. An approval comes as a Form I-797 with a new I-94 attached showing the new status and date. A denial after your I-94 date has passed puts you out of status on the denial date, with the consequences described in our post on [what happens if your change of status is denied](/change-of-status-denied-what-happens).

## Mistakes that cost applicants their status

Filing after the I-94 date because the visa still looked valid. Leaving a child off the I-539A and discovering it after the child's own I-94 expired. Starting the new activity early, such as attending classes before a change to F-1 is approved, which our post on [changing from B-2 to F-1](/b2-to-f1-change-of-status) explains in detail. Asking for a B-2 extension with no stated reason, or with a reason that sounds like employment. Dependents assuming they were included in the principal's I-129 when the employer's petition covered the worker only; spouses of H-1B workers can read about the employer's side of that filing on our [H-1B visa](/practice-area/key-services/h-1b-visa) page. Visitors who want to understand the limits of their category before asking for more time should read our guide to [the B-1/B-2 visitor visa](/understanding-the-b-1-b-2-visitor-visa).
