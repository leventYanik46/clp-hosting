Most foreign companies structure their U.S. market entry through a U.S. subsidiary, usually a corporation or an LLC, rather than operating as a branch of the parent. A subsidiary keeps U.S. liabilities inside a U.S. entity, gives American customers and banks a familiar counterparty, and supports the visa petitions that bring key staff over. A branch is simpler on paper but exposes the parent company directly to U.S. lawsuits and U.S. tax filings. The structure is only the first decision; the state, the tax setup, the contracts, and the immigration plan all follow from it.

## Branch or subsidiary

### Branch
The foreign company registers to do business in a U.S. state and operates under its own name. There is no separate entity, so the parent's assets are exposed to U.S. claims, and the parent itself files U.S. tax returns for income connected with its U.S. business. A branch can also owe the branch profits tax, which is designed to approximate the tax a subsidiary would pay on dividends to its foreign parent. Branches suit limited, low-risk activity, and some companies use one briefly before forming a subsidiary.

### Subsidiary
A U.S. corporation or LLC owned by the parent. It signs contracts, hires, and pays taxes in its own name. The parent's exposure is generally limited to its investment, as long as the subsidiary is adequately capitalized and run as a separate company with its own bank accounts, records, and decisions. Mixing funds or ignoring formalities can let a court disregard the separation.

Corporation or LLC? A C corporation is the usual choice for a foreign parent because it is a clear taxpayer in its own right and fits most investors' expectations. A single-member LLC owned by a foreign parent is treated by default as a disregarded entity for U.S. tax purposes, which can raise filing and treaty questions the parent did not expect. Get tax advice in both countries before choosing.

## Choosing a state for your U.S. market entry

Delaware is popular for its corporate law and courts, but forming there does not excuse you from other states. If your office and employees are in Virginia or New York, the company must also register there and follow that state's tax and employment rules. Many companies with a single U.S. location simply form in that state and avoid paying for and maintaining two registrations. Investor expectations, the expected size of the U.S. operation, and future plans should drive the choice. The [documents required to set up a company in America](/documents-required-to-set-up-a-company-in-america) are broadly similar from state to state.

## Tax and reporting setup

Every U.S. entity needs an Employer Identification Number. A foreign owner without a U.S. Social Security number can still obtain one, though not always through the standard online application. A U.S. corporation that is at least 25 percent foreign-owned generally must file Form 5472 to report transactions with related parties, and foreign-owned single-member LLCs have a similar filing duty. Penalties for missing these forms can be significant. Transfer pricing rules also require that charges between parent and subsidiary reflect arm's-length terms.

Beneficial ownership reporting under the Corporate Transparency Act has changed several times since it took effect, and as of this writing the federal rules focus on entities formed outside the U.S. that register to do business here. Check FinCEN's current requirements when you form or register.

## Contracts, IP, and people

Put intercompany agreements in place early: a services or distribution agreement between parent and subsidiary, an IP license that lets the subsidiary use the parent's brand and technology, and a management fee or cost-sharing arrangement. These documents support the tax position and make clear who owns what. Register key trademarks with the USPTO, because a registration in your home country does not by itself protect the mark in the U.S.

For U.S. customers, adapt your contract terms to U.S. law, including limitation of liability, governing law, and dispute resolution clauses that work in American courts or arbitration. For U.S. hires, prepare at-will offer letters, state-compliant policies, and Form I-9 verification.

## Visas for transferred staff

The entity structure shapes your immigration options. An L-1 intracompany transfer requires a qualifying relationship between the foreign and U.S. companies, such as parent and subsidiary or a branch, and the employee must have worked for the foreign company for at least one continuous year within the three years before the transfer. A new U.S. office can obtain L-1 approval for an initial one-year period, with evidence of physical premises and a realistic plan to grow. Treaty investor and treaty trader visas (E-2 and E-1) are another route for nationals of treaty countries.
