PERM does not have one timeline. It has three stages that each run on a different clock, and the Department of Labor's review stage is the one you control least. If you want to know how long PERM takes for your own case, you need to look at each stage separately and check the agency's current published processing times.

## Stage 1: Prevailing wage determination

Before the employer can recruit, it must ask the Department of Labor (DOL) for a prevailing wage determination for the job and location. DOL publishes how long it is taking for this step, and the number moves with workload. Some employers file the request early, even before they are ready to start the rest, to avoid waiting later.

## Stage 2: Recruitment

Once the wage is in hand, the employer runs the recruitment steps the rules require. For professional positions, these include a job order with the state workforce agency, which runs for 30 days, two Sunday newspaper advertisements, and a notice of filing posted at the worksite, plus additional recruitment steps for professional occupations. The employer then reviews responses from U.S. applicants and documents lawful, job-related reasons for rejecting them.

After the last recruitment step, the employer must wait at least 30 days before filing, and the recruitment has to fall within a defined window before the filing date. Planning matters here, since a gap in the sequence can force the employer to start over.

## Stage 3: Filing and DOL review

The employer files Form ETA-9089 online. DOL then reviews the application, and it can certify it, deny it, or select it for audit. DOL posts its current processing times for analyst review and for audits. An audit adds time, because the employer must produce its recruitment file and DOL reviews it.

## What causes delays

- **Prevailing wage backlog.** The wait at the DOL wage stage can be significant.
- **Errors in the recruitment.** Missed deadlines, wrong ad content or incomplete records can cause a denial or an audit.
- **Audits.** Some are random, and others are triggered by features of the application.
- **Supervised recruitment.** In rare cases DOL requires the employer to run recruitment under its supervision.
- **Changes to the job.** Altered requirements midstream can force a restart.

## What layoffs do to a pending PERM

Layoffs matter in two ways.

First, if the employer has laid off workers in the same occupation, or a closely related one, in the area of intended employment within the six months before filing, DOL expects the employer to notify them and consider them for the job. Skipping this can cause a denial, so the employer should discuss it with its attorney before filing.

Second, if you are the worker and your own job ends before the case is complete, the PERM generally cannot continue for you. Once an I-140 is approved, you may keep your priority date even if the employer withdraws, depending on timing and circumstances. If your layoff comes before the I-140, you will likely need a new employer to start over.

## Tips for the employee

- Ask your employer when each step started and what DOL's current processing times are.
- Keep your own records, such as degree certificates, experience letters and pay stubs.
- Respond to your employer's requests quickly, since a delay on your side can slow the case.
- Do not change jobs without discussing the effect on your green card case first.
