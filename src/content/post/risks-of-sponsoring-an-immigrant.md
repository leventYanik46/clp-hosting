Friends and relatives are often asked to serve as a co-sponsor for an immigrant's green card. It can seem like a simple signature, but signing Form I-864 creates a legally enforceable contract with the U.S. government. Before you agree, understand the real risks of sponsoring an immigrant.

## What a sponsor promises

When you sign Form I-864, you agree to:

1. **Maintain the immigrant at an income** of at least 125% of the federal poverty guidelines
2. **Reimburse the government** for certain means-tested public benefits the immigrant receives
3. **Notify USCIS** of any change of address within 30 days

The obligation is legally enforceable by the immigrant and by government agencies that provide benefits.

## Joint sponsors vs household members

**A joint sponsor** files their own I-864 and is fully liable for the obligations. Liability is joint and several, meaning the government or the immigrant can pursue either the petitioner or the joint sponsor for the full amount.

**A household member** who signs Form I-864A is also legally bound and agrees to make their income available for the immigrant's support.

## How long the obligation lasts

The obligation continues until one of these events:

- The immigrant becomes a U.S. citizen
- The immigrant has worked, or can be credited with, 40 quarters (about 10 years) of work
- The immigrant leaves the U.S. permanently and abandons their residence
- The immigrant dies
- The sponsor dies (but debts that already arose can still be pursued against the estate)

Divorce doesn't end the obligation. That surprises many sponsoring spouses, and courts have enforced I-864 obligations after divorce.

## Which benefits trigger reimbursement

Means-tested public benefits include:

- Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP)
- Medicaid (with some exceptions, such as emergency care)
- Temporary Assistance for Needy Families (TANF)
- Supplemental Security Income (SSI)
- State Children's Health Insurance Program (CHIP)

Public schools, emergency medical care, and certain other services aren't included.

## Lawsuits by the immigrant

An immigrant can sue the sponsor in court to enforce the promise of support at 125% of the poverty guidelines. These cases often come up after a marriage ends, when the immigrant spouse has little income.

## Questions to ask before signing

1. How well do you know the immigrant and their plans?
2. Does the immigrant have job skills or prospects?
3. Can you afford support if needed?
4. Are you comfortable with an obligation that may last 10 years or more?
5. Do you understand that divorce or a falling out won't end it?

## Reducing your risk

- Help the immigrant find employment quickly
- Encourage naturalization when the immigrant is eligible
- Keep records of support you provide
- Talk to an attorney before signing

## If you've already signed

If you're already a sponsor and worried about your obligations, an attorney can explain when the obligation might end and how to respond to a benefit reimbursement request or a lawsuit.

## Practical example

A cousin agrees to be a joint sponsor for a spouse-based case. Five years later, the couple divorces, the immigrant spouse loses her job, and she enrolls in a means-tested benefit program. The benefit agency can seek reimbursement from both the petitioning spouse and the joint sponsor. If she sues for support, a court can order either sponsor to pay the difference between her income and 125% of the poverty guidelines for her household size. The cousin's obligation would continue until one of the terminating events happens, regardless of the divorce.

## Can you withdraw?

A sponsor can withdraw an I-864 before the immigrant becomes a permanent resident, by notifying USCIS or the consulate in writing. After the green card is granted, withdrawal is no longer possible.

## Alternatives to serving as a joint sponsor

If you want to help but are uneasy about the obligation, there are other ways to support the case:

- **Help the petitioner increase documented income or assets,** for example by helping them document household members who can sign Form I-864A.
- **Offer a job to the immigrant,** where lawful, so their own income counts after they arrive.
- **Provide housing or other support informally,** without signing a legal undertaking.

None of these creates the same legal liability as signing Form I-864. They may or may not be enough for the case, but they are worth discussing with the petitioner and their attorney first.
