The difference between strict liability vs negligence in a product case is the difference between proving the product was defective and proving the company was careless. Strict liability looks at the product. Negligence looks at the conduct. In Washington, DC, and New York you can plead both. In Virginia you cannot plead strict liability at all, which changes how a Virginia product case is built from the first letter. CLP's [defective product practice](/practice-area/key-services/dangerous-or-defective-products) handles claims in all three jurisdictions, and the choice of theory is the first strategic decision in each one.

## What strict product liability requires

Strict liability does not mean automatic liability. It means the plaintiff does not have to prove fault. The elements are that the defendant sells the product in the ordinary course of business; the product was defective and unreasonably dangerous when it left the defendant's control; it reached the user without substantial change; and the defect caused the injury. Whether the manufacturer tested carefully, followed industry practice, or had no way of knowing about the hazard is beside the point. If the product was defective, every seller in the chain is liable, from the manufacturer to the retailer.

The theory grew out of a recognition that consumers cannot inspect a sealed product or investigate a factory, so the law places the risk on the companies that profit from the product and can insure against it. DC adopted it decades ago, and New York applies it with a risk-utility test for design defects. The three defect categories (manufacturing, design, and warning) all fit inside strict liability, as explained in [the three types of product defects](/three-types-of-product-defects).

## What a negligence claim requires

Negligence requires proof that the defendant failed to act as a reasonable manufacturer or seller would have. The failure can be in design, in testing, in quality control, in inspection, or in warning. The plaintiff has to show what the company knew or should have known, which means discovery into internal emails, test reports, complaint logs, and the decisions made when the design was approved.

That is more work, and it is also more persuasive. A jury that hears a manufacturer knew about a hazard for three years and chose not to fix it reacts differently than one told a product was simply defective. Negligence also opens the door to punitive damages in jurisdictions that allow them, which strict liability generally does not. Most strong product cases outside Virginia plead both, so that the strict liability claim survives even if the negligence evidence is thin and the negligence story drives the value if it is not.

## Breach of warranty: the third route

Every sale of goods carries an implied warranty of merchantability, meaning the product is fit for its ordinary purpose, and sometimes an implied warranty of fitness for a particular purpose the seller knew about. Express warranties arise from statements on packaging and in advertising. A product that injures a user during ordinary use is, almost by definition, not merchantable.

Warranty claims have their own rules. The limitations period is four years from the date of delivery rather than from the injury, so a product bought five years ago may have lost its warranty claim while the personal injury claim remains alive. The buyer must notify the seller of the breach within a reasonable time after discovering it. Privity requirements that once limited warranty claims to the direct buyer have been relaxed by statute in Virginia and elsewhere for personal injuries, so a family member or bystander injured by the product can usually sue.

## Why Virginia is different

Virginia has never adopted strict liability in tort for products. A Virginia plaintiff sues in negligence and in breach of the implied warranty of merchantability, and in practice the warranty claim does much of the work that strict liability does elsewhere, because it focuses on whether the product was unreasonably dangerous rather than on the manufacturer's care. The two claims carry different defenses. Contributory negligence is a complete defense to the negligence claim in Virginia, as it is to almost every negligence claim there. For the warranty claim, the defenses are unforeseeable misuse and assumption of a known risk, which are narrower.

DC applies contributory negligence to negligence claims but generally not to strict liability claims, where the defenses are again misuse and assumption of risk. New York applies comparative fault to every theory and reduces the award by the plaintiff's share. Which theory you plead therefore determines whether your own conduct ends the case, reduces it, or is irrelevant.

## Strict liability vs negligence: the defenses under each theory

Some defenses apply across the board. Misuse means the product was used in a way the manufacturer could not reasonably foresee; foreseeable misuse, such as standing on a chair, is not a defense. Substantial alteration means someone changed the product after it left the defendant, which breaks the chain. Federal preemption can bar claims about certain FDA-approved devices and generic drugs regardless of theory. The statute of limitations runs two years in Virginia and three in DC and New York for the injury claim.

Other defenses are theory-specific. Compliance with government or industry standards is strong evidence of reasonable care in a negligence case and weaker in strict liability, where the question is the product rather than the effort. A state-of-the-art defense, meaning no safer design was known when the product was made, defeats negligence and most design-defect strict liability claims but not a manufacturing defect claim.
