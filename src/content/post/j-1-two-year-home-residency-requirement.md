The J-1 two-year home residency requirement applies to you if any one of three things is true: your program was funded in whole or part by the U.S. government or your home government, your field appears on the Exchange Visitor Skills List for your country of nationality or last permanent residence, or you came for graduate medical education or training. If none of those apply, you are not subject to it, even if a notation on your visa says otherwise. If one of them does apply, you must return home for two cumulative years before you can hold H, L, or K status or receive permanent residence, unless you obtain a waiver.

## How to check whether you are subject

Start with two documents. The lower left corner of your Form DS-2019 has a box where the consular officer marks whether Section 212(e) applies. Your J-1 visa stamp often carries an annotation reading "Bearer is subject to 212(e)" or "not subject." Neither mark is final. Consular officers make preliminary determinations, and they are sometimes wrong in both directions. The binding answer turns on the facts: who paid, what the Skills List said on the date your program began, and what category you were in.

The Skills List is the trigger that surprises people. It lists fields a country has told the State Department it needs. A country can be added or removed, and the list has been revised in recent years; removal from the list has been applied retroactively to exchange visitors whose programs were ongoing or completed. If your country's status changed, ask counsel to confirm which version governs you. For a program-level view of how sponsors structure these placements, our [J-1 exchange visitor visa](/practice-area/key-services/j-1-visa) page explains what we review before a participant commits to a category.

If you are unsure, you can request an Advisory Opinion from the State Department's Waiver Review Division. It is free, it takes time, and it gives you a written answer you can rely on later.

## What the requirement actually blocks

The requirement does not prevent you from staying in the United States in every status. It prevents three specific things until you satisfy the two years or receive a waiver:

- A change of status inside the United States from J-1 to any other nonimmigrant category, with limited exceptions (A, G, and U statuses, and certain T cases).
- Issuance of an H, L, or K visa at a consulate.
- Adjustment of status or an immigrant visa, including through marriage to a U.S. citizen.

You can still obtain an F-1, B-2, O-1, or TN visa at a consulate and return in that status. That gap matters for planning: many researchers subject to the rule move to O-1 status through consular processing and address the waiver later. The requirement also cannot be satisfied by living in a third country; the two years must be spent in the country of nationality or last permanent residence, and the time is counted cumulatively, so several long visits can add up.

## The five waiver bases

A waiver under Section 212(e) is requested through the State Department (Form DS-3035 and the online application) and, if recommended, approved by USCIS on Form I-612 or through the recommendation process. The five bases differ sharply in difficulty.

1. No objection statement. Your home government tells the State Department it does not object to your staying. This is the most common route for Skills List cases, but it is not available if your program was funded by the U.S. government, and it is rarely enough on its own for physicians who received graduate medical training.
2. Interested government agency request. A U.S. federal agency asks for the waiver because your departure would harm a program of interest to it. Common for researchers at federally funded labs and for physicians under agency programs.
3. Persecution. You would face persecution on account of race, religion, or political opinion if you returned. This is adjudicated by USCIS on Form I-612 and requires evidence comparable to an asylum claim.
4. Exceptional hardship. Your departure would cause exceptional hardship to a U.S. citizen or permanent resident spouse or child. Ordinary separation is not enough; medical, financial, and country-conditions evidence must show hardship beyond the normal consequences of relocation.
5. State health agency (Conrad 30) request. Available to physicians who commit to three years of full-time practice in a federally designated shortage area.

Each state's Conrad program has its own application cycle and slot count, so physicians need to plan a year ahead. Timing also affects the H-1B transition: a physician cannot change to H-1B until the waiver is approved, which is one reason the [J-1 to H-1B change of status](/change-j-1-to-h-1b-status) question deserves its own analysis.

## Common mistakes

The errors we see most often are avoidable. People assume the DS-2019 box is conclusive and skip the Advisory Opinion. Participants on short programs such as [Summer Work and Travel](/work-and-travel-2026-expenses-things-students-want-to-know) rarely trigger the rule, but some do through the Skills List and find out only when an H-1B petition is denied. Spouses forget that J-2 dependents carry the same requirement as the principal. And applicants file a no objection request without checking whether U.S. funding appears anywhere in the program record, which makes that route unavailable and wastes months.
