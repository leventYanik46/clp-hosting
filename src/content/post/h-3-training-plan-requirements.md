An H-3 training plan that "deals in generalities with no fixed schedule, objectives, or means of evaluation" cannot be approved. That language comes from the regulation itself, and it is the single most common reason H-3 petitions are denied. USCIS wants a document that reads like a curriculum: what the trainee will learn in each phase, how many hours per week will be spent in classroom instruction versus supervised practice, who supervises, how progress is measured, and how much of the time, if any, involves productive work. If a reviewer cannot reconstruct a typical week from the plan, it is not finished.

## The seven statements the regulation requires

Form I-129 and its H supplement must be accompanied by a statement from the petitioner covering seven points. Treat them as section headings in the plan:

1. The type of training and supervision to be given, and the structure of the program.
2. The proportion of time that will be devoted to productive employment.
3. The number of hours that will be spent in classroom instruction and the number spent in on-the-job training.
4. The career abroad for which the training will prepare the beneficiary.
5. The reasons why the training cannot be obtained in the beneficiary's country and why it is necessary for the beneficiary to be trained in the United States.
6. The source of any remuneration the trainee will receive.
7. Any benefit that will accrue to the petitioner from providing the training.

Point five is addressed in detail in our post on [proving the training is not available in the home country](/h-3-training-not-available-home-country). The rest are about the plan's internal design, and our [H-3 visa](/practice-area/key-services/h-3-visa) page explains how we turn a company's informal onboarding into a document that answers all seven in the order an adjudicator expects.

## Building the schedule

Phases work better than a single block. A twelve-month program might be divided into four quarters: orientation and classroom fundamentals, supervised observation, supervised practice with increasing responsibility, and a capstone with evaluation. For each phase, state the dates, the topics, the instructional method, the trainer by title, and the hours per week split between classroom and on-the-job components. A table is not required, but the numbers must be explicit: "approximately 10 hours per week of classroom instruction and 30 hours of supervised on-the-job training" is adequate; "a mix of classroom and practical training" is not.

The on-the-job component is where productive employment becomes an issue. The regulation permits productive work only if it is incidental and necessary to the training. The plan should say what the trainee will do with their hands, why that activity is needed to learn the skill, and that the output will not replace work normally performed by a U.S. employee. If the trainee will produce anything of commercial value, quantify it as a small fraction of the week and explain why it is unavoidable. A plan that shows the trainee working a full production schedule under the label "on-the-job training" will be read as a job.

## Supervision and evaluation

Name the supervisors by title and qualification and describe how often they meet with the trainee. State the evaluation method: written assessments at the end of each phase, practical demonstrations, or a final project, with a description of what a passing result looks like. Include any certificate or credential the trainee receives at the end. The point is to show the petitioner has the trained staff to deliver the program, which is a separate regulatory requirement (the physical plant and sufficiently trained manpower to provide the training specified), and that the program has a defined end.

## Remuneration and benefit to the petitioner

Trainees may be paid, and most are. State the source and amount of the stipend or salary and whether the petitioner or a foreign affiliate pays it. Paying a market wage does not by itself turn training into employment, but it invites the question, so pair the salary disclosure with a clear statement of the trainee's non-productive role.

On the benefit question, be candid. USCIS expects the petitioner to gain something, usually a better-trained workforce abroad, a stronger relationship with a foreign distributor or client, or an employee prepared for a role at the foreign affiliate. What the petitioner may not say is that the program will prepare the trainee to staff U.S. operations; programs designed to recruit and train foreign nationals for the ultimate staffing of domestic operations are barred.

## Errors that draw Requests for Evidence

The following appear repeatedly in RFEs and denials:

- Hours that do not add up to a full week, or that change between the plan and the support letter.
- A plan copied from a job description, with duties listed instead of lessons.
- No explanation of why the trainee's existing experience does not already cover the material.
- A duration that matches the two-year maximum with no justification, which suggests the length was set by the visa rather than the curriculum.
- Silence on what the trainee will do after the program, which undermines the career-abroad requirement.

Employers comparing options should know that the J-1 trainee program uses a similar document, the DS-7002, reviewed by a sponsor rather than USCIS; our post on [J-1 intern and trainee categories](/j-1-intern-vs-trainee-category) describes it, and the [J-1 visa](/practice-area/key-services/j-1-visa) page covers the sponsor process. Companies that already run structured programs for U.S. hires often find the H-3 plan easier to write because the curriculum exists.
