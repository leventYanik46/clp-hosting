A will and a revocable living trust both say who gets your property when you die. The difference is in how the property gets there. A will works through probate, a public court proceeding that appoints your executor and supervises distribution. A funded revocable trust passes the assets it holds to your beneficiaries through your successor trustee with no court involved. Choosing between a revocable living trust vs will is less about which document is better and more about whether avoiding probate is worth the extra work of a trust in your particular situation.

## What a will does that a trust cannot

A will is the only document that can nominate a guardian for minor children, and it is the only document that catches assets you never got around to retitling. For that reason nearly every trust-based plan our [wills and trusts team](/practice-area/key-services/wills-trusts-probate-administration) prepares includes a short "pour-over" will that sends any stray probate assets into the trust at death. A will is also simpler to sign and cheaper to prepare. In Virginia, DC, and New York it requires two witnesses, and the rules on what makes it valid are strict but well understood. Our note on [will execution requirements](/what-makes-a-will-valid-in-texas-what-are-the-requirements) walks through one state's formalities; the logic is similar in the firm's home jurisdictions.

## What a revocable trust does that a will cannot

### Avoids probate for funded assets

Assets titled in the trust's name pass under its terms without a court filing, notice to creditors by publication, inventories, or accountings to a clerk. Beneficiaries can receive distributions sooner, and the trustee does not need letters from a court to deal with banks. Our post on [the probate process step by step](/how-probate-works-step-by-step) shows what the trust lets you skip.

### Keeps the plan private

A probated will becomes a public record, along with the inventory of assets in many jurisdictions. A trust is a private contract. Families with a business, a public profile, or a beneficiary they would rather not advertise often choose a trust for this reason alone.

### Handles incapacity without a court

If you become unable to manage your affairs, your successor trustee steps in over the trust assets immediately, under terms you wrote. A will does nothing during your life. A durable power of attorney covers incapacity too, but institutions sometimes resist powers of attorney, while a trustee's authority over trust accounts is rarely questioned.

### Avoids ancillary probate in other states

Real estate in your sole name in a second state requires a separate probate proceeding there. Real estate deeded to your trust does not. For clients with a condominium in Florida, a family home in Virginia, and an apartment in New York, this is usually the deciding factor.

## What a revocable trust does not do

A revocable trust provides no protection from your creditors and no estate tax savings. You keep full control, so the law treats the assets as yours for every purpose, including lawsuits, Medicaid eligibility, and taxes. Clients who want protection need a different, irrevocable structure, which our [estate planning and asset protection](/practice-area/key-services/estate-planning-asset-protection) team designs, and our post on [revocable versus irrevocable trusts](/revocable-vs-irrevocable-trust-asset-protection) explains the trade.

The trust also does nothing for assets that were never transferred into it. Funding means retitling accounts, recording new deeds, and assigning business interests. A trust that is signed and filed in a drawer, with every asset still in the client's individual name, produces a full probate anyway, plus the cost of a trust that was never used. In New York, a lifetime trust must also be signed with specific formalities, either acknowledged before a notary or witnessed by two people, to be valid at all.

## Revocable living trust vs will: cost, upfront and later

A will costs less to prepare. A trust costs more upfront because the document is longer and because funding takes time. The comparison flips at death: a probated estate pays court costs, a probate tax in Virginia, publication fees, bond premiums if required, and legal fees for the administration, while a trust administration usually involves less of each. Whether the later savings justify the earlier spend depends on the size of the estate, the number of states involved, and how much the family values speed and privacy.

## Who usually needs a trust

A trust tends to earn its cost for people who own real estate in more than one state, want privacy, expect a period of incapacity (a single older client without a nearby family member, for instance), have a blended family where the sequence of distributions matters, or have beneficiaries who should receive money over time rather than in a lump sum. A will-based plan is often enough for a married couple with a single home held jointly, retirement accounts with current beneficiary designations, and adult children who get along. The will then controls very little, because most assets pass outside it, and probate is modest.

## Either way, the rest of the plan is the same

Both routes still require a durable power of attorney, a health care directive, and correct beneficiary designations on retirement accounts and life insurance.
