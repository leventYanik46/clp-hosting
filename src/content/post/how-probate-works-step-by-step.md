Probate is the court-supervised process of proving a will, appointing someone to administer the estate, paying what the deceased owed, and distributing what is left. The sequence is the same in Washington, DC, Virginia, and New York even though the courts, forms, and deadlines differ. Understanding the probate process step by step helps a family know what to expect, what the executor must do, and why one estate closes in months while another drags on for years.

## Step 1: Locate the will and confirm what passes through probate

The original will matters; a copy can be admitted only with extra proof. Before filing anything, list the assets. Only property titled in the decedent's sole name without a beneficiary designation passes through probate. Jointly owned property with survivorship, retirement accounts, life insurance, payable-on-death accounts, and assets held in a living trust pass outside it. Many estates turn out to be mostly non-probate, which shortens the process considerably. If there is no will, the estate still goes through probate under the intestacy statute, as explained in [what happens when someone dies without a will](/what-happens-without-a-will).

## Step 2: Open the estate and appoint the personal representative

The nominated executor files to open the estate in the county where the decedent lived. The mechanics differ. In Virginia, the executor qualifies before the clerk of the circuit court, often without a hearing, takes an oath, and receives a certificate of qualification; a probate tax is assessed on the estate's value. In the District of Columbia, a petition for probate goes to the Superior Court's Probate Division, which issues letters of administration. In New York, the petition goes to the Surrogate's Court, which issues citations to the distributees (the people who would inherit without a will) so they have a chance to object before letters testamentary issue. Bond may be required unless the will waives it, and nonresident fiduciaries face additional conditions. ## Step 3: Give notice to heirs, beneficiaries, and creditors

Once appointed, the personal representative must notify the people with an interest in the estate. Virginia requires a written notice of probate to heirs and beneficiaries shortly after qualification, with an affidavit filed to prove it was sent. DC and New York have their own notice rules. Creditors are notified in the form the jurisdiction requires, which can mean newspaper publication, direct notice to known creditors, or both. The creditor period that follows sets a floor on how long the estate stays open: in DC, claims are generally barred six months after the first publication; in New York, an executor who distributes in good faith more than seven months after letters issue is protected against claims not yet presented. These windows exist to protect the executor, who can be personally liable for paying heirs before valid debts.

## Step 4: Inventory, value, and protect the assets

The personal representative collects the assets, obtains date-of-death values, opens an estate bank account under a new taxpayer identification number, and files an inventory. Virginia requires the inventory to be filed with the commissioner of accounts within four months of qualification. Real estate, business interests, and collectibles usually need an appraisal. During this period the representative must keep property insured, pay the mortgage, and avoid commingling estate funds with personal funds. The duties in this phase are described in our post on [an executor's duties after a death](/executor-duties-after-death).

## Step 5: Pay debts, expenses, and taxes

Valid claims are paid in the order state law sets, with administration expenses and funeral costs typically first. The representative files the decedent's final income tax return, a fiduciary income tax return for the estate if it earns enough income, and a federal estate tax return only if the gross estate exceeds the federal exemption or the surviving spouse wants to preserve the unused exemption through a portability election. New York and DC impose their own estate taxes with lower exemption thresholds, so a return may be due at the state level even when nothing is owed federally. Virginia has no estate tax. Distributing before taxes are settled is the mistake that creates personal liability for executors most often.

## Step 6: Account and distribute

Before distribution, the representative prepares an accounting showing every receipt and disbursement. In Virginia the accounting goes to the commissioner of accounts for approval; in DC supervised estates it is filed with the court; in New York it may be settled informally with receipts and releases from the beneficiaries or formally through a judicial accounting. Once the accounting is approved or the beneficiaries sign releases, the representative distributes the remaining assets according to the will, obtains receipts, and files whatever closing paperwork the jurisdiction requires. The estate is then closed and the representative discharged.

## What determines how long the probate process takes

No estate is finished before the creditor period runs, and most take longer. The factors that stretch the timeline are a will contest or a dispute among beneficiaries, real estate that must be sold, a business that must be valued or wound down, assets in more than one state (each requiring its own ancillary proceeding), a federal or state estate tax return awaiting review, and beneficiaries who cannot be found or will not sign releases. An estate made up of a house, a few accounts, and cooperative heirs can close shortly after the creditor period. An estate with any of the complications above should expect a considerably longer process, with the court's own calendar adding time in busy jurisdictions.
