A strategic partnership agreement is signed with optimism and tested by its exit clause. Two companies agree to build, sell, or distribute something together, the business teams shake hands on the commercial outline, and the document that follows is expected to fill in the rest. The questions that cause trouble later are the ones that felt premature at signing: who owns the product the two teams build together, what happens to the customers if the partnership ends, and whether the exclusivity one side asked for will block the other side's next deal. Our [contract review and executive counsel](/practice-area/key-services/contract-review-and-executive-counsel) practice advises leadership on these agreements before the term sheet hardens, and the questions below are the ones we put on the table first.

## Contract or joint venture entity?

Most strategic partnerships are contractual: each company stays separate and the agreement governs how they cooperate. That is simpler, faster, and reversible. A separate joint venture entity, usually an LLC owned by both companies, makes sense when the partnership will hold assets, hire its own staff, raise its own capital, or needs a liability wall between the venture and its parents. The entity route brings its own governing documents and tax questions, which our [business formation and structuring](/practice-area/key-services/business-formation-structuring) team handles alongside the partnership agreement itself. Decide this first, because every other question is answered differently depending on the structure.

## Who owns what is built together

This is the question most often left vague, and the one most likely to end in litigation. The agreement should separate three categories. Background IP is what each party brings in and keeps. Foreground IP is what is created under the partnership. Improvements to one party's background IP made during the work are a third category that needs its own rule.

Joint ownership of foreground IP sounds fair and works badly. Under U.S. law, each joint owner can generally use and license jointly owned patents and copyrights independently, with rules that differ between the two, so neither party controls the asset and neither can stop the other from licensing it to a competitor. A cleaner structure assigns ownership to one party and grants the other a defined license, or divides ownership by component. Address data the same way: who owns the customer data, the usage data, and any models or analytics derived from it. If the partnership involves confidential processes or formulas, CLP's [trade secret protection](/practice-area/key-services/trade-secret-protection) team layers the use and access restrictions needed to keep those protected across two organizations.

## How money moves

Define the economics with the precision of a pricing schedule. If revenue is shared, state what counts as revenue (gross or net of specific deductions), who collects from the customer, when the share is paid, and what reporting accompanies it. If costs are shared, define which costs, how they are approved, and what happens when one side overspends. Add audit rights, so each party can verify the other's numbers, and a mechanism for adjusting the split if the contribution changes over time.

Also address what each party commits to contribute: people, marketing spend, engineering hours, minimum purchase volumes. A partnership where one party's commitment is specific and the other's is "commercially reasonable efforts" will produce the dispute that phrase invites. The negotiation of these terms follows the same priorities as any commercial deal, which we describe in [how to negotiate a business contract](/how-to-negotiate-business-contract).

## Exclusivity and non-competition

Exclusivity is the term most often requested and least often thought through. If you agree not to work with the partner's competitors, define the competitors by name or by a narrow category, limit the restriction by territory and product, set a duration, and add an exit if the partner fails to meet its own commitments. Open-ended exclusivity in a pilot-stage partnership can block a better deal for years.

When the partners are themselves competitors in any market, antitrust law shapes what the agreement can contain and what the teams can discuss. Agreements on pricing, allocation of customers or territories, and sharing of competitively sensitive information outside the partnership's scope can create exposure regardless of intent. Involve counsel before those conversations start, not after.

## Decision rights, resources, and deadlock

Partnerships need a decision-making structure that matches their scope. Common elements include a steering committee with named representatives from each side, a list of decisions that require both parties' approval, a process for resolving disagreements by escalation to executives, and named key personnel who cannot be reassigned without consent. Define service levels and response times if one party depends on the other's operations. Without this structure, decisions default to whoever is more persistent, and the quieter partner accumulates grievances until the relationship fails.

The agreement should also cover the standard allocation of risk between the parties: warranties about each side's contributions, indemnification for third-party claims arising from each side's products or conduct, and a limitation of liability calibrated to the size of the partnership. Those clauses interact in ways that are easy to get wrong, as our post on [how limitation of liability and indemnity clauses work](/limitation-of-liability-indemnification-clauses) explains.

## Term, exit, and what survives

Set an initial term that matches the time needed to know whether the partnership works, with renewal by agreement rather than automatically. Provide termination for convenience on reasonable notice, termination for material breach after a cure period, and termination on a change of control of either party, because a partnership with a company is not a partnership with its acquirer. If an acquisition is a plausible end state for the partnership itself, our [mergers and acquisitions](/practice-area/key-services/mergers-acquisitions-business-transactions) team can build in the right of first offer or negotiation period that keeps that path open.

Then write the wind-down. Who owns the customer relationships and the contracts signed under the partnership? What transition support does each side provide, and for how long? Which licenses survive termination and which end? Do non-solicitation of employees, confidentiality, and the IP allocations survive, and for how long? These are the clauses that decide whether the end of a partnership is an orderly separation or a second dispute.
