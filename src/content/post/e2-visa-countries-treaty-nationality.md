The E-2 investor visa is only available to nationals of countries that have a qualifying treaty with the United States. Before anything else, check whether your nationality is on the list of E-2 visa countries. If it isn't, no amount of investment will qualify you, and you will need a different path or a second citizenship.

## How E-2 treaty eligibility works

The E-2 visa exists because of bilateral treaties of commerce and navigation, or bilateral investment treaties, between the U.S. and other countries. The State Department publishes the current list of treaty countries along with the date each treaty entered into force.

Two nationality rules apply:

1. **The investor must hold the nationality of the treaty country.** Permanent residence there is not enough. A Canadian permanent resident who is a citizen of a non-treaty country does not qualify through Canada.
2. **The U.S. business must be at least 50% owned by nationals of that same treaty country.** If two partners from different treaty countries each own 50%, the business can qualify for nationals of both countries, but a business owned 50% by a treaty national and 50% by a non-treaty national needs careful analysis.

Green card holders and U.S. citizens who own part of the business don't count toward the treaty nationality requirement.

## Common treaty country questions

**Is Canada an E-2 country?** Yes. Canadian citizens are eligible. One detail surprises many Canadians: although they enter the U.S. without a visa in most categories, E-2 is an exception. Canadians need an actual E-2 visa from a U.S. consulate, or a change of status through USCIS if they are already in the U.S. in another status.

**Is India an E-2 country?** No. India has no qualifying treaty, so Indian nationals cannot obtain an E-2 visa based on Indian citizenship. The same applies to China, Brazil, Russia, and several other large economies.

**Which countries do qualify?** The list includes most of Western Europe, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, Mexico, Colombia, Turkey, Australia, Canada, and many others. Because treaties can be added or suspended, always check the State Department's current list rather than a third-party chart.

## Second citizenships and the E-2 visa

Nationals of non-treaty countries sometimes obtain a second citizenship from a treaty country to qualify. Grenada is the best-known example, because it has an E-2 treaty and a citizenship-by-investment program. Turkey and Montenegro have also attracted attention for similar reasons.

This approach can work, but it gets scrutiny:

- **Newer treaties may require prior domicile.** For treaties that entered into force after a 2013 change in U.S. law, an investor who obtained citizenship through a financial investment must have been domiciled in that country for at least three years before applying. Check whether your treaty country is covered.
- **The consulate will ask about your connection to the country.** You don't need to live there for older treaties, but expect questions about how and why you acquired citizenship.
- **You apply where you can.** Many second-citizenship holders apply at a consulate in the treaty country itself or in their country of residence. Each post has its own scheduling rules for third-country nationals.
- **The passport must be valid and the citizenship lawful.** Any irregularity in how the second citizenship was granted can sink the case.

Consider the total cost before going this route. Citizenship-by-investment programs carry their own fees, due diligence, and timelines, and the E-2 investment comes on top of that.

## If your country isn't an E-2 country

If you can't qualify through nationality, consider these alternatives:

- **EB-5,** which has no nationality restriction but requires a larger investment.
- **L-1,** if you own or manage a business abroad that can open a U.S. affiliate.
- **O-1,** for founders with a strong individual track record.
- **H-1B,** sponsored by a company you don't control, or in some cases one you do (with limits on how the employer-employee relationship is structured).

## Dual nationals and spouses

If you hold two nationalities and one is from a treaty country, you can apply through that nationality. The U.S. business must be majority-owned by people of that same nationality.

Your spouse and children don't need to share your nationality. They receive E-2 dependent status based on your status, regardless of their own citizenship.

## Check eligibility first

Confirming your treaty eligibility is free and quick, and it should happen before you sign a lease, form a company, or move money. If your situation involves a second citizenship, dual nationality, or mixed ownership, get advice on the structure early.
