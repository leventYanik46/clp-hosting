The structure question comes before the price question. In an asset purchase, the buyer picks which assets and liabilities it will take and leaves the selling entity behind. In a stock purchase (or a membership interest purchase, for an LLC), the buyer takes the entity itself, with every contract, permit, debt, and lawsuit attached. Most negotiations over asset purchase vs stock purchase turn on three things: who carries the unknown liabilities, how the deal is taxed, and how much work it takes to move the business across. ## What changes with an asset purchase

In an asset deal, the purchase agreement lists what transfers: equipment, inventory, customer lists, trademarks, domain names, specific contracts, and goodwill. It also lists the liabilities the buyer agrees to assume, often limited to ordinary trade payables and performance under the assigned contracts going forward. Everything else stays with the seller's entity, which usually winds down after closing and distributes the proceeds to its owners.

That selectivity is the main reason buyers prefer asset purchases. If the seller has an undisclosed tax exposure, a wage-and-hour claim, or a product liability problem from last year, the buyer did not buy it. The protection is not absolute. Courts in many states apply successor liability doctrines (de facto merger, mere continuation, or fraudulent transfer) when a buyer takes substantially all of the assets and keeps the same management, ownership, and operations. Careful structuring and a fair price reduce that risk; they do not erase it.

The tax side also favors buyers. An asset purchase lets the buyer allocate the price among the purchased assets and take a stepped-up basis, which produces depreciation and amortization deductions after closing. The parties report that allocation to the IRS on Form 8594, and both sides must use the same numbers, so the allocation itself becomes a negotiated term.

The cost of an asset deal is friction. Each contract has to be assigned, and many commercial contracts contain anti-assignment clauses that require the counterparty's consent. Leases, government contracts, and software licenses are common sticking points. Licenses and permits often cannot be transferred at all, so the buyer must apply for new ones. Employees are technically terminated by the seller and rehired by the buyer, which triggers new offer letters, benefit enrollment, and in some cases notice obligations under federal or state law.

## What changes with a stock purchase

In a stock purchase, the target company keeps operating exactly as it did the day before. Contracts, permits, bank accounts, employer identification numbers, and employee relationships all stay in place because the legal entity has not changed. For a business built on a few hard-to-assign agreements, a long-term lease, or regulated licenses, that continuity can be worth more than any tax benefit.

The tradeoff is that the buyer inherits everything, known and unknown. The purchase agreement responds with detailed representations and warranties, disclosure schedules, and an indemnification package that lets the buyer recover for pre-closing problems that surface later. Our [post on reps, warranties, and indemnities](/reps-warranties-indemnification-explained) walks through how those protections are usually built.

Stock purchases still have consent issues, just fewer of them. Many contracts contain change-of-control clauses that let the counterparty terminate or renegotiate when ownership shifts, so diligence has to read for those clauses as closely as an asset deal reads for anti-assignment language. Sellers generally prefer stock deals: the proceeds are typically taxed once, at capital gains rates, and the seller walks away without a shell entity to wind down.

## How the two sides usually bridge the gap

Because the structures pull in opposite directions, the final answer often lands somewhere in the middle. A few tools come up in deals of every size:

- A Section 338(h)(10) election, which lets certain stock purchases (for example, of an S corporation or a corporate subsidiary) be taxed as if they were asset purchases, giving the buyer its basis step-up while the seller keeps the simplicity of selling stock.
- A price adjustment that compensates the seller for the extra tax cost of an asset deal, sometimes called a gross-up.
- A stronger indemnity, a larger escrow, or representation and warranty insurance in a stock deal to offset the inherited-liability risk.
- An asset deal with a carefully drafted list of excluded liabilities and a consent plan that starts before signing, so the hardest counterparties are approached early.

Entity type matters too. If the target is an LLC taxed as a partnership, a purchase of all the membership interests by a single buyer is generally treated as an asset purchase for federal tax purposes anyway, which removes much of the tax argument and leaves the liability and continuity questions to decide the structure. That is one reason entity choice at formation, discussed on our [business formation and structuring](/practice-area/key-services/business-formation-structuring) page, has consequences years later at exit.

## Questions to answer before you pick a structure

Start with the liabilities. Ask what the seller's entity has done in the last several years that could generate a claim, and whether diligence can actually surface it. A [due diligence review when buying a business](/due-diligence-checklist-buying-a-business) usually answers this better than instinct does.

Then count the consents. List every contract, lease, license, and permit the business depends on, and mark which ones have anti-assignment or change-of-control language. If the critical ones cannot be moved without a counterparty who may say no, an asset deal may not be realistic no matter how clean it looks on paper.

Finally, run the tax numbers both ways with an accountant before the letter of intent is signed, not after. Structure is often fixed in the LOI, and reopening it later costs goodwill and time.
