A Notice to Appear (NTA) is the document that starts removal proceedings in immigration court. Receiving one is frightening, but it is not a deportation order. It is a charge, and you have the right to respond to it. What you do in the weeks after you receive it can shape everything that follows.

## What a Notice to Appear is

The NTA, issued on Form I-862, tells you the government's allegations about you and the reasons it believes you can be removed from the United States. It is filed with the immigration court, which is part of the Executive Office for Immigration Review (EOIR), not USCIS. The notice gives you information about your first hearing, although the date and time sometimes come in a separate notice.

Do not ignore it. If you miss a hearing, the judge can order you removed in your absence. That is called an in absentia order, and it can be difficult to reverse.

## What happens in immigration court

Cases usually move through two stages.

**Master calendar hearings** are short, early hearings. The judge confirms your identity, asks whether you admit or deny the allegations, and asks whether you will seek relief. This is also when you can ask for time to find a lawyer or to prepare your case.

**Individual hearings** are the full trial. You present evidence and testimony, a government attorney may question you, and the judge decides whether you are removable and whether you qualify for any relief. A decision can be given at the hearing or later in writing.

If either side disagrees with the decision, an appeal to the Board of Immigration Appeals generally must be filed within 30 days. Missing that deadline can end the case.

## Possible forms of relief

Whether you have a defense depends on your history, your family, and the facts of your case. Options an attorney may look at include:

- **Asylum**, for people who fear persecution because of race, religion, nationality, political opinion, or membership in a particular social group, generally filed within one year of arrival unless an exception applies
- **Withholding of removal and protection under the Convention Against Torture**, which have a higher standard but are available in some cases where asylum is not
- **Cancellation of removal**, with separate requirements for permanent residents and for certain non-permanent residents, which usually involve long residence, good moral character, and, for non-permanent residents, hardship to a qualifying U.S. citizen or permanent resident relative
- **Adjustment of status**, for people who qualify for a green card through family or another path
- **Waivers**, which can forgive certain grounds that otherwise block relief
- **Voluntary departure**, which lets some people leave on their own terms and avoid some consequences of a removal order
- **Motions to terminate or dismiss**, when the government cannot prove its case or the NTA has legal defects

Not every option fits every person, and some can be lost by waiting. Deadlines, eligibility rules, and priorities in government enforcement change over time, so rely on current legal advice rather than what worked for someone else.

## Why the details of your case matter

Two people with the same NTA can have very different outcomes. Your manner of entry, the length of your time in the United States, family ties, past arrests, prior immigration filings, and any fear of returning to your country all affect what is possible. A criminal conviction can limit options sharply, and in some cases the exact wording of a plea matters.

## Steps to take after receiving an NTA

- Read the notice carefully and note every date
- Do not miss any hearing, and update your address with the immigration court on the required form
- Gather identity documents, immigration papers, and proof of how long you have lived in the United States
- Write down your history, including dates of entry, any arrests, and your family members
- Speak with an immigration attorney as early as possible
- Avoid signing anything you do not understand, especially forms that give up your rights

## Beware of unauthorized help

Immigration fraud is common among people who pose as consultants. Only licensed attorneys and certain accredited representatives can represent you in court. A consultant who promises a result, asks for cash only, or tells you to ignore the NTA is a warning sign.
