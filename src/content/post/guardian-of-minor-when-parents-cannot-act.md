When a child's parents die, become too ill to care for them, or are detained or deported, the law does not automatically hand the child to the nearest relative. Someone has to be appointed guardian of a minor, and how that happens depends on whether the parents planned ahead. A guardian named in a will is a nomination the court will usually honor; a standby guardianship takes effect on a trigger the parent chooses; a petition by a relative with no plan behind it is decided on the child's best interests, often with competing family members in the room. Here is how each route works in Washington, DC, Virginia, and New York, and what to do about the child's money.

## Two jobs: the person and the property

A guardian of the person decides where the child lives, goes to school, and gets medical care. A guardian of the estate (the title varies by state) manages money that belongs to the child. Often the same person holds both, but not always, and the court looks at them separately. A loving aunt who is careless with money might be the right guardian of the person while a trustee or a professional handles the funds. Our [guardianship practice for minors](/practice-area/key-services/legal-guardianship) handles both appointments, and the first question we ask is which one the family actually needs.

## Naming a guardian of a minor in your will

A parent can nominate a guardian for minor children in a will. All three jurisdictions let a parent's nomination guide the court, and judges follow it unless the nominee is unfit or the child's circumstances have changed in a way the parent could not have foreseen. The nomination is not self-executing: after the death, the named person still files with the court (the circuit court in Virginia, the Superior Court in DC, the Surrogate's Court or Family Court in New York), and the court confirms the appointment.

Two cautions. If the other parent is alive and has parental rights, that parent takes precedence over anyone named in a will; a nomination matters most when both parents are gone or the other parent is unfit. And a nomination in a will does nothing while the parent is alive but incapacitated, which is where the next tool comes in. Our [wills and trusts page](/practice-area/key-services/wills-trusts-probate-administration) explains how a guardian clause fits with the rest of a will.

## Standby guardianship for illness or detention

A standby guardianship lets a parent designate, in advance, a person who will step in on a defined trigger, such as the parent's incapacity, death, or hospitalization, without the parent giving up custody today. Virginia, DC, and New York each have a standby guardianship statute. The parent signs a designation; in some versions the court approves it ahead of time, in others the designation is filed after the trigger occurs. Until the trigger, the parent keeps full authority. After it, the standby guardian acts, and the court confirms the appointment within a set period.

The tool was designed for parents with serious or terminal illness. It has become just as important for immigrant parents, because New York extended its statute to parents facing immigration detention or removal, and other jurisdictions have been asked to do the same. A parent in removal proceedings who has not signed anything leaves a child's care to whichever relative gets to the courthouse first, or to the child welfare system. A signed standby designation, with copies in the school file and with the designated guardian, prevents that.

## Petitioning when there is no plan

When nothing was signed, a relative or other adult petitions the court for guardianship. The petition identifies the child, the parents, the reason the parents cannot act, and why the petitioner is suitable. Living parents must be served and may consent or object. If a parent objects, the court will appoint a guardian over that objection only on a finding, such as unfitness or abandonment, that state law requires; the exact standard varies, and this is where contested cases are won or lost. The court may appoint a guardian ad litem for the child and may interview an older child about their preference.

Expect the court to ask about the home, the petitioner's finances, criminal history, and any prior involvement with child protective services. Expect, too, that other relatives may file competing petitions. The sequence in [how to file for guardianship of an adult](/how-to-file-adult-guardianship-petition) is a reasonable guide to the mechanics, though the standards differ for a child.

## Guardianship orders and immigration status

For an immigrant child who has been abused, neglected, or abandoned by a parent, a state guardianship order can be the first step toward lawful status. [Special Immigrant Juvenile Status](/practice-area/key-services/special-immigrant-juvenile-status) requires a state court order placing the child in someone's custody or guardianship and finding that reunification with one or both parents is not viable and that return to the home country is not in the child's best interests. The guardianship petition must be drafted with those findings in mind, and timing is critical: the state court must still have jurisdiction over the child, and the age at which that jurisdiction ends differs between courts, with some stopping at 18 and others continuing to 21. A guardianship filed a month too late cannot be fixed.

## The child's money

Insurance companies, retirement plans, and probate courts will not pay a lump sum to a minor. Without planning, those funds sit in a court-supervised account or require a guardian of the estate who posts a bond and files accountings until the child reaches majority. Two tools avoid that: a trust for minor children written into the parents' wills, with a trustee who holds the money to an age the parents choose, and custodial accounts under the Uniform Transfers to Minors Act for smaller sums. Both should name the same person, or at least compatible people, as the guardian of the person. The basics of these documents are in [what estate planning means in the United States](/what-is-estate-planning-in-the-us).
