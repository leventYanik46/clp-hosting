Every U.S. employer must complete Form I-9 for every new hire, including U.S. citizens. I-9 compliance sounds like simple paperwork, but small errors repeated across a workforce add up to significant fines during a government audit. Most problems are preventable with a clear process and a periodic self-audit.

## The basic I-9 rules

- **Section 1** must be completed by the employee no later than the first day of work.
- **Section 2** must be completed by the employer within three business days of the employee's first day. The employer examines original documents from the Lists of Acceptable Documents.
- **Section 3** is used for reverification when a worker's employment authorization document expires, and for rehires within three years.
- **Retention:** keep each I-9 for three years after the hire date or one year after employment ends, whichever is later.

The employee chooses which acceptable documents to present. Employers can't demand specific documents, such as a green card, or ask for more documents than the form requires. Doing so can violate anti-discrimination rules even when the intent is good.

## Common errors that lead to fines

The errors auditors find most often:

1. **Missing forms.** No I-9 at all for some employees, often because a manager assumed HR handled it.
2. **Late completion.** Section 2 completed weeks after the start date.
3. **Incomplete Section 1.** Missing signatures, dates, or the employee's attestation box.
4. **Wrong documents.** Accepting a List C document alone, or a combination that doesn't satisfy the requirements.
5. **Missed reverification.** Letting a worker continue after their EAD expired without updating Section 3.
6. **Over-documentation.** Asking for extra documents or specific ones, which creates discrimination risk.
7. **Using an outdated form.** Always use the current edition from the USCIS website.

## Remote verification

Since August 2023, employers enrolled in E-Verify and in good standing can use an alternative procedure to examine documents remotely. The employer reviews copies of the documents, conducts a live video interaction with the employee, and keeps copies of the documents. The employer must apply the procedure consistently: you can't offer remote verification only to some employees based on characteristics like citizenship.

Employers not enrolled in E-Verify must still inspect original documents in person, either directly or through an authorized representative.

## How to run an I-9 self-audit

A self-audit is the best way to find and fix problems before the government does. A practical approach:

1. **Gather all I-9s** for current employees and former employees still within the retention period.
2. **Compare the list against payroll** to find employees without forms.
3. **Review each form** for missing information, late dates, and document errors.
4. **Correct errors properly.** Draw a line through the incorrect information, write the correct information, and initial and date the correction. Never backdate or white out entries.
5. **Attach a note** explaining any corrections or late completions.
6. **Discard forms you don't need to keep,** consistently and in line with retention rules.

If you find substantive problems, such as a worker who doesn't appear to be authorized, get legal advice before taking action. Terminating someone based on a mistaken reading of documents can create its own liability.

## Responding to a Notice of Inspection

An I-9 audit usually starts with a Notice of Inspection from Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE). The employer generally has three business days to produce the forms. Do not use that time to create new I-9s for missing employees and backdate them. Instead:

- Contact counsel immediately.
- Gather the forms and supporting documents requested.
- Request a short extension if needed, which ICE may grant.
- Produce what you have, without altering it.

After review, ICE may issue a notice of technical violations, giving time to correct them, or a notice of intent to fine for substantive violations. Fines are assessed per form and can increase for repeat violations.

## Building a compliance process

- Assign one trained person or team to handle I-9s.
- Use an electronic I-9 system with audit trails if you have many hires.
- Calendar EAD expiration dates for reverification.
- Conduct a self-audit at least once a year.
- Train hiring managers on what they can and can't ask employees.

## Rehires and acquisitions

Two situations generate a lot of quiet I-9 errors. When you rehire a former employee within three years of the original I-9, you can use Section 3 instead of a new form, but only if the original form is still on file and the worker remains authorized. When you acquire another company and keep its workforce, you can choose to treat the employees as continuing in employment and keep the seller's I-9s, but you inherit every error on those forms. Many buyers have new hires complete fresh I-9s for that reason, applied consistently to the whole acquired group.
