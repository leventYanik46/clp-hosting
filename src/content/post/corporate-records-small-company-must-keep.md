The corporate records a small company must keep fit in one binder or one well-organized folder, and the list has not changed much in decades: the formation documents, the governing documents, the record of who owns the company, the record of what its directors and owners decided, and the periodic filings that keep it in good standing. Companies that keep these current close financings and sales without drama. Companies that do not spend the first week of any transaction reconstructing history. Capitol Law Partners' [corporate governance team](/practice-area/key-services/corporate-governance-and-internal-policies) sees both, and the difference is rarely about size; it is about habit.

## Formation and state filings

Keep the filed articles of incorporation (or certificate of incorporation, or articles of organization for an LLC) with the state's stamp or acknowledgment, every amendment, and the receipt or confirmation for each. Keep the IRS letter assigning the employer identification number and any tax election filed with the IRS, such as Form 2553 for S corporation status or Form 8832 for an entity classification election.

Add the foreign qualification certificate for every other state where the company is registered to do business, and the periodic report or fee confirmation each state requires. Virginia corporations file an annual report with the State Corporation Commission and Virginia LLCs pay an annual registration fee; DC companies file a biennial report; New York corporations and LLCs file a biennial statement. Missing these leads to loss of good standing, and reinstatement is a step a buyer or lender will notice. Federal beneficial ownership reporting requirements have changed more than once; confirm the current rule for your entity type as of the date you read this.

## Governing documents

Keep the bylaws or operating agreement, every amendment, and any shareholder agreement, buy-sell agreement, voting agreement, or investor rights agreement. These documents define who decides what. When they conflict with how the company actually operates, that is a governance problem to fix rather than a filing problem, and our post on [when to update your bylaws or operating agreement](/when-to-update-bylaws-operating-agreement) explains the triggers.

If the company has adopted written policies, such as a conflict of interest policy, a delegation of authority schedule, or an employee handbook, keep the adopted version with the resolution that adopted it and a log of revisions.

## Ownership records

For a corporation, the stock ledger lists every issuance, transfer, and cancellation of shares: the holder, the number and class of shares, the date, and the consideration paid. Keep copies of stock certificates or, for uncertificated shares, the notices sent to holders. Keep every subscription agreement, option grant, warrant, convertible note, and SAFE, because each is a claim on equity that a buyer will count.

For an LLC, the equivalent is a membership interest schedule, usually an exhibit to the operating agreement, showing each member's percentage or units and capital contributions. Many small LLCs never update this schedule after formation, which makes a later transfer or admission of a new member difficult to document.

Keep the capitalization table reconciled to these records. When the cap table in a spreadsheet says one thing and the ledger and grant documents say another, the ledger and documents control, and resolving the difference during diligence is painful.

## Minutes, consents, and resolutions

Record every board and owner action in minutes (for meetings) or written consents (for action without a meeting). At a minimum, a small corporation should have an annual shareholder consent electing directors and an annual board consent electing officers, plus resolutions for the decisions listed in our post on [when a company needs a board resolution](/when-do-you-need-board-resolution): equity issuances, borrowing, major contracts, related-party transactions, and anything a bank asks for.

File resolutions chronologically with the documents they approved. A resolution approving a loan should sit next to the loan agreement, or at least reference it by date and amount, so that a reader years later can match the approval to the act.

## Contracts, licenses, and compliance records

The corporate records in the narrow sense stop at the minute book, but a company that is organized for transactions keeps the next layer in the same system: signed copies of material contracts, leases, loan documents, and insurance policies; business licenses and permits with their renewal dates; IP registrations and assignment agreements; and employment-related documents, including offer letters, invention assignments, and the handbook. This is the set a buyer requests on day one of diligence, as described in our post on [what due diligence covers when buying a business](/due-diligence-checklist-buying-a-business), and having it ready shortens the process.

## Why these records protect the owners

Limited liability depends on the company being treated as separate from its owners. When a creditor asks a court to disregard the entity and reach the owners personally (piercing the corporate veil), the court looks at factors that include whether the company observed its own formalities, kept records, held required meetings, kept its finances separate from the owners' finances, and was adequately capitalized. The standards vary by state and no single missing document decides the question, but a company with no minutes, no ledger, and commingled accounts has given the creditor its best argument.

Good records also matter in disputes among owners. When a minority member claims the majority acted without authority, the written consents answer the question. When an estate claims a decedent owned more than the ledger shows, the subscription agreements answer it. Our post on [what corporate law covers](/what-is-corporate-law) gives the broader context; the records are where those rules become evidence.
