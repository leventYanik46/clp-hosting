The R-1 visa was never designed to lead anywhere by itself. It expires after five years, and the only green card category built for the same work is the EB-4 special immigrant religious worker classification, filed on Form I-360. Moving from R-1 to green card through EB-4 is a well-worn path, but it has two features that catch people out: a two-year experience requirement that must be satisfied before the I-360 is filed, and a visa availability problem that in recent years has pushed the finish line past the R-1 limit for some workers. Both are manageable if the case is started early.

## Who qualifies for the EB-4 religious worker category

The special immigrant religious worker classification covers ministers and, under a separate program, workers in religious vocations and religious occupations. The eligibility tests look like R-1's: the worker must have been a member of the denomination for at least two years immediately before filing, must be coming to work at least 35 hours per week in a qualifying religious position for a bona fide nonprofit religious organization in the United States or an affiliated organization, and the position must be compensated or fall within the narrow missionary self-support exception. Note the hours: EB-4 requires full-time work, 35 hours a week, where R-1 accepts 20.

The ministerial category is permanent in the statute. The non-minister category, covering religious vocations and occupations, has always carried a sunset date that Congress has extended repeatedly, sometimes with gaps. As of this writing, anyone relying on the non-minister program should confirm its current status before filing and before timing an adjustment application around it. ## The two-year experience rule

The I-360 requires that the worker has been performing the religious work continuously, either abroad or in the United States, for at least two years immediately before the petition is filed. The prior work must have been qualifying religious work, though it need not match the new position exactly, and if performed in the United States it must have been authorized; unlawful work does not count. For work in the United States, compensation during those two years must be proved with IRS documentation such as W-2s or tax returns, or for non-salaried workers, with evidence of the support actually received. Work abroad is proved with comparable records. Breaks are allowed only for further religious training or sabbaticals that did not exceed two years, and only if the worker remained a member of the denomination throughout.

For a worker who entered on R-1, this usually means the I-360 cannot be filed until roughly two years into the R-1 period, and only if the employer has actually paid what the R-1 petition promised. The documents that prove the R-1 employment, described in [R-1 visa documents the employer must provide](/r-1-visa-documents-employer-checklist), are the same ones the I-360 will need.

## How the I-360 is filed

The employer files Form I-360 with USCIS, with an attestation similar to the R-1 attestation, the organization's IRS determination letter and denominational evidence, proof of the worker's membership and experience, and proof of the ability to pay. USCIS may conduct a site inspection of the employer, as it does for R-1 petitions. Unlike most employment-based categories, the religious worker I-360 cannot be filed concurrently with the Form I-485 adjustment application; the I-360 must be approved first. That sequencing adds time, and it is the main reason the case should start as early as the experience rule allows.

Once the I-360 is approved and a visa number is available, the worker either files Form I-485 to adjust status in the United States or completes consular processing abroad. Either way, the family (spouse and unmarried children under 21) can immigrate as derivatives on the same petition.

## The visa availability problem

EB-4 is a single category shared by religious workers and several other groups of special immigrants, and in recent years demand has produced a waiting line on the Visa Bulletin for all countries. When the category is backlogged, an approved I-360 does not allow the worker to file the I-485 until the priority date (the I-360 filing date) becomes current. That is the collision point with the R-1 limit: a worker whose priority date is not current by the end of the fifth year has no way to extend R-1 and no basis to adjust. The mechanics of priority dates and the Visa Bulletin are explained in [priority date vs filing date](/priority-date-vs-filing-date).

There is no easy fix. The practical responses are to file the I-360 at the earliest possible date, to keep travel records so that any argument for extending the R-1 period is available (see [R-1 maximum stay and extensions](/r-1-visa-maximum-stay-extensions)), and to look at whether the worker can hold another status, such as a cap-exempt H-1B through an affiliated school or an F-1 for further study, while the priority date moves. A worker who must leave after five years keeps the I-360 and the priority date and can consular process when a number becomes available, but the family's life is disrupted in the meantime. The right answer depends on the denomination, the position, and the worker's other qualifications, and is a conversation worth having in year one rather than year four. The [business and employment immigration overview](/practice-area/key-services/business-employment-immigration-solutions) describes the other employment-based categories that may be in play.

## Does filing for a green card hurt the R-1?

Not by itself. The statutory definition of the R category does not require the worker to keep a residence abroad, and filing an I-360 does not end R-1 status. A worker can hold R-1 status and have a pending I-360 at the same time. Consular officers may still ask about plans, and the worker should answer truthfully: the intention is to complete the authorized R-1 period and to immigrate through the lawful channel if and when it becomes available.
