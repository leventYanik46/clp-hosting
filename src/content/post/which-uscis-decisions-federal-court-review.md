A federal court can review a USCIS denial that says you did not prove eligibility. It generally cannot review a decision Congress left to the agency's discretion, and the Supreme Court has recently confirmed that revoking an approved petition falls on the unreviewable side of that line. Before anyone spends money on a lawsuit, the first question is whether the decision is one of the USCIS decisions a federal court can review at all. The answer depends on which statute governs the decision, what the agency actually decided, and where the case sits procedurally. ## The starting point: final agency action

The Administrative Procedure Act allows review of final agency action for which there is no other adequate remedy in court. A denial is final. A request for evidence, a notice of intent to deny or a pending case is not; for those the remedy is a response, or if the case has stalled, a delay suit as described in [how courts decide whether a USCIS delay is unreasonable](/how-courts-decide-uscis-delay-unreasonable). Approvals are not reviewable by the beneficiary, who has nothing to complain about, and in most circumstances not by anyone else.

## USCIS decisions a federal court can usually review

Denials based on eligibility requirements set by statute or regulation are the heart of reviewable territory. When USCIS denies an I-140 because it finds the beneficiary lacks extraordinary ability, denies an I-129 because the position is not a specialty occupation, or denies an I-130 because it finds a marriage not bona fide, it is applying legal standards to facts, and a court may ask whether it did so lawfully. The Supreme Court in Kucana v. Holder made clear that the statutory bar on reviewing discretionary decisions reaches only discretion conferred by statute, not discretion the agency gave itself by regulation, which keeps most eligibility determinations open to review.

Naturalization has its own generous provisions. A denied applicant who completes the N-336 hearing may seek de novo review in district court, where the judge decides eligibility fresh, and an applicant whose case has sat 120 days past the interview may ask the court to step in.

## Decisions Congress shielded from review

Section 1252(a)(2)(B) of Title 8 strips courts of jurisdiction over two categories: judgments regarding certain forms of relief, including adjustment of status and cancellation of removal, and any other decision or action the statute specifies to be in the discretion of the Secretary of Homeland Security or the Attorney General.

The second category is where petition revocations fall. The revocation statute says the Secretary may revoke approval of a petition for what the Secretary deems good and sufficient cause. In Bouarfa v. Mayorkas, decided in late 2024, a unanimous Supreme Court held that this language makes revocation discretionary and therefore beyond judicial review, even when the revocation rests on a ground that would have been reviewable had it been the basis of an initial denial. For employers and families with approved petitions, the practical lesson is that the fight over a revocation happens at the notice of intent to revoke stage, in the response to USCIS, not later in court.

The first category creates a harder question for adjustment of status denials issued by USCIS outside removal proceedings. Courts have divided on whether the bar covers those denials or only decisions made in removal proceedings, and the Supreme Court's reading of the provision in Patel v. Garland, which barred review of factual findings underlying discretionary relief in removal cases, has strengthened the government's hand. Treat review of an I-485 denial as uncertain and venue-dependent until counsel has checked the law in your circuit.

## Consular decisions

Visa refusals by consular officers are not USCIS decisions, but the question arises constantly. The doctrine of consular nonreviewability means courts will not review the merits of a refusal. What may be reviewable is an officer's failure to decide at all, which is the theory behind suits over [221(g) administrative processing](/mandamus-221g-administrative-processing).

## Removal proceedings change the forum

If your denial happened in or is bound up with removal proceedings, the district court is usually the wrong place. Review of a removal order and of most decisions made within it goes through the Board of Immigration Appeals and then a petition for review in the court of appeals, where constitutional claims and questions of law remain reviewable even for discretionary relief. ## A working checklist

- Identify the statute under which USCIS acted and whether its text uses discretionary language.
- Ask whether the decision is a denial of eligibility or a discretionary judgment on a case that was otherwise eligible.
- Check whether the decision is a revocation; if so, the court path is almost certainly closed.
- Determine whether removal proceedings are pending, which moves the case to the petition-for-review track.
- Confirm the decision is final and that the record contains your legal arguments, since review is confined to [the administrative record](/administrative-record-immigration-lawsuit).
