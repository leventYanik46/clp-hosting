The three EB-1 subcategories share a preference level, a form and an exemption from PERM labor certification, and almost nothing else. Choosing among EB-1A vs EB-1B vs EB-1C is really a choice about who petitions, what USCIS tests, and how much your case depends on one employer. Pick the wrong one and you can spend months documenting acclaim you did not need, or filing through an employer when you could have filed alone. Our [EB-1 first preference green card](/practice-area/key-services/eb-1-green-card) page summarizes the three; this post works through the decision.

## Who petitions: the first filter

The EB-1A is the only EB-1 subcategory you can file yourself. Form I-140 lists you as petitioner and beneficiary, and no employer, job offer or labor certification is required, though you must show you will continue working in your field in the United States.

The EB-1B is filed by an employer: a university or institution of higher education offering a tenured, tenure-track or comparable permanent research position, or a private employer whose research department employs at least three full-time researchers and has documented accomplishments in the field.

The EB-1C is filed by a U.S. company that has been doing business for at least one year and is the parent, subsidiary, affiliate or branch of the foreign company where you worked. If no qualifying employer will sign, EB-1A is your only EB-1 route, regardless of how strong your management record is.

## What USCIS tests in EB-1A vs EB-1B vs EB-1C

### EB-1A: the very top of the field

You must show sustained national or international acclaim and a record placing you among the small percentage at the very top of the field, through a major internationally recognized award or at least three of ten criteria, followed by a final merits determination under Kazarian v. USCIS. Acclaim is the whole case. See our [EB-1A extraordinary ability](/practice-area/key-services/eb-1a-green-card) page for the criteria.

### EB-1B: international recognition as outstanding

You must be recognized internationally as outstanding in a specific academic area, have at least three years of teaching or research experience, and document at least two of six criteria, again followed by a final merits review. The standard is lower than EB-1A, but the category is limited to academics and researchers, and the job offer is part of the test. For a researcher deciding between the two, our post on [EB-1B vs EB-1A for researchers](/eb-1b-vs-eb-1a-researchers) goes deeper.

### EB-1C: corporate relationship and duties

Acclaim is irrelevant. You must have worked abroad for the related company in a managerial or executive capacity for at least one year in the three years before transfer (or before your most recent admission to the United States, if you are already working here for the same employer), and the U.S. position must also be managerial or executive. The case turns on organizational charts, the qualifying relationship between the entities, and whether your duties are genuinely about managing people or a function rather than doing the work yourself. Our post on [EB-1C requirements](/eb-1c-multinational-manager-requirements) sets out the tests, and the [EB-1C green card](/practice-area/key-services/eb-1c-green-card) page covers the category.

## Portability and dependence on the employer

This is where petitioners most often misjudge the tradeoff.

- An approved EB-1A belongs to you. You can change jobs, change cities, or work for yourself, so long as you remain in your field.
- An approved EB-1B or EB-1C belongs to the employer. Before approval, the employer can withdraw it. Once it has been approved for 180 days, a withdrawal no longer automatically revokes it and you keep the priority date, but a new employer cannot use it to finish your green card unless your Form I-485 has been pending for at least 180 days and the new job is the same or similar.
- Both employer-filed categories require the employer to prove ability to pay the offered wage from the priority date onward.

If you expect to change employers before the green card is issued, that weighs toward EB-1A or toward pairing an employer petition with a self-petition.

## Evidence burden and risk

The EB-1A has the highest denial risk because the final merits standard is demanding, and because many petitioners file on a record that is good rather than extraordinary. The EB-1B fails most often on the job offer: a postdoctoral appointment with an end date, or a private employer that cannot document three full-time researchers. The EB-1C fails most often on duties: a manager who supervises no professional staff or whose day is spent on operational tasks, or a small U.S. office whose structure does not support an executive role. Documenting the right thing for the right category is most of the work.

## A simple decision path

Ask the questions in this order. Does a multinational employer that you managed for abroad want to transfer you into a managerial or executive role here? If yes, EB-1C, and acclaim never enters the picture. Has a university or research employer offered you a permanent research or tenure-track position, and do you have three years of experience? If yes, EB-1B, often paired with a self-petition. Otherwise, or if you want a petition that survives a job change, test your record against the EB-1A criteria honestly and consider the EB-2 National Interest Waiver as the alternative or the companion filing.
