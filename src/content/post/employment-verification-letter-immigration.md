An employment verification letter for immigration confirms where someone works, what they do, and what they are paid. USCIS, consulates, and schools rely on these letters in H-1B extensions, green card cases, F-1 OPT reporting, and naturalization. A vague letter is one of the most common, and most avoidable, reasons for a request for evidence.

## What every employment letter should include

- **Company letterhead** with address, phone number, and website
- **Date** the letter was signed
- **Employee's full legal name**, matching the passport and immigration documents
- **Job title** and the start date of employment
- **Employment status**: full time or part time, with hours per week
- **Salary or wage**, stated as an annual figure or hourly rate
- **Duties**, described specifically enough to show the nature of the work
- **Worksite address**, especially if different from the company headquarters
- **Signer's name, title, and contact information**, with a handwritten or valid electronic signature

## Letters for specific situations

### F-1 OPT and STEM OPT

Students on OPT often need a letter confirming that the job is related to their field of study. For STEM OPT, the letter should line up with the training plan on Form I-983. Include the supervisor's name and how the role uses the student's degree. A letter that does not connect the work to the degree can raise problems with SEVP compliance.

### H-1B extensions and transfers

USCIS may ask for proof that the worker has been employed as stated in the prior petition. The letter should confirm the job, wage, and worksite match the LCA. If anything changed, address it with counsel before writing the letter.

### Green card cases

For the I-485, a job offer letter confirms the sponsored job is still available. For PERM and I-140, experience letters from past employers prove the worker met the job's requirements. Those experience letters need specific dates, full-time status, and detailed duties showing the skills listed in the PERM.

### Naturalization

N-400 applicants may be asked for employment history. A letter helps fill gaps and supports continuous residence.

## Who should sign

The letter should come from someone with authority and direct knowledge: an HR manager, a direct supervisor, or a company officer. For former employers, an HR department letter is usually best. If a former employer no longer exists or refuses to cooperate, a sworn statement from a former supervisor or coworker, plus pay records, can sometimes substitute.

## Mistakes that cause RFEs

- Missing dates or listing only "currently employed"
- Duties written as a generic job posting rather than the actual work
- Salary omitted or inconsistent with tax records
- Unsigned letters or letters on plain paper
- Inconsistencies between the letter and other filings

## A note for employers

Keep a template that HR can adapt, and route immigration-related letters through someone who understands what each type of case needs. A letter that overstates duties can create problems as serious as one that understates them.


## Frequently asked questions

### Can I write my own employment letter?

No. The letter must come from the employer. You can give HR a draft or a list of what the case needs, but they must review and sign it.

### Does the letter need to be notarized?

Usually not. A signed letter on letterhead is standard. Some foreign employers' experience letters are supported with a sworn declaration if company records are limited.

### How recent should the letter be?

Ideally dated within a few weeks of filing. Older letters may prompt USCIS to ask whether the facts are still current.

### What if my former employer went out of business?

Gather what you can: pay stubs, tax records, offer letters, and performance reviews. Add a sworn declaration from a former supervisor or coworker who has firsthand knowledge of your role and dates. Explain the situation in your filing.
