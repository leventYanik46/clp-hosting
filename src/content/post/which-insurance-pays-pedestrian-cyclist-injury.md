Four different policies can pay for a pedestrian or cyclist injury, and they pay in a particular order that most injured people learn only after they have already used the wrong one. The driver's liability policy pays for fault. Your own auto policy pays when the driver is uninsured, underinsured, or gone, even though you were not in a car. New York's no-fault system pays medical bills and lost wages before fault is decided. Health insurance pays in the meantime and then asks for its money back. Knowing which insurance pays for a pedestrian or cyclist injury, and in what sequence, is the core of the work CLP's [pedestrian and cyclist injury practice](/practice-area/key-services/pedestrian-and-bicycle-accidents) does in the first month of a claim.

## The driver's liability policy

The driver's bodily injury liability coverage is the primary source of compensation in Virginia and DC, and the source for pain and suffering in New York. It pays only if the driver was at fault, up to the policy limit, and only when the claim is settled or won. Every state in this region requires minimum liability limits, and the minimums are low enough that a serious injury will exceed them. The adjuster works for the driver, not for you. A fast call asking for a recorded statement is an effort to lock in a version of events before you have seen the crash report, and you can decline it.

## Your own auto policy, even if you were walking

If you or a relative in your household has an auto policy, read it before you do anything else. Uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage applies to the named insured and resident relatives while they are pedestrians or cyclists, not just while they are driving. It pays when the driver has no insurance, when the driver's limits are below your damages, and in hit-and-run cases, which we explain in [how pedestrians get compensated after a hit-and-run](/hit-and-run-pedestrian-compensation). Virginia policies carry uninsured motorist coverage matching the liability limits unless the owner chose less, DC requires it, and New York requires it with optional supplementary limits that are worth buying.

Virginia policies often include medical expense coverage, which pays medical bills regardless of fault and, under Virginia law, cannot be clawed back from your injury recovery. If that coverage exists in your household, use it first.

## New York no-fault for pedestrians and cyclists

New York treats a pedestrian or cyclist struck by a motor vehicle as a covered person under that vehicle's no-fault policy. The vehicle's insurer pays medical bills and a portion of lost wages up to the basic policy limit regardless of who caused the crash. The application must be filed within 30 days of the crash, a deadline that trips up people who are still in the hospital. If the vehicle was uninsured or unknown, the no-fault claim goes to a household policy, and if none exists, to the state's MVAIC fund.

No-fault pays the bills but not pain and suffering. To sue the driver for that, New York requires a "serious injury" as defined by statute, a threshold that includes fractures, significant disfigurement, permanent loss or limitation of use of a body part or system, and injuries that prevent usual daily activities for most of the first 180 days. Soft-tissue injuries without objective findings often fail this test, which is why early imaging and consistent treatment matter in New York claims.

## DC's optional no-fault and the election trap

The District runs a hybrid system. Drivers can buy personal injury protection, and a pedestrian struck in DC may be offered PIP benefits under the driver's policy. Accepting those benefits can limit your right to sue the driver for pain and suffering unless the injury meets statutory thresholds, and the election must be made within a short window after the crash. Do not elect PIP in DC without advice, because the choice can cost far more than the bills it pays.

## Health insurance, liens, and who gets paid back

Health insurance pays the hospital in the weeks after a crash and then asserts a right to be repaid from any settlement. How much it can take depends on the plan and the state. Virginia law bars most health insurers from asserting a lien against a personal injury recovery, and New York bars most of them too, but self-funded employer plans governed by ERISA, Medicare, and Medicaid are exceptions that can reach your settlement. Workers' compensation applies instead if you were on the job, including delivery cyclists, and it has its own repayment right. Resolving these liens is part of settling the case, and ignoring them is how a settlement check shrinks after it arrives.

## Deadlines for every insurance that pays a pedestrian or cyclist injury

Lawsuit deadlines are two years in Virginia and three years in DC and New York. Policy deadlines are shorter: notice to your own insurer "promptly," the New York no-fault application within 30 days, MVAIC notice within 90 days of a hit-and-run, and government notices within 90 days to six months when a public road or vehicle is involved, which we cover in [can you sue the city for a pedestrian or bike crash](/sue-the-city-pedestrian-bike-crash).
