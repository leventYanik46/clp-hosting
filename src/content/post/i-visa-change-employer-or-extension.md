An I visa is issued for work with the specific foreign media organization you named at the consular interview. If you move to a different outlet, even another foreign one, you need a new I visa before you work for the new employer; the State Department's position is that a change of employer requires a new visa application, and there is no in-country substitute for that step. Extensions are a separate question and depend entirely on what your I-94 says: duration of status needs nothing, a date needs a timely Form I-539.

## Why the employer is part of the status

In petition-based categories such as the H-1B, the employer files a petition and a change of employer means a new petition. The I visa has no petition, so the employer letter and the DS-160 serve that role, and the visa itself is the record of which organization you represent. The admission at the border is to work for that organization. Working for a different one is outside the terms of admission, and immigration officers treat it as unauthorized employment regardless of whether the new outlet is also foreign media.

The practical sequence is to finish the current assignment, obtain the new employer's letter and contract, and apply for a new I visa at a consulate, usually in your home country or a third-country post that accepts applicants. Our [I visa](/practice-area/key-services/i-visa) page describes the documents a renewal interview needs, which are largely the same as the first application with the new employer substituted. Applying from inside the United States is not possible; consulates do not issue visas domestically.

Journalists who hold concurrent engagements should plan ahead. If you will represent two foreign outlets at once, both should be disclosed at the original interview and both employer letters presented, so the admission covers both. Adding a second outlet later means a new visa. Our post on [how to apply for an I visa](/i-visa-application-steps-documents) covers how to present multiple employers in one application.

## Side work for U.S. outlets

A correspondent on an I visa who accepts a paid contribution to a U.S. publication, appears as a paid commentator on a U.S. network, or takes a staff role at a U.S. bureau of a U.S. outlet is working without authorization. The I visa covers foreign media only. The fix is a separate work authorization from the U.S. employer, which in practice means an O-1 or H-1B petition, or declining the work. Unpaid contributions are a grey area; the safer reading is that any productive work for a U.S. entity on an assignment basis needs authorization. The comparison in our post on [I visa versus B-1 versus O-1 for media work](/i-visa-vs-b1-vs-o1-media) explains the categories a U.S. employer can use.

Family members on dependent I visas may not work for anyone. A spouse who is also a journalist needs an I visa in his or her own right, with an employer letter, rather than dependent status.

## Extensions: read your I-94 first

How long you may stay is set at the port of entry, not by the visa validity date. As of this writing, I visa holders have generally been admitted for duration of status (D/S), meaning for as long as the assignment with the named employer continues; DHS has proposed replacing D/S with fixed admission periods for the I, F, and J categories, so check the rule in force when you travel. Look up your electronic I-94 after each entry.

- If the I-94 shows D/S, no extension filing is needed while you continue with the same employer. Keep an updated employer letter on hand to show the assignment is ongoing.
- If the I-94 shows a date, you must file Form I-539 with USCIS before that date, with a new employer letter confirming the assignment continues and the current USCIS filing fee. A timely filing keeps you in a period of authorized stay while USCIS decides. Dependents are included on the same form or a supplement.

An expired visa stamp with a valid admission is fine while you remain inside the United States. The stamp matters only for re-entry: if your visa has expired and you travel, you must obtain a new one abroad before returning, even though your status was valid when you left. Our [change and extension of status](/practice-area/key-services/change-and-extension-of-status) page covers the general I-539 rules, including what travel during a pending request does.

## Changing to a different category

I visa holders can change status inside the United States to another nonimmigrant category if they qualify and file before their authorized stay ends: an O-1 petition from a U.S. employer, an F-1 for a degree program, or H-1B through an employer that wins the lottery or is cap-exempt. The I category is not dual intent, so filing an immigrant petition while in I status can affect re-entry if you travel, though it does not by itself end the status. Journalists with a long record often qualify for O-1 or for an EB-1A or EB-2 NIW green card, and the path from I status to [an O-1 based on extraordinary ability](/o-1-visa-extraordinary-ability) is one we map regularly.

## Ending the assignment

When the assignment ends and you have no new basis to stay, depart promptly. There is no statutory grace period for the I category comparable to the 60 days some employment categories receive, so treat the end of the assignment as the end of the status and plan travel accordingly. Overstaying a dated I-94 starts the unlawful presence clock; overstaying a D/S admission does not until a formal finding is made, but it still damages future applications.
