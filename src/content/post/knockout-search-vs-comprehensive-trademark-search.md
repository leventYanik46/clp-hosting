A knockout search answers one question: is this name obviously taken? A comprehensive trademark search answers a harder one: is this name safe to build a brand on? The first takes hours and looks only at the USPTO database. The second takes days, reaches sources the USPTO never sees and ends in a written opinion. Most naming projects need both, in that order, and the mistake we see most often at CLP's [trademark clearance search service](/practice-area/key-services/trademark-search-and-clearance) is treating a clean knockout result as clearance.

## What a knockout search covers

A knockout search reviews federal applications and registrations for marks identical or very close to yours on related goods and services. It includes pending applications, which matter because an earlier-filed application will be cited against yours even though it has not registered. A good knockout search also checks obvious phonetic equivalents and the dominant word of the mark on its own.

Its job is elimination. If a direct hit exists, the name is dead and no further money should be spent on it. Because it is quick and inexpensive, a knockout search is the right tool for a list of five or ten candidate names, and it typically removes most of them. Our guide to [how to search for a trademark](/how-to-search-for-a-trademark) describes how to run a reasonable version of this yourself.

What a knockout search cannot do is confirm availability. It misses state registrations, unregistered users, design marks that conflict with your logo, and the long tail of spelling and translation variants that an examining attorney will find with better tools and more time.

## What a comprehensive trademark search adds

A comprehensive search is usually compiled by a professional search vendor and then analyzed by an attorney. It reaches well beyond the federal register.

- Federal records searched with full variant logic: phonetic equivalents, alternate spellings, foreign-language equivalents, truncations and design codes.
- State trademark registrations in all fifty states.
- Common-law sources: business directories, trade publications, press databases, product listings and industry databases.
- Business entity and assumed-name filings with state corporations offices.
- Domain name registrations and active websites.
- Social media handles, app stores and marketplace storefronts.

The report that results can run to hundreds of pages. The value is not the page count but the reading. An attorney goes through the hits, discards the irrelevant ones, flags the marks that an examiner or a competitor would raise, and writes the analysis up as an availability opinion. Our post on [what a trademark availability opinion tells you](/trademark-availability-opinion-explained) describes that document.

## Which search you need at each stage

During brainstorming, run knockout searches. They keep the creative process honest and prevent the team from falling in love with a name that is plainly unavailable.

Before you commit to a finalist, order a comprehensive search. Commitment means any of the following: filing a [USPTO trademark application](/practice-area/key-services/trademark-and-intellectual-property-registration), buying the domain, designing packaging, announcing the name publicly or signing a lease with the name on it. The cost of the search is small next to any of those.

Order a fresh comprehensive search when the brand expands into new goods or services, since a name cleared for software was never cleared for apparel, and when a logo is adopted or redesigned, since design elements are searched separately from words. A rebrand after an acquisition deserves the same treatment as a new name.

## What neither search can guarantee

No search finds every unregistered use. A business with a single storefront and no web presence can still hold common-law rights in its area. An application can be filed the day after your search. And the analysis involves judgment: reasonable attorneys can disagree about whether two marks on neighboring goods are likely to confuse, and the examining attorney assigned to your file may see it differently from either of them. A comprehensive search reduces risk to a level you can decide on with open eyes. It does not remove it, and an honest opinion says so.

## The cost and timing tradeoff

A knockout search is cheap enough to run on every candidate. A comprehensive search costs more and takes longer because the report is compiled from many sources and then analyzed mark by mark, so it is reserved for the names you are ready to adopt. Set against the price of a [trademark office action response](/practice-area/key-services/trademark-office-action-responses) after a refusal, an opposition proceeding, or a rebrand after launch, it is the least expensive step in the process. Build it into the naming timeline rather than treating it as a formality the week before filing.
