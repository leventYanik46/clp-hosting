You can usually start working for your new employer as soon as USCIS receives the H-1B transfer petition, not when it is approved. That rule, called portability, is the reason an H-1B transfer is a very different experience from the first H-1B filing.

## How the H-1B transfer process works

A transfer means your new employer files a new Form I-129 petition asking USCIS to let you work for them in H-1B status. The steps look like this:

1. **The new employer files a Labor Condition Application (LCA)** with the Department of Labor for the new job and worksite.
2. **The employer files the I-129 petition** with USCIS, marked as a change of employer, along with supporting evidence.
3. **USCIS issues a receipt notice** once the filing is accepted. This is the date portability kicks in.
4. **USCIS decides the case**, either approving it, denying it, or sending a Request for Evidence.

There is no new lottery. If you were counted against the cap in the past, you generally do not need to go through selection again.

## Who can use portability

Portability depends on being in valid H-1B status when the new petition is filed and not having worked without authorization. It is also affected by how your prior employment ended and whether any earlier petition had problems. If you are between jobs or your status is close to expiring, timing matters more than usual, and this is where an H-1B transfer lawyer earns the fee.

## How long does an H-1B transfer take

Timelines move around with USCIS workload, the service center that gets the case, and whether you choose premium processing. Regular processing can take months. Premium processing, available for a separate fee, gives a fixed response window that USCIS publishes on its website. In either case, you can generally work for the new employer from the receipt date, which is why many people choose not to rush a decision.

If USCIS sends an RFE, the clock effectively pauses until you respond and USCIS reviews your answer.

## Risks while you wait

Portability protects your ability to work, but it does not guarantee approval. If the transfer is later denied, the right to work for the new employer ends. Before leaving a job, consider:

- **Whether the new job qualifies as a specialty occupation.** A change in duties or wage level can draw a closer look.
- **Travel.** Leaving the country while a transfer is pending can complicate re-entry, since you will need a valid visa stamp for the new employer or other evidence.
- **Gaps.** Time out of status between employers can break portability.
- **Green card timing.** If your employer has started the PERM process for you, a transfer can affect that path, so talk through the order of events first.

## Planning the move

Give your new employer's attorney a complete copy of your immigration history: prior approval notices, I-94 records, recent pay stubs and your degree documents. Cleaner records mean fewer surprises.

If you are weighing an offer and not sure whether a transfer is the right move, ask for a consultation before you resign.
