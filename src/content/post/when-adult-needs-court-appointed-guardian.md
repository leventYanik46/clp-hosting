An adult needs a court-appointed guardian when two things are true at the same time. First, the person can no longer make or communicate responsible decisions about their own health, safety, or living situation. Second, no less restrictive arrangement, such as a power of attorney, an advance directive, or a supported decision-making agreement, can close that gap. A diagnosis by itself does not get you there. Courts in Washington, DC, Virginia, and New York all apply a functional test, and the petitioner must prove the case by clear and convincing evidence. If you are weighing whether to petition for a parent, a spouse, or an adult child, the questions below are the ones the judge will ask.

## What incapacity means to the court

Every jurisdiction words the standard a little differently, but the substance is the same. The court looks at what the person can and cannot do, not at what label a doctor has applied.

Virginia asks whether the adult is unable to receive and evaluate information, or to respond to people and events, to such an extent that they cannot meet the essential requirements for their health, care, safety, or therapeutic needs without a guardian. New York's Article 81 adds a second element: the person must be likely to suffer harm because of those limitations, and must be unable to understand and appreciate the nature and consequences of the inability. The District of Columbia follows a similar functional definition in its intervention proceedings.

In practice, this means a parent who forgets names but still refuses a nursing home for reasons they can explain is probably not incapacitated in the legal sense. A parent who leaves the stove on, cannot say what medications they take, and does not recognize that a stranger has moved into the house probably is. Our [legal guardianship practice](/practice-area/key-services/legal-guardianship) starts every matter with this functional picture, because it is what the evaluation report and the hearing will turn on.

## When a court-appointed guardian is the right tool

- Dementia or another progressive condition has advanced past the point where the person can direct their own care, and no advance directive or health care power of attorney was signed while they could.
- A young adult with an intellectual or developmental disability is turning 18 and will need someone with legal authority to consent to treatment, sign educational and benefits paperwork, and deal with providers. New York handles many of these cases under Article 17-A of the Surrogate's Court Procedure Act rather than Article 81.
- A brain injury, stroke, or severe psychiatric episode has left the person unable to consent to treatment or to leave a hospital safely, and the family disagrees about what should happen next.
- The person is being isolated or exploited by someone who now controls access to them, and only a court order will give a trusted family member the standing to intervene.

Notice that most of these are about personal decisions: medical consent, residence, daily care. If the main problem is money, unpaid bills, or someone draining an account, the right petition is usually a [conservatorship over the person's finances](/practice-area/key-services/conservatorship), either alone or alongside a guardianship. Virginia and DC treat the two as separate appointments; New York folds both into a single Article 81 guardian of the person and property.

## The alternatives the judge will ask about

Each of the three jurisdictions requires the court to consider whether something less than a full guardianship will do. Expect the petition, the guardian ad litem or court evaluator, and the judge to walk through these.

A durable power of attorney and an advance medical directive, if the person signed them while they still had capacity, usually make a guardianship unnecessary. We compare the two tools in [guardianship vs power of attorney](/guardianship-vs-power-of-attorney). The catch is timing: once capacity is gone, these documents can no longer be signed.

Default surrogate statutes let a spouse, adult child, or other close relative make medical decisions for a patient who cannot, without any court involvement. Virginia's Health Care Decisions Act, the DC Health-Care Decisions Act, and New York's Family Health Care Decisions Act each set out a priority list. For routine treatment decisions in a hospital or nursing home, this list often does the job.

Supported decision-making agreements, which DC now recognizes by statute, let the person keep legal authority while naming supporters who help them understand options and communicate choices.

A representative payee appointed by the Social Security Administration can manage benefit income without a court order at all.

If one of these covers the actual problem, a judge is likely to deny or narrow the petition. If none of them does, that gap is the core of your case, and the petition should say so plainly.

## Limited versus full guardianship

Even when a guardian is needed, the court is supposed to grant only the powers the evidence supports. A limited guardianship might cover medical consent and placement decisions while leaving the person free to manage a small allowance, choose friends, or decide how to spend the day. Every right the order does not transfer stays with the individual.

Ask for the narrow version when the facts support it. Judges and evaluators notice when a petition requests every power on the form without explaining why, and an overbroad request can slow the case or invite opposition from the respondent's counsel. The person at the center of the case also keeps more dignity, which matters to the family relationships that will continue long after the order is signed.

## The cost of waiting too long

Families often hold off on filing because the conversation is painful or because a sibling objects. The risk of waiting is that the decision gets made in a crisis instead: a hospital will not discharge a patient to an unsafe home, a bank freezes an account after a suspicious withdrawal, or a landlord starts eviction. At that point the only option is an [emergency guardianship petition](/emergency-temporary-guardianship-how-fast), which is faster but gives the court less information and the family less control over who is appointed.

A better sequence is to raise the question early, get a capacity evaluation while the person can still participate, and talk through [estate planning](/practice-area/estate-planning) tools that might avoid the courthouse entirely.
