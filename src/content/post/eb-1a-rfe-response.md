A Request for Evidence in an EB-1A case is a written notice listing the points on which the officer is not yet persuaded, with a deadline for your response. It is not a denial, and it is not a formality. The RFE tells you exactly how the officer currently reads your record, which is information no petitioner has at the time of filing. Responding to an EB-1A request for evidence well means treating the notice as an outline for a second, better petition on the points raised. CLP's [EB-1A petition practice](/practice-area/key-services/eb-1a-green-card) takes over RFEs on petitions prepared elsewhere, and the method below is the one we apply.

## First steps when the EB-1A request for evidence arrives

Calendar the deadline the day the notice arrives. USCIS sets the response period in the notice; the regulations allow up to 12 weeks for an RFE, and the notice controls. The response must be received, not postmarked, by that date, and a late response is treated as a failure to respond, which usually produces a denial on the existing record. Extensions are not granted.

Then read the notice twice. The first read is for structure: which criteria does the officer accept, which does the officer reject, and does the notice raise the final merits determination? The second read is for the reasoning behind each rejection. An officer who rejects an award because the selection criteria were not documented wants different evidence from an officer who rejects it because the award is institutional. Many RFEs follow a template, but the specific sentences about your evidence are where the response is won.

## Classify every point

Sort the officer's points into three groups.

- Documentation gaps: the evidence exists but was not in the file, or was in the file without the context the officer needed. Missing circulation data for a publication, missing membership bylaws, a citation count without a field benchmark. These are the easiest points to cure.
- Characterization disputes: the officer read the evidence and disagreed with how it was described. A role called critical that the organizational chart does not support, or a contribution called major without adoption evidence. These require new evidence plus argument.
- Final merits: the officer accepts three criteria but questions sustained acclaim. This requires a rewritten brief and comparative evidence rather than more exhibits of the same kind. Our post on [the EB-1A final merits determination](/eb-1a-final-merits-determination) explains what the officer weighs at that stage.

Do not respond only to the points you can win. If the notice raises five issues, answer all five, even the weak ones, because an unanswered point is conceded.

## What to add, and what not to add

Add documents that supply what the notice says is missing: award selection criteria from the awarding body's own materials, media kits or circulation figures for publications, invitation letters and completed review records for judging, citation reports with field-normalized comparisons, licensing agreements or adoption letters for contributions, and wage data for remuneration. Where the officer questions independence, add letters from experts who have never worked with you, each addressing a specific use of your work; our post on [recommendation letters for extraordinary ability cases](/recommendation-letters-extraordinary-ability) explains how to brief them.

Do not add volume for its own sake. Fifty more pages of similar publications do not answer a question about significance. Do not rely on events after the filing date to prove eligibility; eligibility is judged as of filing, though later evidence can corroborate what existed then, such as a citation report run now for papers published before filing. And do not quietly change your field or your theory of the case. If you defined the field one way in the petition, the response must either keep that definition or explain the change.

## Write a response brief, not a cover letter

The response should open with a short statement of what is enclosed and then address the officer's points in the order the notice raised them, using the notice's own headings. For each point, state the standard, identify the new exhibit by tab number, and explain in one or two paragraphs why the exhibit answers the concern. Close with the final merits argument, rewritten to incorporate the new comparative evidence, even if the notice did not raise final merits expressly, because the officer will reach it next.

Place the original RFE notice on top of the package, as the instructions require, and index every exhibit. An officer who cannot find the document you are describing treats it as absent.

## When the RFE signals a bigger problem

Some RFEs reveal that the petition was filed on a record not yet ready. If the response would require comparative evidence that does not exist, consider answering the RFE as well as you can while preparing an EB-2 National Interest Waiver or planning a stronger EB-1A for later. Our [EB-1 green card](/practice-area/key-services/eb-1-green-card) page describes the other first preference options, and if the petition is denied despite the response, our post on [EB-1 denial reasons and next steps](/eb-1-denial-reasons-next-steps) covers motions, appeals and refiling. If instead the case has gone silent long past the posted processing time after your response, a [mandamus action](/practice-area/key-services/immigration-mandamus) to compel a decision may be appropriate.
