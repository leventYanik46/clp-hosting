Special Immigrant Juvenile Status (SIJS) is a way for certain young people in the United States to become lawful permanent residents when they cannot safely be reunited with a parent. It sits at the crossroads of two legal systems, state family or juvenile court and federal immigration, which is why the process can feel confusing. Understanding the order of the steps makes it much easier to follow.

## What SIJS is

SIJS is a federal immigration classification created by Congress to protect children who have been abused, neglected, or abandoned. A young person who qualifies can eventually apply for a green card. SIJS is not available to adults, and a child's SIJS case does not give any immigration benefit to the parent whose conduct led to the case.

## Who may qualify

The requirements come from federal law, but the state court plays a key role. In general, the applicant must:

- Be under 21 when the petition is filed
- Be unmarried
- Be physically present in the United States
- Obtain a state juvenile court order that makes specific findings

The state court must find that the young person was declared dependent on the court, or placed in the custody of an agency or an individual appointed by the court. The court must also find that reunification with one or both parents is not viable because of abuse, neglect, abandonment, or a similar basis under state law. And the court must find that it is not in the young person's best interest to return to their country of origin.

State courts do not decide immigration status. They make factual findings about the young person's circumstances, and USCIS decides whether the federal requirements are met.

## Age rules differ by state

Many states allow juvenile or family courts to act only until age 18. Others extend jurisdiction to 19 or 21, and the types of orders available differ. Because the law requires the young person to be under 21 when filing with USCIS, timing is critical. Waiting can close doors. A young person who is close to a state's age limit should speak with a lawyer quickly.

## The process step by step

**Step 1: State court order.** A guardianship, custody, dependency, or similar case is filed in the appropriate state court. The young person asks the court to issue an order with the required SIJS findings. The court holds a hearing, and the findings are put in writing.

**Step 2: Form I-360.** With the state court order in hand, the young person files Form I-360, Petition for Amerasian, Widow(er), or Special Immigrant, with USCIS. Along with the court order, the petition includes proof of age and identity and a statement about the young person's circumstances. USCIS reviews whether the state court order was sought to obtain relief from abuse, neglect, or abandonment and not solely for immigration benefit.

**Step 3: Green card application.** After USCIS approves the I-360, the young person may apply for permanent residence on Form I-485 if a visa number is available. SIJS green cards fall under the EB-4 employment-based category, which is a quirk of how the law is structured, not a sign that the applicant needs a job.

## Visa availability and waiting

Visa numbers in the EB-4 category can be limited, and some applicants, particularly those from countries with heavy demand, may have to wait after the I-360 is approved. Check the monthly Visa Bulletin to see where your category stands. USCIS has at times offered deferred action to some young people with approved SIJS petitions who are waiting for a visa number, but policies can change, so confirm current rules.

Because SIJS applicants can be in removal proceedings, an approved I-360 can also be useful in immigration court. People in proceedings may ask the judge to pause the case while they pursue SIJS.

## Special benefits in the green card step

SIJS applicants get a number of favorable rules. Several grounds of inadmissibility that block other green card applicants, such as lack of a valid visa or entering without inspection, do not apply or can be waived. That makes SIJS an important option for young people who came without papers.

## Common problems

- Waiting too long and aging out of the state court's authority
- Seeking the state court order without knowing the required findings
- Missing documents proving age, identity, or custody
- Court orders that do not clearly state the necessary findings
- Not reporting address changes, which can cause missed notices
