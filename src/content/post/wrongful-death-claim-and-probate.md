A wrongful death claim cannot be separated from probate. The claim is filed by the estate's personal representative, so the estate must be opened before the lawsuit can begin. The settlement often requires court approval, and the proceeds are distributed under rules that differ from the will. Families who expect the injury case and the estate to proceed on separate tracks are surprised to find them tied together at every stage. This post explains the connection in Virginia, Washington, DC, and New York, and the decisions that need to be made in the right order.

## Opening the estate and appointing the representative

The first step is qualification of a personal representative. If the deceased left a will, the named executor presents it to the probate court and receives letters testamentary. If there is no will, a family member petitions for letters of administration, with priority generally given to the surviving spouse, then adult children, then parents. In Virginia this happens before the circuit court clerk; in DC before the Probate Division of the Superior Court; in New York before the Surrogate's Court in the county where the deceased lived.

The court may require a bond, which protects the beneficiaries against mismanagement. New York permits limited letters of administration that authorize the representative to pursue the wrongful death claim only, which avoids a full administration when the deceased had few other assets. ## The representative's duties to the beneficiaries

Once appointed, the personal representative is a fiduciary for everyone with an interest in the claim. That includes the statutory wrongful death beneficiaries, who may not be the same people who inherit under the will, and the estate's creditors, who have an interest in any survival claim proceeds. The representative hires counsel, makes decisions about the litigation, and must act in the interests of all beneficiaries rather than their own. A representative who favors one family member, settles cheaply to end the process, or fails to account for the proceeds can be removed and held personally liable. Our post on [who is allowed to file a wrongful death claim](/who-can-file-wrongful-death-claim) describes the beneficiaries in each jurisdiction.

## Court approval of settlements

A wrongful death settlement is not final when the insurer and the family agree on a number. Virginia requires that wrongful death settlements be approved by the court, which reviews the amount and the proposed distribution among beneficiaries. New York requires the Surrogate's Court, or the court where the action is pending, to approve any compromise and to allocate the proceeds between the wrongful death claim and any survival claim, and among the distributees. DC courts likewise supervise the distribution of wrongful death proceeds among the spouse or domestic partner and next of kin. The approval process requires a petition, notice to all interested parties, and often a hearing.

- The petition identifies the beneficiaries and the basis for the proposed division.
- Beneficiaries who are minors or incapacitated need a guardian or similar protection, and their shares are often placed in restricted accounts or structured settlements.
- Attorney fees and costs are reviewed as part of the approval.

This step adds time, but it also protects the family: an approved distribution cannot later be challenged by a beneficiary who claims they were shortchanged.

## How the proceeds are distributed, and why the will may not control

Wrongful death proceeds generally do not pass through the will. They belong to the statutory beneficiaries in the shares the statute or the court sets, and in all three jurisdictions they are generally shielded from the deceased's own creditors. A deceased person with large medical or credit card debt does not lose the family's wrongful death recovery to those creditors.

Survival claim proceeds are different. They compensate the deceased's own suffering and losses, so they are assets of the estate: subject to creditor claims, administration expenses, and the terms of the will or intestacy. The allocation of a settlement between the two claims, which we discuss in our post on [wrongful death vs survival action](/wrongful-death-vs-survival-action), therefore determines how much reaches the family and how much goes to creditors. In Virginia the question is simpler because the claims are merged, and the statute directs that wrongful death damages are not subject to the deceased's debts.

## Estate planning questions the claim raises

A wrongful death recovery frequently creates planning issues for the people who receive it. A minor child's share needs a custodian, a trust, or court supervision until adulthood. A beneficiary with a disability may lose public benefits if the money is paid outright, and a supplemental needs trust may be appropriate. A surviving spouse who receives a substantial sum may need a will and powers of attorney for the first time. Our [estate planning](/practice-area/estate-planning) team handles these questions alongside the litigation, so that the recovery is protected once it arrives. The amounts at stake are described in our post on [what damages a family can recover in a wrongful death case](/wrongful-death-damages-explained).

## Order of operations

Open the estate first; nothing else can start without letters. Send preservation letters and begin investigating while the appointment is pending. File the lawsuit well within the two-year deadline from death. Negotiate with the allocation between claims and among beneficiaries in mind. Petition for approval, resolve any minor or disability issues, and then distribute. Done in this order, the probate process supports the claim rather than delaying it.
