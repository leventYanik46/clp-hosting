A blanket L petition is a one-time USCIS approval that covers a whole group of related companies, so that each later transferee can apply for an L-1 visa directly at a U.S. consulate instead of waiting for an individual petition. For a company that moves several managers or specialists to the United States every year, that difference is real: the corporate relationship is proven once, and each employee's case is decided at the visa interview. The tradeoff is that the consular officer, not USCIS, decides whether each person qualifies, and that officer has limited time to read a complicated file.

## Which companies qualify for a blanket L petition

The regulation sets a threshold that keeps blanket petitions for established multinational groups. The petitioner and each qualifying organization must be engaged in commercial trade or services. The U.S. office must have been doing business for at least one year. The group must have three or more domestic and foreign branches, subsidiaries or affiliates. And the group must meet one of three size tests:

- At least ten L-1 approvals for the group in the previous twelve months.
- U.S. subsidiaries or affiliates with combined annual sales of at least $25 million.
- A U.S. workforce of at least 1,000 employees.

A company that opened its first U.S. subsidiary last year will not qualify yet. Its first transfers go through individual petitions, including the [new office L-1A petition](/new-office-l-1a-petition-requirements) if the U.S. entity has been doing business for less than a year. Our [L-1A intracompany transfer visa](/practice-area/key-services/l-1a-visa) team usually sees blanket filings once a company's U.S. operation has grown past that early stage.

The blanket petition itself is filed on Form I-129 with the L Supplement and lists the qualifying entities, with evidence of ownership and control for each. The first approval runs for three years. After that, the company can request an indefinite extension, and it can add new entities by amendment as the group grows or restructures.

## Who can transfer under the blanket

A blanket covers managers, executives and specialized knowledge professionals. That last group is narrower than the full L-1B category: the employee must hold specialized knowledge and also work in a profession, which in practice means a position that normally requires at least a bachelor's degree. A technician with deep product knowledge but no degree may still qualify for an [L-1B specialized knowledge visa](/practice-area/key-services/l-1b-visa), but only through an individual petition filed with USCIS.

Each transferee still needs one continuous year of qualifying employment abroad with the group within the three years before the application, and the U.S. job must still be managerial, executive or specialized knowledge. The blanket proves the corporate relationship; it does not prove the employee's eligibility.

## How the I-129S process works at the consulate

Once the blanket is approved, the employer prepares Form I-129S for each transferee. That form identifies the employee, the foreign and U.S. entities, the U.S. position and the duties, and confirms the year abroad. The employee completes the DS-160, books a visa interview, and brings the I-129S, a copy of the blanket approval notice, a support letter and supporting documents to the consulate. Canadian citizens, who do not need a visa, can present the same package to Customs and Border Protection at a port of entry or a preclearance location.

The consular officer decides two things at the interview: whether the employee meets the L-1 requirements and whether the case is clearly approvable. If the officer is not satisfied on the record presented, the officer can refuse the blanket application. The company is then left to file an individual petition with USCIS, which costs time the blanket was supposed to save.

## Blanket or individual petition: how to choose

Use the blanket for the straightforward cases: a regional director moving into an equivalent U.S. role, or a degreed engineer joining a U.S. project team after years on the product. File an individual petition when the case has a weak point that needs a written argument. Common examples are a function manager who supervises no one directly, a U.S. team with very few employees, a year abroad interrupted by leave or transfers, or a recent change in the ownership chain. An approved individual petition also gives the employee an I-797 approval to show at the interview, which reduces the officer's discretion on the merits.

Timing matters too. Consular appointment wait times vary widely by post, while USCIS offers premium processing on individual I-129 petitions. For a transfer that must start on a fixed date, compare the local interview backlog with premium processing before choosing. ## Keeping a blanket in good order

A blanket is only as good as the list of entities behind it. Mergers, spin-offs, dissolved subsidiaries and new affiliates all change the group, and an outdated blanket can lead a consular officer to refuse an employee whose employer is no longer covered. Review the list each year, file amendments when the structure changes, and keep the ownership evidence current.
