DACA provides protection from deportation and work authorization, but it isn't a path to a green card on its own. Still, many DACA recipients can get a green card through other routes. Whether you qualify depends largely on how you entered the U.S. and whether you've traveled on advance parole.

## Why entry matters

To adjust status inside the U.S., you generally must have been inspected and admitted or paroled. That's the key issue for many DACA recipients:

- **Entered with a visa or were inspected at the border:** You may be able to adjust status inside the U.S. through a qualifying relative.
- **Entered without inspection:** You generally can't adjust status inside the U.S. You may need to process abroad, which can trigger unlawful presence bars.

## Advance parole

DACA recipients who traveled abroad on advance parole and returned were paroled into the U.S. That parole entry can satisfy the "inspected and admitted or paroled" requirement, opening the door to adjustment of status through a U.S. citizen spouse or parent.

Advance parole for DACA recipients has been limited and has changed over time. Travel abroad carries risks. Get advice before traveling.

## Marriage to a U.S. citizen

If you entered lawfully or were paroled, marriage to a U.S. citizen may allow you to adjust status as an immediate relative. Overstaying a visa doesn't bar adjustment for immediate relatives.

If you entered without inspection, you may need to apply for an immigrant visa abroad, with a provisional waiver (Form I-601A) for unlawful presence, which requires extreme hardship to a U.S. citizen or green card holder spouse or parent.

## Parent or adult child who is a U.S. citizen

A U.S. citizen parent can petition for you, but if you're over 21 or married, you're in a preference category with a wait. A U.S. citizen child over 21 can petition for you as a parent.

## Employment-based green cards

Employment-based green cards are harder for DACA recipients because DACA isn't a lawful status. Section 245(c) bars adjustment for people who have worked without authorization or failed to maintain status, with exceptions. Consular processing may be required, which can trigger bars.

## The May 2026 policy

USCIS guidance issued in May 2026 tells officers to approve adjustment of status for people in temporary status only in limited circumstances. How this applies to DACA recipients is still developing. Get advice before filing.

## The status of DACA

DACA has been the subject of years of litigation. Rules for renewals and new applications have changed, and they may change again. Keep your DACA renewed while exploring green card options.

## What to do

1. Gather proof of how you entered the U.S.
2. Find any advance parole documents
3. Identify any qualifying relatives
4. Get a legal review before filing or traveling

## Practical example

A DACA recipient entered the U.S. as a child without inspection, received DACA, and traveled to visit a grandparent abroad on advance parole in 2016, returning lawfully. She later marries a U.S. citizen. Because her last entry was a parole, she may be eligible to adjust status in the U.S. as an immediate relative, without leaving. Another recipient with the same history but no advance parole trip would generally need to consular process abroad with an I-601A waiver, which requires showing extreme hardship to a U.S. citizen or green card holder spouse or parent.

## Avoid filing too soon

Filing a green card application without confirming eligibility can expose you to enforcement risk and can create a record that complicates a later case. Gather your records and get a legal review before you file.

## Employment-based options in more detail

The section 245(c) bars generally prevent people who worked without authorization or fell out of status from adjusting status through an employer petition. Some exceptions exist, such as section 245(k) for certain employment-based applicants with limited periods of violations. DACA recipients typically don't fit these exceptions, so most employment-based routes require consular processing. If the DACA recipient has accrued unlawful presence before turning 18 and receiving DACA, that time doesn't count toward the bars, and DACA time itself doesn't count as unlawful presence, which helps some recipients. Each case depends on dates, so the history needs careful review.
