The E-2 visa has no fixed minimum investment, and that surprises many applicants. Instead of a dollar threshold, officers apply a proportionality test: the investment has to be substantial in relation to the cost of the business, large enough to make the enterprise viable, and large enough to show the investor is seriously committed. The business plan is where you prove all of that.

## How "substantial" is judged

The regulations and consular guidance look at several factors together:

- **Proportionality.** The lower the total cost of the business, the higher the percentage of it the investor generally must put in. For a modest business, the investor may need to commit nearly all of the cost. For a very expensive business, a smaller share can still be substantial.
- **Viability.** The amount has to be enough to get the business running and keep it operating.
- **Commitment.** The funds must be committed and at risk, meaning actually spent, or placed in an escrow that releases only if the visa is approved. Money in a personal bank account does not count.
- **Real and operating.** The business has to be a real enterprise, not a speculative or passive holding.

Because of that, an answer to "what is the E-2 minimum investment" depends on the business. A lawyer can look at your specific numbers and tell you how an officer is likely to see them.

## What a credible E-2 business plan contains

A strong plan reads like something a lender would take seriously, not a template. Typical sections:

1. **Executive summary.** What the business is, who owns it and what it will do.
2. **Business description and ownership.** Corporate structure, treaty-national ownership and control.
3. **Market analysis.** Customers, competitors and location, with evidence behind the claims.
4. **Operations.** How the business will run day to day, suppliers and facilities.
5. **Marketing and sales plan.**
6. **Staffing plan.** The people you will hire, when and in what roles. Job creation helps show the enterprise is not marginal.
7. **Financial projections.** Usually covering five years, with the assumptions explained. Projections should be realistic and show that the business can support the investor's family and likely create jobs.
8. **Use of funds.** A breakdown of how the investment will be spent.

Plans that copy generic assumptions or overstate revenue tend to look weak. Adjudicators look for consistency between the plan, the evidence of funds and the actual business.

## Costs to expect

The investment itself is separate from the costs of the application. The application costs include government fees, which are set by the Department of State and USCIS and change from time to time, professional fees for a business plan if you hire a writer, and legal fees. Confirm current government fees on the official websites. Ask your attorney what the legal fee covers and which items are billed separately.

## Common mistakes

- Placing funds in a business that is not yet operating, with no refund path if the visa is refused.
- Choosing a business so small it cannot generate more than a minimal living.
- A source-of-funds trail with gaps.
- Treating the plan as a formality.

## Before you commit money

Have the business reviewed first, along with its price, your funds, and the plan's assumptions. A preliminary consultation is a low-cost way to check the plan before the investment becomes irreversible.
