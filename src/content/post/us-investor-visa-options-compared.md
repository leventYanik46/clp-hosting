"Investor visa" is not one visa. It is a label people use for at least five different routes, and they differ on the questions that matter most: how much you have to put in, whether your nationality qualifies, and whether the status can ever turn into a green card. Picking the wrong one usually costs a year, not just a filing fee.

This guide lays the main options side by side so you can see which one deserves a closer look.

## The five routes most investors consider

**E-2 treaty investor.** You invest a substantial amount in a U.S. business you will develop and direct. There is no fixed minimum in the regulations; the investment has to be large relative to the cost of the business and at risk. The catch is nationality: you must hold citizenship of a country with a qualifying treaty with the United States. E-2 status can be renewed indefinitely, but it is a nonimmigrant status and does not lead to a green card on its own.

**E-1 treaty trader.** Built for people whose company already trades substantially between the U.S. and their treaty country. More than half of the company's international trade must be with the United States. Like the E-2, it is renewable and nationality-limited, and it is not a green card path by itself.

**EB-5 immigrant investor.** This is the route that produces a green card directly. The standard investment is $1,050,000, or $800,000 in a targeted employment area, and the investment must create at least ten full-time jobs for qualifying U.S. workers. Any nationality can apply. The tradeoffs are size of the check, source-of-funds documentation, and waiting times that vary by country.

**L-1A new office.** If you own or run an established company abroad, you can open a U.S. branch, subsidiary, or affiliate and transfer yourself as an executive or manager. You must have worked for the foreign company for at least one continuous year in the last three. The first approval for a new office is usually limited to one year, and you must show real growth to extend. The L-1A also connects to the EB-1C green card for multinational managers.

**O-1A for founders.** Not an investment visa at all, but often the right answer for founders with a strong record. It rests on extraordinary ability, not capital, so the amount you invest is irrelevant. Your company can sponsor you if it is a real employer with a structure that can control your work.

## A quick comparison

| Route | Capital required | Nationality limit | Leads to green card |
|---|---|---|---|
| E-2 | Substantial, proportional to the business | Treaty countries only | Not directly |
| E-1 | No investment; trade volume matters | Treaty countries only | Not directly |
| EB-5 | $800,000 or $1,050,000 | None | Yes |
| L-1A new office | Enough to establish and staff the U.S. office | None | Through EB-1C |
| O-1A | None | None | Through EB-1A or NIW, often |

## How to narrow it down

Start with nationality. If you are not a national of an E treaty country, the E-1 and E-2 are off the table unless you hold a second qualifying citizenship, and citizenship acquired through investment now comes with its own domicile rule.

Then ask whether you need permanence. Investors who want a green card for their children, or who do not want to renew a visa every few years, usually end up looking at EB-5, an L-1A to EB-1C plan, or a founder track built around EB-1A or the national interest waiver.

Then look honestly at your existing business. The L-1A route requires a foreign company that will keep operating while you build the U.S. office. If your business is only an idea, the L-1A is not available yet.

## What every investor visa needs: a business plan that holds up

Whatever route you choose, the business plan is where most cases are won or lost. Adjudicators want to see a real operation, not a passive holding. For the E-2, the plan has to show the business will do more than support you and your family. For a new-office L-1A, it has to show the U.S. company can support an executive or manager within a year. For EB-5, a plan that meets the standards in Matter of Ho is close to mandatory when you invest directly.

A plan written to satisfy an immigration adjudicator is different from one written for a bank. It needs hiring timelines tied to specific positions, startup costs that match the funds you are committing, and assumptions a skeptical officer can check.

## Common mistakes

- Spending the investment before confirming eligibility. Money already committed to a lease or equipment does not help if your nationality does not qualify.
- Treating the E-2 as a green card. Many families find out years later that their children age out of E-2 dependent status at 21.
- Choosing the cheapest route instead of the one that fits. A lower investment that leads to repeated renewals and no permanence can cost more over a decade.
