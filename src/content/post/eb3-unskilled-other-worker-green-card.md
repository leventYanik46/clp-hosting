The EB-3 category is the most accessible employment-based green card, and it includes a subcategory for jobs that need less than two years of training or experience. This is often called the EB-3 unskilled worker or "other worker" category. It can open permanent residence to workers in industries like hospitality, food processing, caregiving, and manufacturing, but it comes with its own visa limits and longer waits.

## The three EB-3 subcategories

1. **Skilled workers:** jobs that require at least two years of training or experience
2. **Professionals:** jobs that require a U.S. bachelor's degree or foreign equivalent
3. **Other workers:** jobs that require less than two years of training or experience

The first two are often grouped together. The other worker group has a separate annual limit of 10,000 visas, which is a small share of the overall EB-3 allotment.

## Which jobs qualify as EB-3 other worker positions

Typical other worker jobs include:

- Hotel housekeepers and maintenance workers
- Food processing and meat packing workers
- Warehouse and production line workers
- Caregivers and home health aides
- Landscaping and groundskeeping workers
- Restaurant workers, such as cooks without formal training requirements

The key is the job's requirements, not the worker's qualifications. A worker with a degree can still be sponsored for an other worker position if the job itself requires less than two years of training.

## EB-3 skilled jobs list

Skilled worker jobs require at least two years of training or experience. Examples include electricians, welders, chefs, mechanics, and some technicians. Employers sometimes write job requirements to fit the skilled category to avoid the other worker backlog, but the requirements must reflect the job's actual minimum requirements. Inflating them can cause the PERM application to fail.

## The process

1. **Prevailing wage determination** from the Department of Labor
2. **Recruitment:** the employer advertises the position to test the U.S. labor market
3. **PERM labor certification:** the employer files Form ETA-9089 if no qualified U.S. workers are found
4. **Form I-140:** the employer files the immigrant petition with USCIS and must show it can pay the offered wage
5. **Adjustment of status or consular processing,** once the worker's priority date is current

## How long the wait is

The other worker category often has longer waits than the skilled and professional categories because of its separate cap. The wait depends on your country of birth and the current Visa Bulletin. Workers from India, the Philippines, Mexico, and other countries with high demand may wait longer. Check the "Other Workers" row of the Visa Bulletin, not just the EB-3 row.

## For employers

EB-3 other worker sponsorship can help retain workers in roles that are hard to fill, but it requires a long-term commitment. Employers should plan for:

- Recruitment costs and attorney fees for PERM, which employers must pay
- Showing ability to pay the offered wage from the priority date onward
- Waiting years for the worker's green card
- Maintaining the job offer throughout the process

## For workers

If you're currently in the U.S. in another status, you'll need to maintain lawful status throughout the wait. Most other worker positions don't come with a temporary visa that can bridge the gap, which makes consular processing common.

## Common mistakes

- Inflating job requirements to fit a different category
- Underestimating the wait for the other worker category
- Missing the employer's ability-to-pay obligation

## Choosing between skilled and other worker classifications

Employers sometimes have a real choice. A role like a cook can be written as an entry-level job requiring no experience, or as a skilled position requiring two years of experience if that is what the employer actually requires of all hires. The classification must match the employer's genuine minimum requirements, and the Department of Labor will compare the PERM requirements with the employer's past hiring practice. If the employer hired U.S. workers without that experience, the higher requirement will not hold up.

## Keeping the worker during the wait

A long other worker wait creates retention risk on both sides. Employers should document the job offer as permanent and continuing. Workers already in the U.S. need their own lawful status throughout the wait, which is often the hardest part. For workers abroad, the process usually ends with consular processing once the priority date is current, followed by entry as a permanent resident ready to start the job.
