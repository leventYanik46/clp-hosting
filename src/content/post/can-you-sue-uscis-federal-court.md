You can sue USCIS, but a federal judge will not approve your green card, review a discretionary call, or award you money for the wait. If your question is whether you can sue USCIS over the problem in front of you, the answer depends on which of four problems it is: the agency has not decided, the agency decided wrong, the agency is holding someone, or the agency will not hand over records. Each has its own statute, its own court and its own limits. Capitol Law Partners handles all four through its [immigration appeals, delays and federal court practice](/practice-area/key-services/immigration-appeals-delays-and-federal-court), and the first job in every case is matching the problem to the right lawsuit.

## Why you can sue USCIS at all

The federal government cannot be sued without its consent. For immigration cases, the consent comes mainly from the Administrative Procedure Act, which waives sovereign immunity for suits seeking something other than money, such as an order to act or an order setting aside a decision. Federal question jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. 1331 gets you into district court. The Mandamus Act, 28 U.S.C. 1361, adds a separate route to compel an officer to perform a duty owed to you. Habeas corpus under 28 U.S.C. 2241 covers detention. The Freedom of Information Act carries its own grant of jurisdiction for records.

None of these statutes lets you demand damages for the years you waited. The remedy is an order: decide, release, produce, or reconsider.

## Problem one: USCIS has not decided

A case pending far beyond the posted processing time can support a mandamus and unreasonable-delay suit. The complaint names the USCIS Director and the Secretary of Homeland Security and asks the court to order a decision within a set period. Courts weigh the delay against the factors described in [how courts decide whether a USCIS delay is unreasonable](/how-courts-decide-uscis-delay-unreasonable). The government has 60 days to respond, and USCIS often adjudicates the case inside that window. Our [writ of mandamus service](/practice-area/key-services/immigration-mandamus) page covers which forms qualify. The one honest warning: the order compels a decision, not an approval. A weak application forced to decision can be denied faster than it would have been.

Naturalization applicants have a second tool. If USCIS has not decided an N-400 within 120 days after the interview, 8 U.S.C. 1447(b) lets the applicant ask the district court to decide the application itself or send it back with instructions.

## Problem two: USCIS decided, and the decision is legally wrong

The APA allows a court to set aside agency action that is arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or contrary to law. In practice that means a denial that misreads the statute, ignores evidence that was in the file, or contradicts the agency's own published guidance without explanation. The judge reviews the record USCIS had, not new evidence, and the usual remedy is to vacate the denial and send the case back for a new decision. [How an APA lawsuit challenges a USCIS denial](/apa-lawsuit-uscis-denial) walks through the standard.

Two limits matter here. First, Congress has placed certain discretionary decisions beyond review, and the Supreme Court recently confirmed that revoking an approved petition is one of them. Second, denials made in removal proceedings go through the Board of Immigration Appeals and then a petition for review in the court of appeals, not a district court suit. CLP's [immigration litigation team](/practice-area/key-services/immigration-litigation) sorts out which path a particular denial allows.

## Problems three and four: detention and withheld records

### Someone is detained

A habeas corpus petition asks a judge in the district where the person is held whether the detention is lawful and, if not, to order release or a bond hearing. It does not decide the removal case. It is often paired with a bond request before the immigration judge, and it moves faster than almost any other federal filing because a person's liberty is at stake.

### The government will not produce records

If USCIS, ICE, CBP or the immigration court ignores a FOIA request past the statutory deadline, you are treated as having exhausted administrative remedies and may sue for the records. The court can order production and review redactions. Since the file often decides whether any other lawsuit is wise, this is frequently the first suit, not the last.

## What a court cannot do

- Approve a petition or application in place of USCIS, apart from the limited naturalization exception.
- Review a consular officer's refusal of a visa; the doctrine of consular nonreviewability largely closes that door.
- Review decisions the statute leaves to agency discretion, such as petition revocations.
- Award damages for delay or for the cost of waiting.
- Stop removal automatically; a stay must be requested separately wherever removal is a risk.

## Before you file

Pull the file. Count the days from any decision, because appeal deadlines are short and a lawsuit does not pause them; see [immigration appeal deadlines after a denial or removal order](/immigration-appeal-deadlines-explained). Have a lawyer read the merits of the underlying case, since forcing a decision on a flawed application is a loss dressed as a win. Then pick the suit that fits the problem, file it in a court with proper venue, and be ready for the government's first move, which in delay cases is usually to decide the case and in denial cases is usually a motion to dismiss.
