Most business contracts are read twice: once before signing, and once when something has gone wrong. The essential contract clauses are the ones that do their work on the second reading. Scope and payment terms decide whether you got what you paid for. Termination, indemnity, and limitation of liability decide who absorbs the loss when a project fails or a third party sues. Governing law and dispute clauses decide where and how the fight happens. Below is the list we check against in our [contract drafting, review, and negotiation](/practice-area/key-services/contract-drafting-review-negotiation) practice, with the reason each clause earns its place.

## Parties, scope, and deliverables

Start with the parties. Use the full legal name of each entity, its state of formation, and the signer's title. A contract signed by "Acme" when the entity is Acme Holdings LLC, or signed by an employee without authority, invites an enforceability argument later. Our post on [what makes a business contract unenforceable](/what-makes-contract-unenforceable) explains how often that argument succeeds.

Then define the scope precisely. What is being delivered, by when, to what standard, and how will the customer confirm it meets the standard? Vague scope is the single most common cause of commercial disputes, because each side fills the gap with its own assumptions. Include an acceptance procedure with a deadline for the customer to reject, after which the deliverable is deemed accepted. Include a change order process so that scope grows only with a signed document and a price adjustment. For recurring work, a master agreement with separate statements of work keeps this manageable; we explain that structure in [how an MSA and a statement of work fit together](/msa-vs-statement-of-work).

## Payment terms, term, and termination

Payment terms should state the amount, the trigger (signing, milestone, delivery, or monthly), the due date, the consequence of late payment, who pays taxes, and whether the customer may withhold disputed amounts. A clause that lets the customer dispute an invoice in good faith within a fixed window, and pay the undisputed remainder, prevents payment disputes from becoming termination disputes.

Term and termination deserve more attention than they get. State the initial term and whether it renews automatically; auto-renewal with a short notice window is a trap for whichever party is less organized. Provide termination for cause, meaning a material breach that is not cured within a stated period after written notice. Decide whether either party may terminate for convenience, on how much notice, and whether any fee is due. Then address the effect of termination: final payments, return of data and materials, transition assistance, and which clauses survive. Confidentiality, indemnity, limitation of liability, and dispute resolution should always survive.

## Warranties, indemnification, and limitation of liability

These three clauses allocate risk, and they should be negotiated as a set. Warranties are the promises each party makes about its performance: that services will be performed professionally, that software will conform to documentation for a stated period, that neither party is violating anyone's rights. Each warranty should come with a specific remedy, such as re-performance or refund, and a disclaimer of all other implied warranties.

Indemnification shifts the cost of third-party claims. The classic example is a vendor indemnifying its customer against claims that the vendor's product infringes a patent or copyright. The limitation of liability clause then caps each party's total exposure and excludes indirect and consequential damages, with carve-outs for the obligations that should not be capped. These two clauses interact closely, and a cap that accidentally swallows the indemnity is a drafting error we see often. We walk through the mechanics in [how limitation of liability and indemnity clauses work](/limitation-of-liability-indemnification-clauses).

## Confidentiality and intellectual property

Even where a separate NDA exists, the contract should contain its own confidentiality clause covering information exchanged during performance. Define what is confidential, list the standard exclusions (public information, independently developed, received from a third party without restriction), state how long the obligation lasts, and require return or destruction on termination.

The IP clause answers who owns what is created under the contract. The default rules surprise people: a contractor owns the copyright in what it creates unless the agreement assigns it, and the work-made-for-hire doctrine covers fewer situations than most business owners assume. State clearly whether deliverables are assigned to the customer, licensed to the customer, or retained by the vendor with a license. Protect each side's pre-existing IP with a background IP clause. If the deal involves proprietary processes or data, CLP's [trade secret protection](/practice-area/key-services/trade-secret-protection) practice can tighten the confidentiality and use restrictions accordingly.

## Governing law, disputes, and the boilerplate that is not boilerplate

The final pages of a contract are where parties stop reading, and where lawyers put the terms that decide the fight. Governing law chooses which state's contract rules apply. Venue chooses where a lawsuit may be filed. An arbitration clause moves disputes out of court entirely, with tradeoffs on cost, speed, appeal rights, and confidentiality. A prevailing-party attorneys' fees clause changes the economics of every dispute, because the American default is that each side pays its own lawyers.

The remaining terms do real work too:

- Assignment: whether a party can transfer the contract, including in a merger or sale of the business
- Notices: where formal notices go and when they are effective, which controls every cure period and termination deadline
- Force majeure: which events excuse performance, and whether payment obligations are excused with them
- Entire agreement and amendment: the signed document controls over emails and proposals, and changes require a signed writing
- Severability, counterparts, and electronic signatures

None of these should be accepted on autopilot because they are "standard." Governing law in a distant state, a one-sided fees clause, or a free assignment right can cost more than any pricing term.

## How to use this list

Treat it as a checklist, not a template. For a low-value, repeat transaction, a short form that covers each item in a sentence is enough. For a contract that carries real revenue or real risk, each clause should be drafted for the specific deal and negotiated with the other side's redlines in mind.
