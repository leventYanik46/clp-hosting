A B-2 visitor may not sit in a single class until USCIS approves a change to F-1 status, and that one rule shapes everything else about a B-2 to F-1 change of status. The application has to reach USCIS before your I-94 date, the school has to be willing to defer your start date while USCIS decides, and the officer has to believe you did not enter as a tourist already planning to enroll. This post walks through the sequence, the timing, and the point at which applying for an F-1 visa at a consulate becomes the better choice.

## Who can apply from inside the United States

You must have been admitted as a B-2 visitor with a visa, not under the Visa Waiver Program, because Visa Waiver entrants cannot change status at all. Your I-94 must still be valid on the day USCIS receives the application, and you must have kept B-2 status in the meantime: no work, no classes. Visitors whose visa was annotated "prospective student" at the consulate have an easier time, because the annotation shows that the consular officer already knew about the plan. Our [change of status and I-539 extension](/practice-area/key-services/change-and-extension-of-status) practice reviews the entry record first, since an admission that is already compromised cannot be fixed by a well-prepared application.

## The B-2 to F-1 sequence, step by step

1. Get admitted to a school certified by SEVP and ask the designated school official to issue Form I-20 marked for a change of status. The school will enter you in SEVIS.
2. Pay the I-901 SEVIS fee and keep the receipt.
3. File Form I-539 with USCIS before the admit-until date on your I-94, with the current USCIS filing fee, the I-20, the SEVIS receipt, your passport and visa copies, your I-94, and financial evidence showing you can pay tuition and living costs for the first year.
4. Add a written statement explaining when and why you decided to study, what you will do after the program, and the ties that will bring you home.
5. Request premium processing on Form I-907 if your timing is tight; it is available for I-539 applications in the F category as of this writing, with action within 30 business days.
6. Wait without enrolling. If the program start date on the I-20 passes before approval, the school defers it to the next term and updates SEVIS.
7. On approval, your F-1 status begins on the approval date with a new I-94 attached to the approval notice. You then report to the school and begin classes.

## The no-study rule and the intent question

Attending classes in B-2 status is a status violation, and USCIS denies change of status applications when transcripts or SEVIS records show enrollment before approval. Even a free community course counts if it is part of the program you later ask to join. The second risk is intent. A visitor who enrolls within weeks of entry invites the conclusion that the plan to study existed when the visa was issued and the admission was obtained by misrepresenting the purpose of the trip. The Department of State applies a 90-day rule in its own adjudications that presumes misrepresentation when conduct inconsistent with the visa occurs within 90 days of entry, and while USCIS does not apply that rule mechanically, officers look at the same timeline. A gap between arrival and the decision to study, an explanation of what changed, and a consular annotation all help.

## Timing: bridge filings and the I-94 date

Before 2021, applicants whose B-2 stay would expire more than 30 days before the program start had to file a separate B-2 extension as a bridge, and a denied bridge sank the F-1 application with it. USCIS now grants F-1 status from the approval date and no longer requires the bridge for most changes to F-1. The I-94 deadline still governs: a timely filed, non-frivolous I-539 places you in a period of authorized stay while USCIS decides, even after your B-2 admission date passes. A late filing is excused only for extraordinary circumstances beyond your control. Our post on [I-94 expiration versus visa expiration](/i-94-expiration-vs-visa-expiration) explains why the date on your I-94 and not the date on your visa controls the deadline.

## Change of status or a new visa from the consulate?

Approval of the I-539 gives you F-1 status, not an F-1 visa. The first time you leave the United States you must obtain an F-1 visa at a consulate before returning, and that interview asks the same intent questions. Some applicants are better off leaving before the I-94 expires and applying for the F-1 visa abroad: the consular route avoids the maintenance-of-status requirement, sidesteps the preconceived intent problem for a recent arrival, and ends with a visa you can travel on. The tradeoffs are appointment availability, the cost of the trip, and the risk of a refusal under INA 214(b), which our post on [what to do after an F-1 visa refusal](/after-your-visa-was-refused-f-1-visa-process) addresses. Applicants from countries with long wait times or high refusal rates often prefer the change of status; applicants with a strong consular record and a short timeline often prefer the visa.

## Mistakes that lead to denials

Filing after the I-94 date. Enrolling early because the school said it was fine. Submitting bank statements that show a lump-sum deposit the week before filing with no explanation of the source. Leaving the country while the I-539 is pending, which abandons it. Forgetting to pay the SEVIS fee before filing. And treating the approval as a visa, then being refused boarding on the return flight from a holiday abroad.
