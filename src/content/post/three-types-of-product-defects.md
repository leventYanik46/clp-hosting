A product can hurt you in three legally distinct ways: it was built wrong, it was designed wrong, or it came without the warning that would have let you use it safely. Those are the three types of product defects, and the choice among them is the first decision in any product liability case, because each one calls for different proof, different experts, and a different set of defenses. A single injury often supports more than one. CLP's [defective product practice](/practice-area/key-services/dangerous-or-defective-products) starts every evaluation by sorting the facts into these three bins, and this post explains how.

## Manufacturing defects: the unit that left the factory wrong

A manufacturing defect means the product departed from its own design. The design was fine; this unit, or this batch, was not. A weld that did not fuse, a missing fastener, a drug lot contaminated at the plant, a tire with a belt that separated because of a curing error. The legal test is simple: compare the product that hurt you to the manufacturer's own specifications or to an undamaged example of the same model. If yours deviates, and the deviation caused the injury, the defect is established.

That simplicity makes manufacturing defects the easiest theory to explain to a jury and the hardest to prove without the product. The deviation has to be shown on the actual unit, which is why keeping it unrepaired matters so much. Our post on [what to keep after a defective product injury](/defective-product-injury-evidence-to-keep) covers that in detail. In DC and New York, a manufacturing defect supports strict liability, so you do not need to show the manufacturer was careless, only that the product left its hands in a defective condition.

## Design defects: the whole product line is dangerous

A design defect means every unit is dangerous because the design itself is. The product did exactly what it was built to do, and that was the problem. A space heater with no tip-over switch, a table saw without a blade guard that stays in place, a lithium-ion battery pack with no thermal cutoff, a vehicle with a center of gravity that invites rollover, a crib with slats far enough apart to trap a child's head.

The proof is heavier. New York and most other jurisdictions ask whether the product's risks outweighed its utility and whether a reasonable alternative design existed that would have reduced the risk at a reasonable cost without destroying the product's function. That requires an engineer who can describe the alternative and show it was feasible when the product was made. Industry and government standards, such as UL listings, ANSI standards, and federal motor vehicle safety standards, are evidence on both sides: compliance helps the manufacturer, and a standard the manufacturer ignored helps you. Compliance is rarely a complete defense, because standards are floors.

## Warning and instruction defects

A warning defect, sometimes called a marketing defect, means the product carried a risk that a user could not reasonably discover and the manufacturer failed to warn about it adequately, or failed to give instructions that would have made safe use possible. The classic examples are a medication without a warning about a dangerous interaction, a power tool whose manual never mentions kickback, and a cleaning product that reacts with another common product without saying so.

Adequacy is the fight. A warning buried on page 40 of a manual, printed in a language the buyer does not read, or placed where it cannot be seen during use may be no warning at all. For prescription drugs and devices, the warning generally has to reach the prescribing physician rather than the patient under the learned intermediary doctrine. Manufacturers also carry a post-sale duty in many jurisdictions to warn about dangers they learn of after the product ships, which is where recalls enter the picture, as we explain in [does a product recall prove the product was defective](/does-product-recall-prove-defect).

## How the three types of product defects change the legal theory

The same three defect types run through every product case, but the legal theory you attach to them depends on where you file. Washington, DC, and New York recognize strict liability in tort for all three. Virginia does not recognize strict product liability at all; a Virginia claim proceeds in negligence and in breach of the implied warranty of merchantability, and the plaintiff has to frame the defect in those terms. Our post on [strict liability versus negligence for a defective product claim](/strict-liability-vs-negligence-defective-product) walks through that difference. In every jurisdiction, you must prove the defect existed when the product left the defendant's control, and the defendant will argue that something changed between the factory and the injury.

## Pleading more than one defect

Most well-built cases plead two theories, sometimes three, because the evidence overlaps and because a jury can reject one and accept another. An e-bike battery fire is a good example. If the cells were contaminated during assembly, that is a manufacturing defect. If the pack had no cutoff to stop charging at full capacity, that is a design defect. If the manual never said to charge it away from exits and never on a fabric surface, that is a warning defect. The same engineer's inspection supports all three, and the choice of defendants follows from them, which is the subject of [who is liable for a defective product](/who-is-liable-defective-product).
