The claim decides the court. Delay suits, habeas petitions, FOIA cases and challenges to USCIS denials are filed in a United States district court, the trial level of the federal system. A challenge to a removal order skips the district court entirely and goes to the court of appeals for the circuit where the immigration judge finished the case. Knowing which federal court hears your immigration case is more than trivia: a complaint filed in the wrong court is dismissed or transferred, and a petition sent to the wrong circuit can cost you the 30-day deadline. CLP's [immigration appeals and federal court group](/practice-area/key-services/immigration-appeals-delays-and-federal-court) appears most often in the federal courts of Virginia, Maryland, New York and the District of Columbia, and this is how we decide where a case belongs.

## District court or court of appeals: the first split

Congress channeled all review of removal orders into a single petition for review in the court of appeals. A district court cannot hear a challenge to a removal order, however it is framed. Everything else about an immigration case, meaning delay, a benefit denial outside removal proceedings, detention, records and most citizenship disputes, begins in district court before a single judge, with the government represented by the local U.S. Attorney's Office.

## Where a mandamus or APA suit can be filed

Suits against federal officers and agencies have generous venue rules. Under 28 U.S.C. 1391(e) you may file where a defendant resides, where a substantial part of the events occurred, or where you live, as long as no real property is involved. Because the Secretary of Homeland Security, the USCIS Director and the Secretary of State all reside in Washington for venue purposes, the District of Columbia is always available. Many plaintiffs instead choose their home district: a Virginia resident with a stalled I-485 can file in the Eastern District of Virginia, and a New Yorker in the Southern or Eastern District of New York. Where the agency acted, such as the service center or field office handling the file, is a third option.

Districts differ in speed, in how their judges have ruled on the unreasonable-delay factors, and in how the local U.S. Attorney's Office handles these cases. Choosing among available venues is a strategic decision, which is why our [writ of mandamus service](/practice-area/key-services/immigration-mandamus) page asks for your address along with your receipt notices. Consular delay cases, where the plaintiff is often a U.S. citizen petitioner and the defendant is the State Department, are frequently filed in the District of Columbia.

## Where a habeas petition must be filed

Habeas is the exception to flexible venue. A petition challenging immigration detention is filed in the district where the person is physically held, and names the immediate custodian, usually the facility's warden or the local ICE field office director, as the respondent. Transfers between detention centers complicate this, since the right court can change overnight. Family members should learn the facility name before anything else.

## Where a petition for review goes

A petition for review of a Board of Immigration Appeals decision is filed in the court of appeals for the circuit in which the immigration judge completed the proceedings. A case heard in the Northern Virginia immigration courts goes to the Fourth Circuit, and a case heard in the New York courts goes to the Second Circuit. Where the person now lives is irrelevant, and so is where the Board sits. The 30-day deadline is unforgiving, so the circuit must be identified before the clock gets short; see [immigration appeal deadlines after a denial or removal order](/immigration-appeal-deadlines-explained).

## FOIA and naturalization suits

A FOIA lawsuit may be filed in the district where you live or have your principal place of business, where the records are kept, or in the District of Columbia. Naturalization disputes are tied to home: an applicant whose N-400 has gone 120 days past the interview without a decision, or whose denial was upheld after a hearing, sues in the district where the applicant resides. [The 120-day rule for a stuck N-400](/n-400-120-day-rule-after-interview) explains that remedy.

## How to tell which federal court hears your immigration case

- A removal order from an immigration judge, affirmed by the Board: the court of appeals for the circuit where the judge sat.
- A delayed USCIS or State Department case: district court where you live, where the agency acted, or in Washington, DC.
- Detention without a bond hearing or for a prolonged period: district court where the person is held.
- A USCIS denial you want set aside under the APA: the same venue options as a delay case.
- An ignored FOIA request: your home district, where the records are, or Washington, DC.
- An N-400 past 120 days after the interview, or a denial after the N-336 hearing: your home district.

Venue is only one part of the filing decision. The merits, the stay posture described in [does an immigration appeal stop deportation](/does-immigration-appeal-stop-deportation), and the judge pool all matter. But getting the court right is the step that cannot be fixed later without cost.
