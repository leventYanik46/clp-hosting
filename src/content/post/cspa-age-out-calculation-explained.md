Immigration law defines a "child" as an unmarried person under 21, and a petition filed for a 19-year-old can easily take longer than two years to reach a visa. The Child Status Protection Act, in force since 2002, lets many of those children keep "child" status after their real 21st birthday. The CSPA age calculation is not one rule but three, and which one applies depends on who filed the petition and whether the child is the main beneficiary or a derivative. Getting it wrong can move a child from a case with no wait into a line measured in years, which is why CLP's [green card for children](/practice-area/key-services/child-green-card) team runs the numbers before every filing.

## Immediate relatives: the age is frozen on the filing date

For the unmarried child under 21 of a U.S. citizen, the rule is simple. The child's age is locked on the date USCIS receives Form I-130. A child who is 20 years and 10 months old on the receipt date remains "under 21" for immigration purposes no matter how long USCIS or the consulate takes. There is no subtraction and no one-year deadline, though the child must still be unmarried when the green card is issued.

A second freeze applies when a permanent resident parent naturalizes while an F2A petition is pending: the child's age is locked on the naturalization date, and the case becomes an immediate relative case. Our post on [how a U.S. citizen gets a green card for a child](/green-card-for-child-of-us-citizen) covers what follows.

## Preference and derivative children: the CSPA age calculation formula

For everyone else, the statute gives a formula. Take the child's age on the date a visa became available. Subtract the number of days the petition was pending, from the receipt date to the approval date. If the result is under 21, the child is protected, provided the child "seeks to acquire" permanent residence within one year.

The formula covers F2A children of permanent residents, derivative children in F2B, F3 and F4 family cases, derivative children in employment-based cases, and diversity visa derivatives. A short worked example: a child is 21 years and 4 months old on the first day of the month the priority date becomes current. The I-130 was pending for nine months before approval. The CSPA age is 20 years and 7 months, so the child qualifies, as long as the one-year step is taken. In a sibling case, where the wait is long and the petition is often approved within a year or two, the subtraction is usually small and many children age out; see our post on [derivatives in a sibling green card case](/sibling-green-card-spouse-children-derivatives).

### Which Visa Bulletin chart counts

"Visa became available" is the later of two dates: the petition approval date, and the first day of the month in which the Visa Bulletin shows the priority date as current. Since early 2023, USCIS has used the Dates for Filing chart for adjustment applicants in months when it allows adjustment applicants to use that chart, which gives children a slightly earlier availability date and a better result. For consular cases, the Department of State has generally looked to the Final Action Dates chart. Because the two agencies can reach different results for the same child, where the child completes the case can matter. Our explainer on [priority dates versus filing dates](/priority-date-vs-filing-date) covers the two charts.

## The one-year seek-to-acquire deadline

Protection under the formula is conditional. Within one year of the visa becoming available, the child must take a concrete step toward residence. USCIS and the State Department accept filing Form I-485, submitting Form DS-260, paying the immigrant visa fee to the National Visa Center, or filing Form I-824 to have a consulate notified about a following-to-join child. A letter saying the family intends to proceed is not enough.

Missing the window usually ends the protection. USCIS may excuse a late step for extraordinary circumstances beyond the family's control, but that is a discretionary argument, not a right. If the priority date retrogresses before the family acts, the analysis is redone when the date becomes current again, but nobody should plan around that. Act within the first window.

## What happens when the CSPA does not save the child

The result depends on the category. An F2A child who ages out converts automatically to F2B, the category for unmarried sons and daughters of permanent residents who are 21 or older, and keeps the original priority date. The wait gets longer, but the place in line survives.

A derivative child in another family category or an employment-based case is in a worse position. In Scialabba v. Cuellar de Osorio, decided in 2014, the Supreme Court upheld the government's reading that those aged-out derivatives do not keep the parent's priority date. The parent must become a permanent resident first and then file a new F2B petition for the son or daughter, with a brand-new priority date. For a family that waited many years in F4, that is the single most painful outcome in family immigration.

Marriage ends child status in every category. A derivative who marries before the visa is issued is out, and a later divorce does not reliably put the child back in.

## Protecting a child before the problem arrives

- File the petition as early as possible; for immediate relatives the filing date is the freeze date, and for everyone else earlier filing means an earlier priority date.
- Keep the USCIS receipt and approval notices. The pending period is calculated from those two dates.
- Parents who are permanent residents and eligible to naturalize should think about timing. Naturalizing while an F2A petition is pending freezes the child's age; naturalizing after the child is already in F2B moves the case to F1, which may or may not be faster for the child's country.
- When the Visa Bulletin shows the date approaching, have the DS-260 or I-485 ready to file in the first month of availability.
- For a child who will age out no matter what, plan the follow-on petition now so it can be filed the day the parent becomes a resident. Our [family-based immigration](/practice-area/key-services/family-based-immigration) overview lists the categories and who can file.
