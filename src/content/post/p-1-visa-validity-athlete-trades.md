How long a P-1 visa lasts depends on who holds it. An individual athlete can be approved for up to five years and extended for up to five more, with total stay capped at ten years. An athletic team or an entertainment group is approved for the time needed to complete the event, up to one year, and extended one year at a time. Professional athletes who are traded get a 30-day bridge while the new club files. These rules come from 8 CFR 214.2(p), and they shape when the club, agent or promoter needs to file next. The eligibility side is covered on our [P-1 visa for athletes and entertainment groups](/practice-area/key-services/p-1-visa) page; this article is about the clock.

## How long a P-1 visa lasts for individual athletes

USCIS approves an individual P-1 athlete for the time needed to complete the event, competition or performance, up to five years. The officer looks at the contract and the event described in the petition: a three-year playing contract supports three years, a single tournament supports far less. Extensions may be granted for up to five years at a time, but the athlete's total period of stay in P-1 status may not exceed ten years.

Three documents carry three different dates. The I-797 approval notice and your I-94 record set how long you may stay and work. The visa stamp in your passport only controls entry, and its length follows the reciprocity schedule for your country. You may be admitted up to ten days before the validity period begins and remain up to ten days after it ends, but you may compete or work only during the validity period itself.

## Teams and entertainment groups: one year at a time

A P-1 athletic team, and a P-1B entertainment group, is approved for the time needed for the event, up to one year. Extensions are also granted in increments of up to one year to continue or complete the same event. A touring group that books a second year of U.S. dates files an extension or a new petition, depending on whether the new dates are part of the same event. Support staff on P-1S status follow the principal's event, so their filings should be timed together.

## Filing an extension

The petitioner files Form I-129 requesting the extension, with a statement explaining why more time is needed and updated evidence such as the new contract or itinerary. A fresh consultation may be needed if the work changes. File before the current I-94 expires. A timely extension filed by the same employer generally lets you keep working for a limited period while it is pending, but leaving the United States while it is pending can complicate the case, so plan travel around the filing. Check the current USCIS filing fee and processing times before you file; premium processing is available.

## The ten-year limit for athletes

The ten-year cap applies to the individual athlete's total time in P-1 status. A player approaching it needs a different basis to stay: an O-1A if the record now shows sustained acclaim, or a green card. [P-1 or O-1: which visa fits athletes and performers?](/p-1-vs-o-1-athletes-performers) explains why some players move to the O-1A, which has no fixed maximum. Athletes at the top of their sport often qualify for the [EB-1A extraordinary ability green card](/practice-area/key-services/eb-1a-green-card), which covers athletics. Start that planning several years before the cap, not in the final season.

## What happens when a P-1 athlete is traded

P status has no general portability: a new employer normally must file its own petition and receive approval before you work for it. Trades are the exception. Under the P regulations, when a professional P-1 athlete is traded from one organization to another, employment authorization continues automatically for 30 days after the new organization acquires the player. The new organization is expected to file a new Form I-129 within those 30 days. If it does not, employment authorization ends at day 30.

In practice, the acquiring club's front office and immigration counsel should treat the trade date as the start of a 30-day filing deadline. The new petition needs the new contract, an updated consultation if the league's players' association requires one, and the same evidence of international recognition. Free agent signings and transfers that are not trades do not get the 30-day bridge, so the new club files and waits for approval before the athlete plays.

## Released, injured or between contracts

If your contract ends early, the basis for your status ends with it. As of this writing, the 60-day grace period that protects O-1, H-1B and several other workers whose employment ends early does not list P-1. A released athlete should therefore act quickly: a new petition from another club, a change to another status, or departure. Do not keep training with a club that has not filed for you. A move from player to coach is a different job and needs its own filing; coaches may qualify as P-1S support personnel or under another classification.
