The EB-1B regulation asks for at least two of six criteria, and almost every working researcher can name two within a minute: scholarly articles and peer review. That is why the criteria are not where EB-1B cases are won. They are the entry ticket, and the evidence assembled under each one becomes the material for the final merits determination, where the officer asks whether the record as a whole shows international recognition as outstanding. The EB-1B criteria at 8 CFR 204.5(i)(3)(i) therefore deserve more documentation than the two-of-six count suggests. The firm's [EB-1B green card practice](/practice-area/key-services/eb-1b-green-card) prepares petitions for institutions and researchers; this post sets out what we gather under each criterion and why.

## The six EB-1B criteria at a glance

The regulation lists: major prizes or awards for outstanding achievement in the academic field; membership in associations that require outstanding achievements of their members; published material in professional publications written by others about your work; participation, individually or on a panel, as the judge of the work of others in the same or an allied academic field; original scientific or scholarly research contributions to the field; and authorship of scholarly books or articles in scholarly journals with international circulation. Comparable evidence is allowed where a criterion does not readily apply to the field. Our post on [who qualifies for the EB-1B](/eb-1b-outstanding-researcher-requirements) covers the other requirements; this one is about the exhibits.

## Awards and memberships

### Major prizes or awards

The word major sets this criterion apart from its EB-1A counterpart. Early-career awards from a national society, best paper awards at leading conferences and named fellowships from funders with competitive selection can qualify, especially with evidence of the selection ratio and the standing of past recipients. Travel grants, internal university awards and student prizes generally do not. For each award, gather the certificate, the awarding body's criteria, the number of applicants or nominees, and any press.

### Memberships requiring outstanding achievement

Most professional societies admit anyone with a degree and a payment, and those memberships do not count. Fellow grades of major societies, academies that elect members, and editorial boards with selective appointment can. Gather the bylaws or election rules showing that outstanding achievement is judged by recognized experts, plus your election letter.

## Published material and judging

### Published material about your work

Articles, reviews or features written by others that discuss your research, in professional publications or major media. Full copies with the publication's name, date and author, plus circulation or readership data, are needed. A citing paper that discusses your findings at length can support this criterion, but a citation in a reference list does not; the material must be about your work.

### Judging the work of others

Peer review for journals and conferences, grant review panels, dissertation committees outside your own institution and editorial roles all fit. Document completed reviews, not just invitations: reviewer records from the publisher's system, thank-you letters from editors, and evidence of the journal's standing. A long list of routine reviews satisfies the criterion but says little at the final merits stage; a few selective roles say more.

## Research contributions and authorship

### Original scientific or scholarly research contributions

This criterion is the heart of the case. The regulation does not require major significance here, unlike the EB-1A, but the final merits determination asks whether the contributions have earned international recognition, so the evidence should show that others have used your work. Gather citation reports with field benchmarks, including the countries and institutions of citing authors; evidence of methods, datasets, software or materials adopted by other groups; patents and licenses; invited talks abroad; and letters from independent researchers describing a specific contribution and how it changed their work. Letters are interpretive evidence and cannot carry this criterion alone; our post on [recommendation letters for extraordinary ability petitions](/recommendation-letters-extraordinary-ability) explains how to brief recommenders so each letter adds something the documents do not.

### Authorship of scholarly articles

A complete publication list with journals, dates and co-authors; first pages of key papers; journal rankings or impact data; and evidence of international circulation, which for indexed journals is straightforward. Authorship satisfies the criterion on its face. At the final merits stage, the questions become your authorship position, the standing of the venues and the citations the papers have drawn.

## Comparable evidence and fields outside the laboratory

Researchers in the humanities, social sciences, law, architecture or the arts often find that citation metrics and journal impact data do not describe their fields well. Under the comparable evidence provision, monographs with university presses, invited lectures, exhibition of design or creative work in scholarly contexts, and reviews in field-leading venues can substitute, provided the petition explains why the listed criterion does not apply and why the substitute shows the same thing. The explanation is part of the evidence; do not leave the officer to infer it.

## From criteria to final merits

Choose the two or three criteria you can document most strongly rather than claiming all six thinly. Then build the final merits argument on the contributions and published material, with the citation record as its spine, and with international reach shown explicitly through the geography of citing authors, collaborators and recommenders.
