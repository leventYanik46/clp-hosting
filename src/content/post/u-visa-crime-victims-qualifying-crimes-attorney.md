A U visa is a form of immigration status for people who have been victims of certain crimes in the United States, suffered substantial harm, and are willing to help law enforcement. It was created to encourage victims to come forward without fear of immigration consequences. The process is slow and the rules are technical, which is why many survivors work with a U visa attorney.

## Who may qualify

To be eligible, you generally must show that:

- **You were the victim of a qualifying criminal activity** that occurred in the United States or violated U.S. law.
- **You suffered substantial physical or mental abuse** because of it.
- **You have information about the crime.**
- **You have been, are being, or are likely to be helpful** to law enforcement or other authorities in the investigation or prosecution.
- **You can obtain a certification** from a certifying agency confirming your helpfulness.
- **You are admissible,** or you can obtain a waiver of inadmissibility.

Some family members may be included as derivatives. A U visa applicant who is an adult can include a spouse and children, and a child applicant can include parents and certain siblings.

## What counts as a qualifying crime

The law lists the covered crimes, and some are broad. Examples include:

- Domestic violence
- Sexual assault, rape and abusive sexual contact
- Felonious assault
- Kidnapping, false imprisonment and unlawful criminal restraint
- Stalking
- Human trafficking and involuntary servitude
- Extortion and blackmail
- Witness tampering and obstruction of justice
- Murder and manslaughter
- Attempts, conspiracy or solicitation to commit any of these

The fact that an offense was charged under a different name does not necessarily disqualify it. The criminal activity has to be substantially similar to a listed crime. A lawyer can compare your situation to the list.

## The certification

The U visa petition, filed on Form I-918, must include a certification on Form I-918 Supplement B, signed by a certifying official at an agency such as a police department, prosecutor's office, court or other authority. Signing is discretionary, and agencies have their own policies. The certification confirms you were a victim, and that you have been, are being, or are likely to be helpful. Requesting a certification can be one of the harder steps, and attorneys often help present the request.

## Cap, waiting list and interim benefits

There is an annual limit on how many principal U visas can be granted. Demand exceeds the cap, so a waiting list has formed and wait times can be long. While waiting, USCIS may conduct a bona fide determination review and, if it is favorable, can grant deferred action and work authorization to eligible applicants. Check the USCIS website for current information on the process, because it has changed over time.

## After approval

U status is temporary and can last for several years. After holding it for three years, a person may be eligible to apply for a green card if they continue to meet requirements, including continued helpfulness to law enforcement unless unreasonably refused. Family members who received derivative status may also be eligible.

## Inadmissibility waivers

Many applicants have immigration or criminal history problems, such as unlawful presence, prior removal or arrests. A separate waiver application on Form I-192 can address many of these grounds. Honest disclosure is vital.

## Where an attorney helps

- Analyzing whether your case involves a qualifying crime
- Approaching the certifying agency
- Writing your declaration
- Assembling evidence of harm
- Identifying inadmissibility and preparing the waiver
- Including family members
- Advising on safety and confidentiality

If you are in danger, contact local emergency services. If you are ready to explore a U visa, find a qualified attorney or an accredited nonprofit early, because police records can be hard to obtain later.
