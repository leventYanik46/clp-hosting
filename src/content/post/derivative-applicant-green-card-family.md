In many green card categories, your spouse and children can qualify through your case without their own petition. They're called derivative applicants. Understanding derivative applicant rules matters most when a marriage or birth happens during the process, or when a child is approaching 21.

## Derivative applicant meaning

A derivative applicant is a family member who receives an immigration benefit because of their relationship to the principal applicant. In employment-based and most family preference categories, the principal's spouse and unmarried children under 21 can be derivatives.

Derivatives share the principal's priority date. They don't need their own I-140 or I-130.

## Which categories allow derivatives

**Allow derivatives:**
- Employment-based categories: EB-1, EB-2, EB-3, EB-4, EB-5
- Family preference categories: F1, F2A, F2B, F3, F4
- Diversity visa lottery

**Don't allow derivatives:**
- Immediate relative categories, such as spouses, parents, and minor children of U.S. citizens. Each family member needs a separate petition.

## Timing of relationships

The relationship must exist before the principal becomes a permanent resident:

- **Marriage before the principal's green card is approved:** The spouse can be a derivative. Children born before the green card approval can too.
- **Marriage after the green card is approved:** The spouse isn't a derivative. The principal must file a new I-130 under the F2A category.

## Following to join

Derivatives don't have to immigrate at the same time as the principal. They can follow to join later, as long as the relationship existed when the principal got the green card. This allows a spouse abroad to complete consular processing after the principal adjusts status in the U.S.

## The age-out problem

Children must be under 21 and unmarried to be derivatives. Because green card waits can be long, a child who was 15 when the case started may turn 21 before a visa is available. That's called aging out.

## How CSPA protects children

The Child Status Protection Act (CSPA) helps by calculating a child's age differently:

1. Start with the child's age when a visa becomes available.
2. Subtract the time the underlying petition (I-140 or I-130) was pending.
3. If the result is under 21, the child may still qualify.

To keep CSPA protection, the child must "seek to acquire" permanent residence within one year of the visa becoming available, usually by filing an application or paying the consular fee.

## Which Visa Bulletin chart applies to CSPA

USCIS has changed its policy on whether CSPA calculations use the Final Action Dates or Dates for Filing chart, most recently in 2025. The chart used can change whether a child qualifies. Get the calculation reviewed if your child is close to 21.

## If a child ages out

If CSPA doesn't help, the child may need a new petition after the principal becomes a permanent resident, often under the F2B category, which has long waits. Some families explore an independent route for the child, such as F-1 status.

## Common mistakes

- Marrying after the principal's green card is approved and assuming the spouse is covered
- Missing the one-year "seek to acquire" deadline
- Ignoring CSPA calculations until the last minute
- Forgetting that immediate relative categories don't allow derivatives

## Practical example

Suppose an engineer from India files an I-140 when her daughter is 17. The petition is pending for 10 months before approval. By the time her priority date becomes current, her daughter is 21 years and 6 months old. Under CSPA, subtract the 10 months the I-140 was pending: the daughter's CSPA age is 20 years and 8 months, so she still qualifies, as long as she seeks to acquire permanent residence within one year. Small changes in the facts, such as a shorter pending period or a different chart, can change the result, which is why families with children near 21 should get the calculation checked early.

## Divorce and the death of the principal

If the principal and spouse divorce before the spouse becomes a permanent resident, the spouse loses derivative eligibility. If the principal dies, derivatives may be able to continue the case in some situations under special provisions for surviving relatives, especially if they were living in the U.S. at the time of death.
