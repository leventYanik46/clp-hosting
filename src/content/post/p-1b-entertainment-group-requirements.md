The P-1B classification is for entertainment groups that perform as a unit, and it is not available to a solo performer. Under 8 CFR 214.2(p)(4)(iii), the group must be internationally recognized as outstanding in its discipline for a sustained and substantial period, and at least 75 percent of its members must have been with the group for at least one year. Those two rules, together with the evidentiary criteria below, are the core P-1B visa requirements for bands, orchestras, dance companies, theater ensembles and circuses. Our [P-1 visa for entertainment groups and athletes](/practice-area/key-services/p-1-visa) page covers how we prepare these petitions; this article explains what USCIS checks.

## The group, not the individual, must be recognized

P-1B status is granted to the group based on the group's international reputation. The regulation says individual entertainers may not receive P-1 classification to perform separate and apart from a group. Two consequences follow. A famous lead singer does not carry a band whose own reputation is undocumented; the press, awards and bookings must be about the group. And a solo artist, however well known, needs a different route, usually the [O-1B visa for artists](/practice-area/key-services/o-1b-visa), which judges the individual under the distinction standard.

The group must also have been established and performing regularly for at least one year. A newly formed supergroup of established musicians starts that clock from the date the group itself began performing.

## The 75 percent one-year rule and its exceptions

At least 75 percent of the members must have had a sustained and substantial relationship with the group for at least one year and must provide functions integral to the performance. The petition proves this with a statement listing each member and the exact dates each has performed with the group, supported by programs, contracts or other records that confirm the dates.

The rule has three release valves:

- Circus personnel are exempt from the one-year requirement if they perform as part of a circus, or are an integral and essential part of its performance, and the circus itself has been recognized nationally as outstanding for a sustained and substantial period.
- USCIS may waive the one-year requirement for a person who replaces an essential member because of illness or other unanticipated and exigent circumstances, or who augments the group by performing a critical role.
- USCIS may waive the international recognition requirement for a group that has been recognized nationally as outstanding for a sustained and substantial period, where special circumstances such as limited access to news media or geography explain the lack of international coverage.

Waivers are discretionary and must be requested with evidence. A late lineup change shortly before a tour is the classic replacement case; document why the change happened and why the new member's role is essential.

## Evidence of international recognition: an award or three criteria

The group qualifies through nomination for or receipt of significant international awards or prizes for outstanding achievement in its field, or through at least three of these six criteria:

1. The group has performed, and will perform, as a starring or leading group in productions or events with a distinguished reputation, shown by critical reviews, advertisements, publicity releases, publications, contracts or endorsements.
2. The group has achieved international recognition and acclaim, shown by reviews in major newspapers, trade journals, magazines or other published material.
3. The group has performed, and will perform, as a leading or starring group for organizations and establishments with a distinguished reputation.
4. The group has a record of major commercial or critically acclaimed successes, shown by ratings, standing in the field, box office receipts, record or video sales and other achievements reported in major media.
5. The group has achieved significant recognition from organizations, critics, government agencies or other recognized experts, in testimonials that show the writer's authority.
6. The group has commanded, and will command, a high salary or other substantial remuneration compared with others similarly situated.

Several criteria look forward as well as back ("has performed and will perform"), so the upcoming U.S. bookings themselves are evidence when the venues and festivals have a documented reputation. The document-gathering work is close to an O-1B case, and the [O-1B evidence checklist for musicians and actors](/o-1b-evidence-checklist-musicians-actors) is a useful guide to proving venue reputation, press reach and pay.

## Petition mechanics for touring groups

One petition can cover the whole group, filed by a U.S. employer, a U.S. sponsoring organization, a U.S. agent, or a foreign employer acting through a U.S. agent. A tour needs an itinerary with dates and venues and copies of the contracts or a summary of terms. The petition must include a consultation from the appropriate labor organization, such as the American Federation of Musicians for musical groups. Crew and other support staff need their own P-1S petition. A P-1B approval runs for the time needed to complete the event, up to one year. [How to file a P-1 visa petition, step by step](/p-1-visa-petition-process-steps) walks through the sequence.

## Common reasons group petitions fail

Group petitions most often stumble on the membership list (dates missing, or too many recent members to reach 75 percent), on press that is about one member rather than the group, on recognition limited to the home country without a waiver request, and on festival appearances listed without proof that the festival is distinguished. A group whose work is culturally unique may fit the [P-3 culturally unique program visa](/practice-area/key-services/p-3-visa) better, and a solo career belongs in an O-1B; [P-1 or O-1: which visa fits athletes and performers?](/p-1-vs-o-1-athletes-performers) helps sort the options.
