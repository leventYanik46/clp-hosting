An E-3 visa comes down to four requirements: you hold Australian citizenship, a U.S. employer has offered you a job that qualifies as a specialty occupation, you have the degree or equivalent that the occupation demands, and the employer has a certified Labor Condition Application on file. Miss any one of the four and the consular officer cannot issue the visa, no matter how strong the rest of the file looks. Our [E-3 visa practice page](/practice-area/key-services/e-3-visa) covers how we build these cases; this article walks through what each E-3 visa requirement actually means and where applicants get tripped up.

## The E-3 visa requirements, one at a time

### Australian citizenship

The E-3 category exists because of the U.S.-Australia Free Trade Agreement, and it is limited to Australian nationals. Citizenship is the test, not residence. A New Zealander who has lived in Sydney for twenty years does not qualify. A dual citizen of Australia and another country does, as long as you apply on the Australian passport. Permanent residents of Australia are not eligible.

### A specialty occupation

The job must be one that normally requires at least a bachelor's degree, or its equivalent, in a specific field. This is the same definition USCIS uses for the H-1B. Software engineers, accountants, architects, physiotherapists, teachers, and most laboratory and engineering roles clear this bar comfortably. Generalist roles are the problem: "business development associate" or "operations coordinator" positions often get refused because an officer cannot see why a degree in a particular specialty is required. The job description should name the duties that call for degree-level knowledge, and the employer should be able to explain its hiring norms for the role.

### A qualifying degree or its equivalent

You need a bachelor's degree or higher in a field related to the job, or an equivalent combination of education and experience. Two issues come up often with Australian applicants. First, many Australian bachelor's degrees are three-year programs, while the U.S. benchmark is four years. A credential evaluation from a recognized service usually resolves this, sometimes by crediting work experience under the standard rule that three years of progressive experience substitutes for one year of university study. Second, the degree must relate to the job. A commerce degree supporting a marketing analyst role is fine; a nursing degree supporting a data scientist role invites questions.

### A license, if the job requires one

If the occupation requires a state license to practice (nursing, law, some engineering roles, teaching in public schools), you must hold it or show you are eligible to obtain it. Consular posts will ask.

## The employer's side: the Labor Condition Application

Before you can apply, the employer files Form ETA-9035, the Labor Condition Application, with the Department of Labor and selects the E-3 Australia classification. The LCA commits the employer to pay the higher of the prevailing wage or the actual wage paid to similar workers, to maintain working conditions for U.S. employees, and to post notice of the filing. The wage level matters more than most employers expect; our article on [how prevailing wage determinations work](/prevailing-wage-determination-explained) explains why a Level I wage on a senior job title raises flags.

The employer does not need to file a petition with USCIS if you will apply at a consulate. The certified LCA, a job offer letter, and your evidence go straight to the visa interview. Part-time positions are allowed, as long as the LCA says so. Self-sponsorship through a company you own is a gray area that needs careful structuring and is not a safe default.

## Intent to return and the green card question

The E-3 is not a dual-intent visa in the way the [H-1B specialty occupation visa](/practice-area/key-services/h-1b-visa) is. You must satisfy the officer that you intend to leave when your status ends. In practice this is a modest showing for most applicants, and a pending or approved immigrant petition does not by itself disqualify you. But you should not tell an officer that you are moving permanently, and if you are far along in a green card process you should get advice before you travel. If you want the two categories side by side, our piece on [E-3 versus H-1B for Australian professionals](/e-3-vs-h-1b-australian-professionals) lays out the tradeoffs.

## The cap and who it does not count

Congress set the E-3 annual limit at 10,500 visas per fiscal year. Spouses and children do not count, and neither do renewals for people already in E-3 status. The limit has not been a practical barrier, so there is no lottery, no registration window, and no reason to wait for a particular month to apply. You can read about the sequence of steps in our guide to [how to apply for an E-3 visa](/how-to-apply-e-3-visa).

## Where E-3 applications fall short

The refusals we see tend to come from a short list of problems:

- A degree in one field paired with a job in an unrelated field, with no explanation of the connection
- A job description that reads like an advertisement rather than a list of degree-level duties
- An LCA wage level that does not match the seniority of the title
- A thin answer on ties to Australia or future plans when the officer asks about intent
- A licensed profession with no license and no explanation of the pathway to one

Each of these is fixable before the interview. None of them is easy to fix afterward, because a refusal under INA 221(g) or 214(b) stays in your record and must be addressed in any later application.
