Of the five protected grounds in asylum law, membership in a particular social group is the one the statute never defines, and it is the ground on which most contested claims are won or lost. The Board of Immigration Appeals filled the gap with a three-part test: the group must share a common immutable characteristic, be defined with particularity, and be socially distinct within the society in question. A group that fails any part of the test loses before anyone asks whether you were harmed. Our [asylum and humanitarian protection attorneys](/practice-area/key-services/asylum-and-humanitarian-protection) spend more time defining the group than on almost any other element of a claim, because the definition controls everything that follows. Here is how the test works, where claims break, and how to frame a group that survives.

## Where the particular social group test comes from

The starting point is Matter of Acosta, a 1985 Board decision holding that a particular social group is one whose members share a characteristic they cannot change, or should not be required to change because it is fundamental to their identity or conscience. Sex, family relationship, sexual orientation, and a shared past experience are the classic examples. In 2014, in Matter of M-E-V-G- and Matter of W-G-R-, the Board added two more requirements: the group must be defined with particularity, and it must be socially distinct. Every asylum officer and immigration judge applies that three-part test today. The federal courts of appeals review it, and some circuits have read the second and third elements more generously than others, so where your case is heard can matter.

## The three elements

### Immutable characteristic

The trait must be one you cannot change or should not have to. Gender, ethnicity within a nationality, family membership, sexual orientation, and gender identity all qualify. So does a shared past experience, because the past cannot be undone: former police officers, former members of a particular organization, survivors of trafficking. The element is rarely the problem on its own. Claims fail here only when the proposed trait is something a person could reasonably abandon, such as a job or a voluntary association, without a connection to identity or conscience.

### Particularity

The group needs clear boundaries: a person looking at the definition should be able to tell who is in and who is out. Groups defined by broad or subjective terms fail. The Board rejected a group of young men who resist gang recruitment in Matter of S-E-G- partly on this basis, because youth, resistance, and recruitment all lack fixed edges. Wealthy people and people who returned from abroad have failed for the same reason. A group defined by nationality plus gender plus a specific relationship or status tends to pass.

### Social distinction

The society in question must perceive the group as a distinct group. This does not mean members must be visible on sight; the Board clarified in M-E-V-G- that the test is about social perception, not ocular visibility. Evidence comes from outside your own account: country-condition reports describing how the group is treated, laws that single the group out, news coverage, and expert declarations from people who study the society. A group that exists only in the asylum application, with no footprint in the society it describes, fails here.

## The circularity trap

The most common drafting mistake is to define the group by the harm. People targeted by the cartel, or women threatened by their former partners, fails as a definition because the group would not exist without the persecution, and the law requires the group to exist independently of it. The way through is to describe the characteristic that made you a target, not the targeting: the family you belong to, the relationship you were in and could not leave, the role you held. The persecutor's conduct can then serve as evidence that the society sees the group as distinct, which is a different use of the same facts.

## Nexus: one central reason

Defining a valid group is half the job. You must also show that your membership in it was at least one central reason the persecutor harmed or will harm you. That is the standard the REAL ID Act wrote into INA 208(b)(1)(B)(i). A gang that extorts every shop owner on the street is not persecuting you on account of a protected ground, even if you can define a group of shop owners. The evidence for nexus is what the persecutor said and did: threats that name your family, your faith, or your past; a pattern of harm to others who share the trait; the absence of any other motive that explains the targeting. When nexus is weak, the claim may still succeed under the Convention Against Torture, which requires no protected ground at all. ## Groups that have been recognized, and groups still in dispute

Some groups rest on settled precedent. Sexual orientation has been recognized since Matter of Toboso-Alfonso in 1990. Women of a particular tribe who oppose female genital mutilation were recognized in Matter of Kasinga in 1996. Family as a social group has been accepted in many circuits, though the nexus question in family cases is argued hard.

Other groups have had a turbulent decade. Claims by survivors of domestic violence who could not leave the relationship, and claims by people who resisted or left gangs, have seen precedent issued, vacated, and reinstated more than once. As of this writing, those claims are decided case by case on their evidence, with real variation among the circuits. That is not a reason to abandon them; it is a reason to build the record on social distinction and nexus more carefully than a claim on settled ground would require.

## How to frame your group before the hearing

Three practical rules follow from the case law. First, define the group exactly, in writing, before the merits hearing. The Board held in Matter of W-Y-C- & H-O-B- that an applicant must delineate the proposed group before the immigration judge and cannot reformulate it on appeal. Second, propose alternatives. A primary group and one or two narrower fallbacks give the adjudicator a way to grant without adopting a definition they find too broad. Third, plead the other grounds that fit. Political opinion, including imputed political opinion, and religion often overlap with a social group claim, and a judge who rejects the group may grant on the overlap.
