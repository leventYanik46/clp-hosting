No statute puts an outer limit on how long ICE can hold someone whose removal case is still pending, and the Supreme Court has twice declined to read one in. That leaves prolonged immigration detention to be tested one case at a time under the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment, in federal district court, through a habeas corpus petition. The question the court asks is whether detention that may have been lawful on day one has become unreasonable without a hearing at which the government must justify it. There is no bright line, but there are factors courts apply consistently, and after roughly six months to a year of detention without a bond hearing the argument becomes serious.

## Why the statutes do not answer the question

Three Supreme Court decisions frame every prolonged-detention case. In *Demore v. Kim* (2003) the Court upheld mandatory detention under INA 236(c) during removal proceedings, reasoning that such detention was typically brief. In *Jennings v. Rodriguez* (2018) the Court rejected the Ninth Circuit's rule that detainees under sections 235(b), 236(a) and 236(c) must receive a bond hearing every six months, holding that the statutes contain no such requirement, but it sent the constitutional question back to the lower courts without deciding it. In *Johnson v. Arteaga-Martinez* (2022) it did the same for post-order detention under 241(a)(6).

The result is that a detainee whose case has dragged on cannot point to a statute and demand a hearing. The claim is constitutional: that continued detention without an individualized hearing violates due process. ## The factors courts weigh in prolonged immigration detention cases

District courts across the country, including many in the Fourth and Second Circuits, have converged on a set of factors for deciding whether prolonged immigration detention has become unreasonable. No single one controls.

- The total length of detention to date. Courts generally treat detention approaching or exceeding a year as weighing heavily, and some treat six months as the point at which scrutiny begins.
- The likely duration of future detention. If the merits case has a pending appeal and a petition for review ahead of it, detention may continue for many more months, which cuts in the detainee's favor.
- Who caused the delays. Continuances requested by the detainee in good faith to gather evidence or find counsel generally do not count against them; delays caused by the government, the court's docket or transfers weigh for the detainee. Dilatory tactics weigh against.
- The conditions of confinement, especially where immigration detainees are held in criminal jails alongside sentenced inmates.
- The likelihood that the proceedings will end in removal at all. A detainee with a strong claim to relief is being held for a removal that may never happen.
- Whether the detainee has ever had a hearing at which the government bore the burden.

Courts in some circuits also ask whether the detainee's criminal history is of the type that justifies continued detention without a hearing, though many treat that as a question for the bond hearing itself rather than for the threshold due process analysis.

## Who the analysis applies to

Prolonged-detention claims arise under every detention statute. For mandatory detainees under 236(c), the claim is that the premise of brief detention in *Demore* has failed in their case. For arriving aliens and others under 235(b), the claim is that parole review by ICE is no substitute for a neutral decision-maker once detention has lasted long enough. For 236(a) detainees who had a bond hearing long ago, the claim may be that a hearing where they carried the burden is not enough after extended detention, and that a new hearing with the burden on the government is required. For people with final orders, *Zadvydas* supplies a separate framework that we explain in [how long ICE can detain you after a final removal order](/ice-detention-after-final-removal-order).

## What the remedy looks like

When a court finds a due process violation, it rarely orders release outright. The usual remedy is an order that an immigration judge hold a bond hearing within a short number of days. The important variable is the burden of proof. In an ordinary 236(a) hearing, you must prove you are not a danger or flight risk. In a court-ordered prolonged-detention hearing, many courts require the government to prove by clear and convincing evidence that continued detention is justified, and some direct the judge to consider ability to pay and alternatives to detention when setting bond. The Fourth Circuit has held that the Constitution does not require the government to bear the burden in ordinary 236(a) hearings, so the burden question in prolonged-detention cases is argued district by district within that circuit, while courts in the Second Circuit have required the government to carry it in prolonged-detention hearings. The law here is circuit-specific and moving, and the petition is drafted to the law of the district where the client is held.

## Building the record from the first month

Prolonged-detention cases are won on a timeline. From the beginning of detention, keep a record of every hearing date, every continuance and who requested it, every transfer, and every request for release or parole and ICE's response. Preserve evidence of conditions of confinement, medical issues and lost access to counsel. Pursue the merits case diligently; a detainee who is actively litigating relief in good faith presents the strongest due process claim, and the strength of that relief is itself a factor. Where the immigration judge refused a bond hearing outright, the groundwork described in [what to do when a bond hearing is denied](/bond-hearing-denied-habeas-corpus) runs in parallel.

A habeas petition can be filed more than once. If a court denies relief at month seven, the same detainee may have a strong claim at month fourteen, and courts expect the second petition to show what has changed.
