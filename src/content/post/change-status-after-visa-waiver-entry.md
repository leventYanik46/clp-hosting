No. A traveler admitted under the Visa Waiver Program on an ESTA authorization cannot change to another nonimmigrant status and cannot extend the 90-day stay, because INA 248 excludes Visa Waiver entrants from change of status and the program's own rules exclude them from extensions. The exceptions are few and specific: adjustment of status as the immediate relative of a U.S. citizen, an asylum claim, and certain humanitarian categories. This post explains what you give up when you enter on the Visa Waiver Program, the exceptions, the emergency measure called satisfactory departure, and the other groups of nonimmigrants who are barred from changing status.

## What a Visa Waiver entry gives up

The program trades a visa interview for a shorter stay and fewer rights. You are admitted for 90 days, the admission cannot be prolonged, and you waive the right to contest removal other than through an asylum claim. The I-94 class shows WT for tourists or WB for business visitors. A Visa Waiver entrant who stays past 90 days can be removed without a hearing before an immigration judge and loses eligibility to use the program in the future. Because of these limits, our [change of status and extension](/practice-area/key-services/change-and-extension-of-status) team's first question to anyone asking about staying longer is how they entered; the answer determines whether any application is possible at all.

## The exceptions: immediate relatives, asylum, U and T

The main exception is adjustment of status. INA 245(c)(4) bars Visa Waiver entrants from adjusting, but then exempts immediate relatives of U.S. citizens: spouses, unmarried children under 21, and parents of adult citizens. A Visa Waiver entrant who marries a U.S. citizen may therefore file Form I-130 and Form I-485 from inside the United States, and the application is treated as an exercise of discretion that most USCIS offices grant when the marriage is genuine. The timing of the marriage matters. Officers look at whether the plan to marry and stay existed before entry, and conduct inconsistent with a tourist visit within the first 90 days raises a presumption of misrepresentation in consular guidance. The [marriage-based green card](/practice-area/key-services/marriage-based-green-card) page explains the evidence that answers that concern. Asylum may be requested by any person physically present, regardless of how they entered, within one year of arrival. U and T status for crime and trafficking victims are also available to Visa Waiver entrants.

## Satisfactory departure for emergencies

The program has one safety valve. If an emergency such as a medical crisis or a cancelled flight prevents you from leaving within 90 days, you may ask for satisfactory departure, a period of up to 30 days to leave. Requests are made before the 90 days end, to CBP at a Deferred Inspection office or in some cases to USCIS, with proof of the emergency. Satisfactory departure is not an extension of status and does not open the door to any other application; it simply means that a departure within the extra days is not treated as an overstay.

## Other nonimmigrants who cannot change status

The Visa Waiver bar is the best known, but INA 248 and the regulations exclude several other groups:

- J-1 exchange visitors subject to the two-year home residency requirement of INA 212(e), who may change only to A or G status until they complete the requirement or obtain a waiver; the [J-1 visa](/practice-area/key-services/j-1-visa) page explains the waiver routes.
- C transit, D crewmember, K fiancé and S informant nonimmigrants, who are barred by statute. A K-1 fiancé may adjust status only through marriage to the petitioner.
- M-1 vocational students, who may not change to F-1 and may not change to H-1B if the vocational training is what qualified them for the job.
- Transit without visa passengers.
- Anyone who has failed to maintain status, since eligibility under INA 248 requires continued lawful status, subject to the narrow excuse for extraordinary circumstances.

## What to do instead

Leave before day 90 and apply for the visa that matches your plan. A B-2 visitor visa allows admissions of six months at a time and preserves the ability to file Form I-539 later, which our post on [what B-1/B-2 visitors can and cannot do](/b1-b2-visas-in-the-united-states-what-you-can-and-cannot-do) describes. A student applies for an F-1 or J-1 visa with the school's I-20 or DS-2019. A worker needs an employer's petition approved for consular notification before the visa interview. The common thread is that the application happens at a consulate, with a clean record of having left on time. Overstaying to wait for an opportunity that cannot arise under the program is the one choice that forecloses the others, and if it leads to a denial or a removal order the consequences are those described in our post on [what happens when a change of status is denied](/change-of-status-denied-what-happens).

## A note on intent at the border

Visa Waiver admissions are discretionary, and CBP can refuse entry to a traveler whose history suggests an intent to stay. Repeated long visits, a pending immigrant petition, or a prior overstay all draw questions. If you expect to need a change of status at some point, obtaining a visa before travel costs an interview but keeps your options open once you arrive.
