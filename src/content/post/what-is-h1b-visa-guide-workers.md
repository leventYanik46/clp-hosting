The H-1B visa lets a U.S. employer hire a foreign worker in a "specialty occupation," a job that normally requires at least a bachelor's degree in a specific field. It is tied to the sponsoring employer, lasts up to six years in most cases, and allows the worker to pursue a green card at the same time. If you are weighing a U.S. job offer that depends on H-1B sponsorship, here is what the visa does and what to ask before you say yes.

## What is an H-1B visa?

The H-1B is a temporary work visa. Your employer files the petition, not you. Approval lets you work for that employer, in that role, at the location and wage described in the filing. The employer must pay at least the higher of the actual wage it pays similar employees or the prevailing wage for the job in that area, as set out in a Labor Condition Application filed with the Department of Labor.

## Who qualifies

Two conditions must both be met:

1. **The job is a specialty occupation.** Software engineering, accounting, architecture, many healthcare and research roles, and similar positions usually qualify. General management or sales roles often require more explanation.
2. **You have the education for it.** A U.S. bachelor's degree or foreign equivalent in a related field, or a combination of education and experience that USCIS accepts as equivalent.

## The lottery, and how to avoid it

Most private employers are subject to an annual cap, so a lottery decides who can file. Registration is typically in March, with jobs starting October 1. Universities, their affiliated nonprofits, and nonprofit or government research organizations are cap-exempt and can hire on an H-1B at any time.

Recent policy changes, including a 2025 presidential proclamation imposing a large payment on certain new petitions for workers outside the U.S., can affect whether and how an employer files. Ask the employer whether it has budgeted for current costs.

## How long the H-1B lasts

An H-1B is approved for up to three years at a time, with a total of six years in H-1B status. Time spent outside the U.S. can be recaptured. If your employer has started a green card case early enough, you may extend beyond six years.

## What you can and cannot do on an H-1B

- **You can** work full-time or part-time for your sponsoring employer, travel internationally with a valid visa stamp, and pursue a green card, since the H-1B allows immigrant intent.
- **You can** change employers through "portability," starting with the new employer once it files a petition for you, if you are in status.
- **You cannot** freelance, consult on the side, or work for a second company unless that company also files an H-1B petition for you.
- **You cannot** stay indefinitely after a job ends. There is a grace period of up to 60 days, or until your approved period ends if sooner, to find a new sponsor, change status, or leave.

## Your family on an H-1B

Your spouse and unmarried children under 21 can come in H-4 status. Children can attend school. Your spouse can work only with an H-4 employment authorization document, which is available in specific cases, mainly once you have an approved I-140 or have extended past six years under AC21.

## Questions to ask before accepting the offer

- Is the employer cap-exempt, or will I go through the lottery?
- Who pays the legal and government fees? The employer must pay H-1B petition costs and cannot deduct them in a way that drops your wage below the required level.
- What wage level is listed for my role, and does it match my experience?
- Will I work at the employer's site, a client site, or remotely? Location changes can require an amended petition.
- When will the company start my green card, and which category?

## The H-1B is a starting point

For many workers the H-1B leads to an employment-based green card through PERM or, for some, through EB-1 or a national interest waiver. Planning for that path early is easier than starting it in year five.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I work for two companies on an H-1B?
Yes, if each company files its own H-1B petition for you. This is called concurrent employment.

### Can I start a green card process while on an H-1B?
Yes. The H-1B allows dual intent, so pursuing permanent residence does not affect your H-1B status.

### Can I work remotely on an H-1B?
Remote work is possible, but the work location must be covered by a Labor Condition Application. Working from home in another metropolitan area can require a new LCA and sometimes an amended petition.

### What happens if I am laid off?
You have a grace period of up to 60 days, or until your authorized stay ends if sooner, to find a new sponsor, change status, or leave.
