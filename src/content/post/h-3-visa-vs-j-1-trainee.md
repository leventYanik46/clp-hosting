The H-3 visa and the J-1 trainee program both bring a foreign national to a U.S. company for structured training, but they are built differently. The H-3 is a petition the employer files with USCIS on Form I-129, approved for up to two years, with the employer answering for every element of the training plan. The J-1 trainee is a program run by a State Department designated sponsor, limited to 18 months, where the sponsor vets the host company and issues the Form DS-2019. For a company training one or two people in a proprietary process, the H-3 usually fits. For a company that wants a predictable, lower-touch process and can live with the shorter period, the J-1 usually fits. The choice is rarely close once the facts are on the table.

## Who decides and who is accountable

Under the H-3, the petitioner carries the burden. It must prove the training is unavailable in the trainee's home country, that the trainee will not fill a regular position, that productive work is incidental, and that the training supports a career abroad. The plan is reviewed by a USCIS officer, who can issue a Request for Evidence or deny. Our [H-3 trainee visa](/practice-area/key-services/h-3-visa) page describes the evidence that carries those four points.

Under the [J-1 trainee category](/practice-area/key-services/j-1-visa), a designated sponsor decides whether to accept the host and the participant. The sponsor reviews the Form DS-7002 training plan, confirms the host's capacity (including a site visit for smaller hosts), and remains responsible for the participant throughout. The host company signs the plan and must comply with it, but it does not file anything with USCIS, and there is no RFE. The trainee applies for the visa at a consulate with the DS-2019. Sponsors also run intern programs, and the eligibility differences are covered in our post on [J-1 intern versus trainee](/j-1-intern-vs-trainee-category).

## Eligibility of the trainee

The H-3 has no formal education or experience threshold. The regulation instead bars trainees who already possess substantial training and expertise in the field, so the ideal candidate is qualified in the general area but new to the specific skill. The J-1 trainee requires a post-secondary degree plus one year of related experience outside the United States, or five years of related experience. A recent graduate without experience cannot be a J-1 trainee (though may qualify as a J-1 intern), but could be an H-3 trainee if the plan is sound.

## Duration and what happens at the end

The H-3 allows up to two years for a trainee (18 months for the special education exchange visitor variant). After two years in H-3 status, the trainee must reside outside the United States for six months before being readmitted in H or L status or changing to it. The J-1 trainee allows up to 18 months (12 months in hospitality and tourism unless the program includes departmental rotation), followed by a 30-day grace period. A former J-1 trainee must spend two years abroad before another trainee program, with limited exceptions.

The larger exit difference is Section 212(e). A J-1 trainee whose field is on the Skills List for their country, or whose program involved government funding, is subject to the two-year home residency requirement and cannot change to H-1B or receive a green card until it is satisfied or waived. The H-3 carries no such requirement. For companies that may later want to sponsor the person for an H-1B, this can decide the question; see our post on [whether the J-1 two-year rule applies](/j-1-two-year-home-residency-requirement). Neither category is dual intent, and neither leads directly to permanent residence. The post on [what happens after H-3 training ends](/after-h-3-training-ends-options) covers the realistic next steps on that side.

## Productive work and pay

Both categories limit productive employment. The H-3 permits it only when incidental and necessary to the training; a plan that shows the trainee generating ordinary output will be denied. The J-1 trainee program limits clerical work to 20 percent and prohibits unskilled labor and any position that displaces U.S. workers, but it is more accepting of the trainee doing real tasks within the training plan, as long as the DS-7002 phases describe them.

Pay is allowed in both. H-3 petitions must disclose the source and amount of remuneration. J-1 hosts commonly pay a stipend or salary and must confirm the participant has health insurance meeting State Department minimums. Neither category requires a prevailing wage or a labor condition application, which separates both from the H-1B.

## Cost, timing, and control

The H-3 involves the USCIS filing fee for Form I-129 and, where available, premium processing; check whether premium processing currently covers H-3 petitions. Preparation cost is front-loaded in the training plan. Timing depends on USCIS processing, which varies by service center and is published on the USCIS website. The J-1 involves sponsor program fees, the SEVIS fee, and the visa application fee, and the timeline is set by the sponsor's review plus the consular appointment, which is often faster than a USCIS adjudication.

Control runs the other way. The H-3 employer can design the program freely within the regulation and does not answer to a sponsor. The J-1 host accepts sponsor oversight, periodic check-ins, and the possibility that the sponsor withdraws the program if the host departs from the plan.

## A short decision guide

- Choose the H-3 when the training is proprietary, the trainee lacks the J-1 experience threshold, the company wants two years, or a later H-1B or green card may follow and the Skills List is a concern.
- Choose the J-1 trainee when the company wants a sponsor to handle compliance, 18 months is enough, the trainee meets the degree-plus-experience test, and the field is not on the Skills List for the trainee's country.
- Consider the H-1B or [L-1 intracompany transfer](/l-1-visa-guide-transfers) instead when the real goal is a working position rather than training; both of these programs are denied when the plan is a job in disguise.
