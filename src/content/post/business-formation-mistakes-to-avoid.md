The most expensive business formation mistakes are invisible for years. The company grows, customers pay, and nobody notices that the shares were never issued, the IP still belongs to a departed co-founder, or the entity was administratively dissolved for a missed annual report. Then an investor runs diligence, a creditor sues, or the IRS asks a question, and the cleanup costs ten times what doing it right would have. Below are the mistakes we correct most often in our [business formation and structuring](/practice-area/key-services/business-formation-structuring) practice, and what the fix looks like for each.

## Treating the company like a personal bank account

An LLC or corporation protects your personal assets only if you treat it as a separate entity. Paying personal expenses from the business account, depositing customer checks into a personal account, or lending money back and forth without documentation gives a creditor's lawyer the argument that the company is your alter ego. Courts call the remedy piercing the corporate veil, and it ends with the owner personally liable for company debts.

The fix is boring and effective. Open a business bank account under the company's EIN before the first dollar comes in. Pay yourself by documented salary or distribution. Sign every contract in the company's name with your title, not your own name alone. Keep the company's money, records, and signatures separate from yours, and the liability shield holds.

## Running a multi-owner company on a handshake

Two or more owners with no operating agreement or shareholder agreement are running on their state's default rules, which may split profits equally regardless of who put in the money, let any member bind the company, or require unanimous consent for routine decisions. The real damage comes when one owner wants out. Without a buyout mechanism and a valuation formula, the exit becomes litigation or a hostage negotiation.

If your company has co-owners and no signed agreement, make one now, even if the business is already running. Our post on [what a multi-member LLC operating agreement should cover](/llc-operating-agreement-what-to-include) lists the terms that matter. For corporations, the equivalent is a shareholder agreement paired with bylaws, which CLP's [corporate governance and internal policies](/practice-area/key-services/corporate-governance-and-internal-policies) team drafts for companies at every stage.

## Issuing equity without the paperwork

We regularly meet founders who have "owned 40 percent" for two years and have never received a stock certificate, a unit grant, or a board consent approving the issuance. Sometimes the shares were promised to an early employee by email. Sometimes an advisor was told they would get "a point or two." None of those promises is self-executing, and every one of them becomes a dispute at the first financing, when the investor's counsel asks for a cap table that matches the documents.

Issue founder equity on the day the company is formed, with a restricted stock purchase agreement, a vesting schedule, and an 83(b) election filed within 30 days. Put every later grant through a written agreement and a board or manager approval. Our post on [how co-founders should split equity and set vesting](/founder-equity-split-and-vesting) explains each of those steps. If promised equity was never issued, the fix is to issue it now at current value and under current terms, which may mean a different tax result for the recipient than they expected.

## Leaving intellectual property with the founders

The company does not automatically own what its founders and contractors create. A founder who wrote the first version of the software before incorporation owns that code personally until a written assignment transfers it. An independent developer who built your app owns the copyright in that app unless the contract says otherwise. Investors and acquirers check this chain of title line by line, and a gap involving a founder who has since left can stall or kill a deal.

Every founder, employee, and contractor should sign an invention assignment and confidentiality agreement at the start of the relationship. For departed contributors, obtain a signed assignment now, before the relationship sours further. Naming is the other IP mistake: choosing a company name, forming the entity, and printing materials before anyone checks whether the name is available as a trademark. Our [trademark search and clearance](/practice-area/key-services/trademark-search-and-clearance) page explains why that search belongs before formation, not after.

## Skipping registrations, licenses, and reports

Formation is one filing. Staying in good standing is a calendar. Companies miss their state annual or biennial report and are administratively dissolved without noticing, which means the liability shield lapses and contracts signed during the gap are open to challenge. Companies formed in Delaware forget to register as a foreign entity in Virginia or New York where they actually operate, and discover the problem when a court refuses to hear their collection lawsuit. DC businesses open without the Basic Business License the District requires for most activities.

Each of these has a reinstatement or late-registration procedure, usually with back fees and penalties. Federal beneficial ownership reporting under the Corporate Transparency Act has changed more than once; as of this writing, domestic companies are exempt from filing, but confirm the current rule rather than assume. Our post on [whether to incorporate in Delaware or your home state](/where-to-incorporate-delaware-or-home-state) covers the state-by-state obligations.

## How formation mistakes get fixed

Most of these problems are repairable. Missing equity gets issued with a ratifying board resolution. Missing assignments get signed. Dissolved entities get reinstated. Unregistered foreign entities get registered with back fees paid. The rules against backdating are strict, so the fix documents what happened and corrects it going forward rather than pretending it was done at the time. Some mistakes are not fully repairable: a missed 83(b) deadline cannot be cured, and an entity type chosen for the wrong reasons, such as an S corporation with a foreign owner who was never eligible, may need a restructuring with tax consequences.

The cheapest time to address any of this is before a financing, a sale, or a lawsuit forces the issue. A formation review takes a few hours and produces a short list of documents to sign and filings to make. Most founders are surprised by how short that list is, and relieved by how much it closes off.
