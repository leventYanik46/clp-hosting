The difference between guardianship vs power of attorney comes down to who makes the choice and when. A power of attorney is a private document: a person with capacity signs it, picks the agent, and sets the limits. A guardianship is a court order: a judge decides that the person has lost capacity and appoints someone to act, with or without the person's consent. One is inexpensive, fast, and revocable. The other is public, supervised, and hard to undo. Families usually end up in court because the first option was never used in time. Here is how to tell which one fits your situation, and what happens when both exist at once.

## What a power of attorney can and cannot do

A durable power of attorney gives a named agent authority over financial matters: paying bills, managing accounts, selling property, dealing with insurers and the IRS. "Durable" means it keeps working after the principal loses capacity, which is the whole point. A separate document, called an advance medical directive in Virginia, a durable power of attorney for health care in DC, or a health care proxy in New York, covers medical decisions.

Three limits matter. First, the principal must have capacity on the day they sign. A parent who already cannot understand what the document does cannot validly execute one, and a notary's stamp will not cure that. Second, the agent's authority is only as broad as the document. If it does not mention gifts, real estate, or retirement accounts, the agent may be stuck. Third, a power of attorney gives authority but not control over the principal. If your father has an agent for finances but keeps driving, refusing care, or letting a scammer into the house, the agent cannot stop him.

## What a guardianship adds, and what it costs

A guardian appointed by the court can make the personal decisions a power of attorney never reaches: where the person lives, whether they accept treatment, who may visit. If finances are the problem, the court appoints a conservator (Virginia and DC) or a guardian of the property (New York). Our [legal guardianship page](/practice-area/key-services/legal-guardianship) describes the petition, and the companion [conservatorship page](/practice-area/key-services/conservatorship) covers the financial side.

The price of that authority is court oversight. There is a petition, a capacity evaluation, notice to relatives, a hearing, and in many cases a bond. The guardian files reports for as long as the order lasts, and a conservator files accountings that someone at the court reviews line by line. The proceeding is on the public record. And the person at the center loses rights, which is why every jurisdiction requires the judge to consider whether a power of attorney or another less restrictive tool would work instead. We lay out the legal threshold in [when an adult needs a court-appointed guardian](/when-adult-needs-court-appointed-guardian).

## When a power of attorney exists but is not enough

Having a signed document does not always keep a family out of court. The common situations:

- A bank or brokerage refuses to honor the document, often because it is old, lacks specific powers, or was signed in another state. Sometimes a lawyer's letter resolves it; sometimes a conservatorship is the only way to reach the account.
- The agent is misusing the money or has simply disappeared. The principal cannot revoke the document because they no longer have capacity, so a relative petitions for a conservator, and the court can suspend or terminate the agent's authority.
- The document covers finances but the real problem is personal: refusing medical care, living in an unsafe home, being isolated by a caregiver.
- The document was signed after capacity was already in doubt, and a sibling challenges it. A court proceeding then has to sort out both the document and the person's current condition.

In Virginia, the Uniform Power of Attorney Act gives the court a way to handle the overlap: if a conservator is appointed, the agent becomes accountable to the conservator, and the court can limit or end the agent's authority. The same statute lets the principal nominate, inside the power of attorney, the person they want as conservator or guardian if one is ever needed. Courts generally follow that nomination absent good cause. That one clause is often the most valuable sentence in the document.

## Guardianship vs power of attorney: which to choose now

If the person still has capacity, the answer is almost always a power of attorney and an advance directive, signed now, with successor agents named and a nomination clause included. Pair them with a funded revocable trust if there is real estate or a sizable portfolio, as explained in [whether a trust or power of attorney can avoid a conservatorship](/avoid-conservatorship-trust-power-of-attorney). These documents belong in any [estate plan](/practice-area/estate-planning), and our guide to [estate planning in the United States](/what-is-estate-planning-in-the-us) shows where they fit.

If capacity is already gone, a guardianship or conservatorship petition is the only route, and the question becomes how narrow the order can be. If the family is split about who should serve, expect the court to decide, and expect the process to take longer.

The worst position is the middle one: capacity is slipping, no documents exist, and everyone is waiting for someone else to raise it. A short meeting with a lawyer while the person can still participate turns a future court case into a signing appointment.
