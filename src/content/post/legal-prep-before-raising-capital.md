The legal prep before raising capital comes down to one test: when an investor's lawyers open your data room, will they find a company whose ownership, intellectual property, and records match what you told them? Most delays in a seed or Series A round come from gaps that were cheap to fix earlier: a cofounder who never signed an IP assignment, stock issued without board approval, an advisor promised equity in an email, or a missed tax election. Fix those before the term sheet, and diligence becomes a formality instead of a renegotiation.

A pre-financing review is one of the most common projects in our [strategic legal guidance for businesses](/practice-area/key-services/strategic-legal-guidance-for-businesses) work with founders. These are the areas it covers.

## Clean up the cap table before raising capital

Your capitalization table should show every share, option, warrant, SAFE, and convertible note, and every line should be backed by a signed document and a board or owner approval. Common problems:

- Equity promised informally to early employees, advisors, or contractors, with no grant document.
- Founder shares issued without vesting, which investors often ask to add.
- Missing 83(b) elections. A founder who receives restricted stock subject to vesting generally has 30 days from the grant to file an 83(b) election with the IRS. Missing that deadline can create taxable income as the shares vest, and it usually cannot be fixed after the fact.
- Stock options granted without a current Section 409A valuation, which can create tax problems for the employees who hold them.

Fix informal promises by documenting them now, with a clear grant or a written release, rather than letting them surface during diligence.

## Confirm the company owns its intellectual property

Investors are buying into the company's assets, and for many startups the main asset is code, a brand, or know-how. Every founder, employee, and contractor who built something should have signed an agreement assigning inventions and work product to the company. Pay special attention to work done before the company was formed, work by offshore developers, and work by a cofounder who has since left. Check your trademark position as well, because an investor will want to know you can keep your name.

## Put corporate records in order

Gather formation documents, bylaws or the operating agreement, every board and stockholder consent, and state filings showing good standing. Many venture investors prefer a Delaware corporation, so an LLC planning a priced institutional round may need to convert, which is easier and cheaper before the financing than during it. Make sure the company is registered in each state where it has employees or operations. Our [corporate governance and internal policies](/practice-area/key-services/corporate-governance-and-internal-policies) team often handles this cleanup before a round.

Collect your key commercial contracts, employment agreements, and a list of any pending or threatened claims too. Diligence request lists will ask for all of them, and surprises late in a deal cost time and price.

## Choose the instrument and follow securities law

Every sale of equity or convertible instruments is a securities offering, and it must be registered or exempt. Most private rounds rely on Regulation D.

### Rule 506(b)
Allows unlimited accredited investors and a limited number of non-accredited but financially sophisticated investors, but prohibits general solicitation. In practice, you raise from people you already have a relationship with.

### Rule 506(c)
Allows general solicitation, such as public posts about the raise, but every purchaser must be accredited, and the company must take reasonable steps to verify that status.

Under either rule, the company files a Form D with the SEC within 15 days after the first sale, and most states require their own notice filing. Early rounds often use SAFEs or convertible notes, which defer the valuation question; priced rounds set a valuation and usually bring board seats, protective provisions, and investor rights agreements. Understand how each instrument converts and how much it dilutes the founders before you sign.

## Build a data room and plan the negotiation

A simple, well-organized data room signals a well-run company. Sort documents into corporate, equity, IP, contracts, employment, financial, and litigation folders, and prepare a short list of known issues. Disclosing a problem along with a plan to fix it lands far better than an investor finding it alone.

Think about the terms you care about beyond valuation: board composition, liquidation preference, protective provisions, and founder vesting. These terms shape control of the company for years, which is where strategic counsel earns its keep.
