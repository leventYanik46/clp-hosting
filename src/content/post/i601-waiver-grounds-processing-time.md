Form I-601 asks USCIS to forgive a ground of inadmissibility so you can get a green card or immigrant visa despite a past problem, such as fraud, certain crimes, or unlawful presence discovered at a consular interview. It is different from the I-601A provisional waiver, which covers only unlawful presence and is filed before you leave the U.S. Most I-601 waivers require showing that your U.S. citizen or permanent resident relative would suffer extreme hardship if you were refused.

## What an I-601 waiver can cover

Common grounds of inadmissibility waivable through Form I-601 include:

- **Fraud or willful misrepresentation** to obtain an immigration benefit, under section 212(i).
- **Certain criminal grounds**, such as crimes involving moral turpitude and some drug offenses involving a single offense of simple possession of 30 grams or less of marijuana, under section 212(h).
- **Unlawful presence** bars of three or ten years, when not handled through the I-601A process.
- **Certain health-related grounds.**

Some grounds cannot be waived, including most drug trafficking findings, false claims to U.S. citizenship in most cases, and the permanent bar for reentering illegally after a year of unlawful presence, which uses a different process.

## Qualifying relatives

Who counts as a qualifying relative depends on the ground:

- **Fraud and unlawful presence**: a U.S. citizen or permanent resident spouse or parent.
- **Criminal grounds under 212(h)**: a U.S. citizen or permanent resident spouse, parent, son, or daughter.

Hardship to you, the applicant, generally does not count except in specific situations. A U.S. citizen child is not a qualifying relative for fraud or unlawful presence waivers, which surprises many families.

## Proving extreme hardship

Extreme hardship means hardship beyond the ordinary consequences of separation or relocation. USCIS looks at the qualifying relative's situation in two scenarios: if they stay in the U.S. without you, and if they move abroad with you. Strong evidence covers:

- **Health**: medical conditions and access to treatment.
- **Finances**: loss of income, debts, and the cost of supporting two households.
- **Family ties**: care for children or aging parents in the U.S.
- **Country conditions**: safety, healthcare, and economic conditions in your country.
- **Education and career**: disruption to the relative's work or schooling.

Documents should be specific and verifiable: medical records, tax returns, letters from doctors and employers, and reports on country conditions.

## Where to file and how long it takes

Applicants abroad file the I-601 after a consular officer finds them inadmissible. Applicants adjusting status in the U.S. file it with or after Form I-485.

I-601 processing times vary widely by office and change through the year. USCIS posts current times on its processing times page. Waits of many months are common. Applicants abroad remain outside the U.S. while the waiver is pending, which is why many families with only an unlawful presence issue use the I-601A instead.

## I-601 vs I-601A

| | I-601 | I-601A |
|---|---|---|
| Grounds covered | Many waivable grounds | Unlawful presence only |
| When filed | After inadmissibility finding, or with adjustment | Before departing for consular interview |
| Applicant location | Often abroad | In the U.S. |

## Common mistakes

- Presenting general hardship that applies to any separated family.
- Missing a second ground of inadmissibility the I-601 also needs to cover.
- Filing without addressing the discretionary factors, positive and negative.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I file the I-601 before my consular interview?
For grounds other than unlawful presence, the I-601 is generally filed after a consular officer finds you inadmissible. The I-601A provisional waiver is the exception for unlawful presence only.

### Does the I-601 cover a prior deportation?
No. A prior removal usually requires Form I-212, Permission to Reapply for Admission, sometimes in addition to an I-601.

### Can a U.S. citizen child be a qualifying relative?
For most grounds, no. Under section 212(h) for certain crimes, a U.S. citizen or permanent resident son or daughter can count.

### Is there a fee for the I-601?
Yes. Check the current USCIS fee schedule before filing.

### What happens if the waiver is denied?
Denials may be appealed to the Administrative Appeals Office or addressed through a motion or new filing with stronger evidence.

### Can I travel while the I-601 is pending?
If you are abroad, you wait abroad. If you filed with an adjustment application in the U.S., travel requires advance parole and carries risks.

### How much evidence is enough?
There is no set number of documents. Strong waivers combine medical, financial, and country evidence into a clear account of how the qualifying relative would be affected.

### Do I need to show remorse for past fraud?
Discretion matters. Acknowledging past misconduct and showing rehabilitation can help in the discretionary part of the decision.
