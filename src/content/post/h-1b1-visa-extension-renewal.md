H-1B1 status is granted one year at a time, so an H-1B1 visa extension is not an occasional event. It is an annual cycle that you and your employer should put on the calendar the day you arrive. You can renew in one of two ways: your employer files Form I-129 with USCIS to extend your stay inside the United States, or you travel to a consulate and apply for a new H-1B1 visa. There is no six-year ceiling of the kind that limits the H-1B, but each renewal is a fresh review of your job, your wage, and your intent to depart. Our [H-1B1 extension and renewal team](/practice-area/key-services/h-1b1-visa) handles both routes, and the choice between them usually turns on your travel plans.

## Why the H-1B1 runs on a one-year clock

The trade agreements with Chile and Singapore produced a category with a short initial period: admission for up to one year, renewable in one-year increments. Compare the H-1B, approved for up to three years at a time, or the E-3 for Australians, approved for up to two. The shorter clock is the H-1B1's main administrative cost. It also means intent to depart is reviewed more often than in any other specialty occupation category, a point we return to below.

## Route 1: Extending your stay with USCIS

The employer files Form I-129 requesting an extension of H-1B1 stay with the same employer. The filing needs a certified LCA that covers the new period. If the LCA used for the first year still runs long enough, the employer may be able to rely on it; if not, a new LCA must be certified before the I-129 can be filed. Premium processing is available.

Two rules make this route attractive.

### The 240-day rule

If the I-129 extension is filed before your current I-94 expires, you may keep working for the same employer, on the same terms, for up to 240 days after the I-94 end date while USCIS decides the case. This protection was extended to H-1B1 workers by regulation in 2017 and is the main reason employers file in-country.

### No consular interview

An in-country extension does not put you in front of a consular officer, which matters for anyone whose immigration plans have become more complicated since the last visa interview.

The limits are real too. An approved extension gives you a new I-94, not a new visa stamp. The next time you leave the United States, you will need a valid H-1B1 visa to return, which means a consular application anyway. And if you travel while the extension is pending, you will need a visa to come back and USCIS may treat the extension request as abandoned.

## Route 2: Renewing at a consulate

The alternative is to leave the United States, apply for a new H-1B1 visa with the current LCA, and re-enter for another year. Bring the LCA, a letter from the employer confirming your continued employment and current salary, your recent pay statements, and your most recent tax documents showing that you were paid at least the LCA wage. The consulate may or may not require an interview, depending on the post and on current State Department policy.

The advantage is a visa stamp that lets you travel freely for the coming year. The risk is a 221(g) hold while you are outside the country, with your job waiting. Choose this route when you were planning to travel anyway, your file is straightforward, and you have an appointment confirmed before you book flights. Our guide on [applying for an H-1B1 visa](/how-to-apply-h-1b1-visa) describes the consular process step by step.

## What has to stay the same, and what triggers a new filing

A pay raise in the same role, or a move within the same metropolitan area already covered by the LCA, does not require a new filing. A different occupation, a worksite outside the area on the LCA, a cut from full-time to part-time hours, or a change of employer does.

The last of those needs particular care. The H-1B1 has no portability rule. Unlike an H-1B worker, you cannot start with a new employer when its petition is filed. The new employer must certify its own LCA and either obtain USCIS approval of an I-129 change of employer request or send you to a consulate for a new visa, and you may start only after one of those is complete. Our [change and extension of status](/practice-area/key-services/change-and-extension-of-status) page covers how we time those moves.

## Intent questions get sharper over time

Each renewal is a new opportunity for an officer to ask whether your stay is still temporary. After several years, a home purchase, or the start of a green card process, the answer takes more care. A pending or approved immigrant petition is not an automatic bar to an H-1B1 renewal, but it has to be explained, and a consular interview is where that explanation is tested hardest. If permanent residence is your goal, our article on [moving from H-1B1 to a green card](/h-1b1-to-green-card-path) explains why many clients rely on in-country extensions once the immigrant process starts, or switch to H-1B along the way.

## If the job ends between renewals

If your employment ends before the next renewal, you are not out of status immediately. H-1B1 workers and their dependents receive a grace period of up to 60 days, or until the I-94 expires if sooner, once per authorized validity period. During that time you may not work, but a new employer can file for you, you can apply to change to another status, or you can prepare to leave. Filing after the grace period ends is too late.

## A renewal calendar that works

Start four to six months before your I-94 end date. Confirm that the job, worksite, and hours still match the LCA, and whether the LCA covers the new period. Decide whether you need to travel in the coming year, because that usually decides the route. If you will extend in-country, have the I-129 filed well before the I-94 expires so the 240-day rule protects you. If you will renew abroad, book the appointment before you book the trip. Then check your new I-94 the week you receive it.
