You have 30 days from a USCIS denial to file Form I-290B, and roughly six years to file a federal lawsuit, and that asymmetry alone explains why most people start with the I-290B appeal even when a court is the better forum. The choice between an I-290B appeal or motion and an Administrative Procedure Act suit turns on four questions: what kind of error USCIS made, whether you have new evidence, how fast you need an answer, and who you want deciding. CLP's [immigration litigation team](/practice-area/key-services/immigration-litigation) works through those questions with clients in the first week after a denial, because the first option expires fast and the second depends on what the first leaves behind.

## What an I-290B appeal or motion can do

Form I-290B, Notice of Appeal or Motion, covers three different filings, and the denial notice tells you which are available.

An appeal goes to the Administrative Appeals Office, which reviews the case fresh, can consider a brief and, in many cases, additional evidence submitted with the appeal, and issues a written decision. Only certain form types are appealable to the AAO; a denied I-130, for example, goes to the Board of Immigration Appeals instead, and most I-485 denials are not appealable at all.

A motion to reopen asks the same office that denied the case to look again based on new facts, supported by evidence that was not previously available. A motion to reconsider argues that the office misapplied law or policy to the facts already in the record, and must cite the authority it relies on. Both are decided by the office that said no.

All three are due within 30 days of the decision, 33 if it was mailed, with the current USCIS filing fee. Every clock that follows a denial is listed in [immigration appeal deadlines after a denial or removal order](/immigration-appeal-deadlines-explained).

## What a federal lawsuit can do

An APA suit asks a district judge to set aside a denial that is arbitrary, capricious or contrary to law, and to send the case back for a lawful decision. The judge is independent of USCIS, decides questions of law without deferring to the agency, and reads the record with fresh eyes. [How an APA lawsuit challenges a USCIS denial](/apa-lawsuit-uscis-denial) describes the standard in detail.

The costs are real. The suit is decided on the administrative record as it stood at the denial, so nothing new comes in. It takes longer and costs more than an I-290B. And the remedy is a remand, after which USCIS decides again, though a reasoned court opinion usually constrains that second decision considerably.

## Do you have to file the I-290B first?

Generally no. Under the Supreme Court's Darby v. Cisneros decision, a plaintiff must exhaust an optional administrative appeal before an APA suit only when a statute or regulation requires it and the agency's decision is held in abeyance during the appeal. USCIS regulations make the AAO appeal optional and do not suspend the denial while it is pending, so most courts have not required an I-290B before suit. Some judges still view a skipped appeal unfavorably when the issue could have been fixed administratively, and the law can vary by district, so confirm the position in your venue before relying on it.

## A side-by-side view

The decision usually resolves along these lines:

- Choose the I-290B motion to reopen when the denial turns on missing or weak evidence that you can now supply. A court cannot take that evidence; USCIS can.
- Choose the I-290B appeal when the issue is legal but you want a faster, cheaper first attempt and the AAO has not already ruled against your position in similar decisions.
- Choose the federal suit when USCIS applied the wrong legal standard, ignored evidence that was clearly in the record, or denied in a way that departs from its own policy, and when the AAO has already rejected your argument in comparable cases.
- Choose a refile when a clean new petition would be approved faster than either route, which is common for employment cases where status is lapsing; [what happens if your H-1B is denied](/what-happens-if-h-1b-denied) walks through that calculation.
- Choose both in sequence when you need the I-290B to put evidence and legal argument into the record, so that any later lawsuit reviews a complete file.

## Using the I-290B to build the record

Because a federal court is confined to the administrative record, the I-290B is frequently the last chance to shape that record. A motion to reopen with a thorough brief, expert letters and organized exhibits means that if the AAO or the field office denies again, the judge sees a complete case and a denial that had to engage with it. A bare I-290B filed to preserve time, with nothing added, does little for the later suit. This is why we treat the two options as stages rather than alternatives in many cases.

## What about the Board of Immigration Appeals?

Denied family petitions follow a different track. An I-130 denial is appealed to the Board on Form EOIR-29 within 30 days, and the Board's decision can then be challenged in district court under the APA.
