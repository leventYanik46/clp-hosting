Filing Form I-130 for a brother or sister is the only family-based route for siblings, and it opens the longest line in family immigration. The petition itself is not complicated: you prove that you are a U.S. citizen who is at least 21, and you prove that you and your sibling share a parent. What matters is filing it early, because the date USCIS receives the I-130 for a sibling becomes the priority date that controls everything afterward. CLP's [F4 sibling green card](/practice-area/key-services/sibling-green-card) practice prepares these petitions; here is what goes into one and what happens after you mail it.

## Who can file, and who counts as a sibling

Only a U.S. citizen aged 21 or older can petition for a sibling. A permanent resident cannot, no matter how long they have held the card, so a resident who is close to naturalizing should have the sibling petition ready to file the week after the oath ceremony.

The beneficiary must be your brother or sister under the Immigration and Nationality Act, which means that each of you once qualified as the "child" of a common parent under section 101(b)(1). Full siblings qualify on two birth certificates. Half-siblings qualify through one shared parent, step-siblings qualify if the parents' marriage took place before the step-sibling turned 18, and adopted siblings qualify if the adoption met the age-16 and two-year custody and residence rules. Our post on [half-siblings and step-siblings](/half-sibling-step-sibling-green-card) goes through each test. Your sibling may be any age and may be married; a spouse and unmarried children under 21 come along as derivatives on the same petition.

## Preparing the I-130 petition for a sibling

You can file Form I-130 online through a USCIS account or on paper to the lockbox listed in the instructions. There is no Form I-130A for a sibling case; that supplement is for spouses only. The petition asks for your sibling's biographic details, address history and, in the section on the beneficiary's family, the names of the spouse and children who will be derivatives. List them all, even a child who seems too young to matter.

The evidence falls into three groups:

- Your status: a copy of your U.S. passport, naturalization certificate, or U.S. birth certificate.
- The common parent: your birth certificate and your sibling's birth certificate, each naming the shared parent. If the shared parent is your father and either of you was born out of wedlock, add proof of legitimation or of a bona fide father-child relationship. For step-siblings, add the parents' marriage certificate and proof that earlier marriages ended. For adopted siblings, add the adoption decree and custody and residence records.
- Name changes and translations: marriage certificates or court orders for any name that differs across documents, and certified English translations for every foreign-language record.

Pay the current USCIS filing fee listed on the form page and keep proof of payment. Our general guide to what Form I-130 is and how it works covers the form sections in more detail.

## What happens after USCIS receives it

Within a few weeks you should receive Form I-797C, the receipt notice. The receipt date printed on it is the priority date. Keep that notice; it is the single most important document in the case for the next decade. USCIS may issue a Request for Evidence if the parental link is unclear, and then an approval notice. Approval means USCIS accepts the relationship. It does not mean a visa is available, and it does not speed anything up. The approved petition is sent to the National Visa Center, where it waits until the Visa Bulletin reaches your priority date. Our post on [why the F4 priority date wait is so long](/f4-sibling-priority-date-wait) explains the mechanics, including the two Visa Bulletin charts you will be watching. USCIS processing of the petition itself varies; check the current USCIS processing times rather than assuming.

## Years later: the National Visa Center and the interview

When the Dates for Filing chart reaches your sibling's priority date, the NVC sends a welcome notice with a case number and invoice. From there the steps are the same as any consular case: you pay the immigrant visa and affidavit of support fees, each family member completes Form DS-260, your sibling uploads civil documents and police certificates, and you submit Form I-864 showing income at or above 125 percent of the Federal Poverty Guidelines for a household that now includes your sibling's family. If you live abroad, you must show you will reestablish a U.S. domicile before they arrive. Once the Final Action Date is also reached and the file is complete, the NVC schedules the interview at the embassy or consulate. Our [family reunification](/practice-area/key-services/family-reunification) page walks through this stage.

If your sibling happens to be in the United States in valid nonimmigrant status when the date becomes current, Form I-485 with USCIS may be possible instead. Unlike a spouse or minor child of a citizen, a sibling is not forgiven an overstay or unauthorized work, so this route is narrow.

## Keeping the petition alive in the meantime

- Keep your own address current with USCIS and, once the case moves, with the NVC. Your sibling should do the same.
- Report marriages, divorces, births and deaths in your sibling's household as they happen, so derivatives are added or removed before the interview.
- Respond to every NVC notice. The State Department can terminate the registration if nobody pursues the visa within one year of being told it is available.
- Keep filing your U.S. tax returns. The I-864 will ask for the most recent one.
- If you die before the case finishes, the petition is revoked automatically but may be saved under section 204(l) or through humanitarian reinstatement; see our post on [what happens to a sibling petition when the petitioner dies](/sibling-petition-when-petitioner-dies).
- If your sibling applies for a visitor or student visa during the wait, the pending petition must be disclosed.
