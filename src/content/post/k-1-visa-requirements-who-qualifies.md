The K-1 visa requirements are short to list and easy to fail. The petitioner must be a U.S. citizen, both partners must be legally free to marry, both must genuinely intend to marry within 90 days of the fiancé's arrival, and the couple must have met in person at least once in the two years before the petition is filed. Add the disclosures required by the International Marriage Broker Regulation Act and a financial showing at the consulate, and that is the whole test. Most denials come from a requirement the couple assumed they met, not from one they ignored.

## K-1 visa requirements for the petitioner

Only a U.S. citizen can file Form I-129F. A lawful permanent resident cannot petition for a fiancé at all; the options there are to marry first and file Form I-130 in the F2A category, or to naturalize and then petition. Citizenship is proven with a passport, a birth certificate or a naturalization certificate.

The citizen must also be free to marry. Any prior marriage must have ended in divorce, annulment or death, with the decree or certificate in the file. Couples sometimes discover at this stage that a divorce abroad was never finalized, or that a religious divorce was never registered civilly. Those gaps must be closed before filing, because an I-129F filed while either partner is still married is denied.

The petitioner's criminal history matters in two ways. IMBRA requires the petitioner to disclose convictions for specified crimes, including domestic violence, sexual assault, child abuse, stalking and certain other violent offenses, and that information is shared with the fiancé at the consulate. A petitioner who has filed two or more prior K-1 petitions, or had one approved within the past two years, must request a waiver of the multiple-filing limit, and a petitioner with a violent criminal history faces a stricter standard for that waiver. Our [K-1 fiancé visa](/practice-area/key-services/k-1-fiance-visa) practice reviews the petitioner's record before anything is filed, because an omission here is treated as misrepresentation.

## Requirements for the fiancé

The fiancé must be free to marry on the same terms, and must be admissible to the United States. The consular stage includes a medical examination, police certificates and the standard inadmissibility review. A prior U.S. overstay, a criminal record, or a previous visa refusal for misrepresentation does not change the I-129F analysis, but it can stop the visa at the consulate, sometimes permanently. We check for those issues at the start so the couple knows whether a waiver will be needed and whether the K-1 is the right vehicle.

The fiancé's unmarried children under 21 can accompany or follow on K-2 visas. They must be named on the I-129F, and a child who follows later must apply within one year of the K-1 issuance.

## The in-person meeting rule

The couple must have met in person within the two years before the petition is filed. Video calls, messaging and years of correspondence do not count. The meeting does not have to be long or romantic; a single trip with passport stamps, boarding passes and dated photographs together satisfies the rule. What fails is proof that shows only one partner, or a meeting that falls just outside the two-year window because the petition was delayed.

USCIS can waive the meeting requirement on two grounds. The first is that meeting would violate strict and long-established customs of the fiancé's foreign culture or social practice, documented with evidence of the custom and the couple's adherence to it. The second is extreme hardship to the petitioner, usually medical. Both waivers are granted sparingly, and a couple that can travel should travel rather than rely on one.

## Intent to marry within 90 days

Each partner signs a statement of intent to marry within 90 days of the fiancé's entry. The statement is the legal core of the petition, but USCIS and the consular officer look behind it for a real relationship and a real plan. Evidence of the relationship over time, communication records, visits, involvement of family and friends and any wedding planning all support it. A couple with a large age gap, little shared language or a very short courtship is not disqualified, but should expect closer questions at the interview and prepare for them.

## The financial showing

At the consulate, the petitioner provides Form I-134, a declaration of financial support, with tax documents and proof of income. The officer uses it to assess whether the fiancé is likely to become a public charge. This is a lighter test than the Form I-864 affidavit of support that comes later; the I-864 is an enforceable contract and requires household income at or above 125 percent of the Federal Poverty Guidelines, and it is filed with the green card application after the wedding. A petitioner who cannot meet the I-864 test should know that before the fiancé arrives, since a joint sponsor is permitted at the adjustment stage. Same-sex couples qualify on identical terms, whatever the law in the fiancé's home country.
