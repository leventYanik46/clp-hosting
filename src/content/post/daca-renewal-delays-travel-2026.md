DACA renewal delays have grown sharply in 2026. Members of Congress have written to DHS and USCIS reporting renewals pending well past the timeframes USCIS posts, and some recipients have lost work authorization while waiting. If your DACA expires in the next year, the single most useful step is to file early and keep proof of everything.

## Why DACA renewals are taking so long

USCIS has not given a complete explanation. Congressional letters sent this spring asked the agency about staffing, added background checks, and whether renewals had been paused. What recipients are seeing in practice:

- Renewals pending longer than the processing times USCIS publishes
- Requests for evidence on cases that would once have been routine
- Expedite requests that are harder to get approved

## When to file your DACA renewal

USCIS guidance has long recommended filing between 120 and 150 days before your current DACA expires. Given current delays, many legal service providers now suggest filing as early as USCIS will accept. USCIS generally may reject a renewal filed more than 150 days before expiration, so confirm the current guidance before you mail it, and file as close to the earliest accepted date as you can.

Keep in mind that DHS ended automatic extensions for most EAD renewals filed on or after October 30, 2025. If your DACA and work permit expire before the renewal is approved, you may not be able to work in the gap.

## If your renewal is taking too long

1. **Track the case online.** Create a USCIS online account and link your receipt number.
2. **Submit an outside-normal-processing-time inquiry** once your case passes the posted timeframe.
3. **Request an expedite** if you face severe financial loss, such as losing a job, or another urgent reason. Provide proof.
4. **Contact your congressional representative's office.** Casework staff can send inquiries to USCIS on your behalf.
5. **Talk to your employer early** if your work permit will expire, so they understand the situation and can plan for reverification.

## Can DACA recipients travel within the U.S.?

Domestic travel is generally allowed with valid identification. REAL ID-compliant state IDs and an unexpired EAD are commonly used for flights. However, enforcement activity has increased, and recipients whose DACA has lapsed face more risk. Carry proof of your DACA approval or pending renewal, and consider avoiding travel near the border or through areas with immigration checkpoints if your status is uncertain.

## Can DACA recipients travel abroad?

Only with advance parole, granted on Form I-131 for humanitarian, educational, or employment purposes. Leaving without it can end your DACA and trigger reentry bars. Even with advance parole, reentry is not guaranteed. Travel on advance parole can sometimes help with a future green card case, but it should be planned with an attorney.

## What a lapse in DACA means

If your DACA expires before renewal:

- You lose work authorization until the renewal is approved
- You may begin accruing unlawful presence, which can matter for future cases
- Your exposure to enforcement may increase

If more than a year passes after expiration, USCIS may require you to file as an initial request, which carries different rules.


## Frequently asked questions

### Does DACA renewal cost the same if I file online?

USCIS fees differ for online and paper filing for some forms. Check the current fee schedule and form instructions for Form I-821D and Form I-765 before filing.

### Can I still file an initial DACA request?

Court rulings have shaped whether USCIS can approve initial DACA requests. Check the current status of the litigation and USCIS guidance before filing an initial request.

### Will a renewal delay hurt my future green card case?

A gap in DACA can lead to accrual of unlawful presence, which can matter if you ever need to leave the U.S. for consular processing. Filing on time and keeping proof helps protect you.

## Looking at longer-term options

Some DACA recipients qualify for a green card through a U.S. citizen spouse, an employer, or other relief. Reviewing those options now, before a gap in status, can open doors that later close.
