When your parent lives outside the United States, the green card is issued as an immigrant visa at a U.S. embassy or consulate, and the parent enters the country as a permanent resident. Consular processing for parents has three stages with three different agencies: USCIS decides your Form I-130, the National Visa Center collects documents and fees, and the consulate interviews your parent and issues the visa. Each handoff has its own forms and its own way of stalling. Here is the sequence as it actually runs.

## Stage one: the I-130 at USCIS

You file a separate Form I-130 for each parent, with proof of your citizenship, proof that you are 21 or older, and evidence of the relationship. On the form you indicate that your parent will apply for an immigrant visa abroad and name the consulate, usually the one serving the country where your parent lives. The evidence that proves the relationship, including the harder father, stepparent and adoption cases, is detailed in [I-130 for parents: documents that prove the relationship](/i-130-petition-for-parents-documents).

Parents of adult citizens are immediate relatives, so there is no priority date wait. The I-130 moves at USCIS's pace; check the USCIS processing times page for Form I-130 immediate relative petitions. On approval, USCIS sends the case to the National Visa Center. Our [green card for parents](/practice-area/key-services/green-card-for-parents) practice starts assembling the next stage's documents while the petition is pending, because the NVC stage is where most of the delay accumulates.

## Stage two of consular processing for parents: the National Visa Center

The NVC assigns a case number and an invoice number and opens the case in its online portal, the Consular Electronic Application Center. From here the work is mostly yours and your parent's:

- Pay the affidavit of support fee and the immigrant visa application fee.
- Complete Form DS-260, the online immigrant visa application, in your parent's name.
- Upload your Form I-864, your most recent federal tax return or transcript, and proof of current income. The household size for the income test includes you, your dependents, anyone you have sponsored before and each parent you are now sponsoring. A joint sponsor files a separate I-864 if your income is short.
- Upload your parent's civil documents: birth certificate, passport biographic page, marriage certificate and any divorce or death certificates for prior marriages, and police certificates from each country where your parent has lived for the required period since age 16.
- Upload certified English translations for anything not in English.

The NVC reviews what is uploaded and either marks the case documentarily complete or sends a checklist of what is missing or wrong. The reciprocity schedule published by the Department of State tells you what form each country's documents must take; a certificate in the wrong format is the most common checklist item. Once the case is complete, the NVC waits for an interview slot at the consulate and schedules it, sending the appointment letter to you and your parent.

## Stage three: the medical exam and the interview

Shortly before the interview, your parent completes a medical examination with a panel physician approved by the embassy; the consulate's website lists them. The exam covers the required vaccinations and the health-related grounds of inadmissibility, and the results go directly to the consulate.

At the interview, a consular officer places your parent under oath, confirms identity and the relationship, reviews the DS-260 answers and the affidavit of support, and asks about your parent's history: prior travel to the United States, any overstays, any criminal record, any prior visa refusals. Your parent brings the original civil documents, the passport and the appointment letter. Most immediate relative interviews are brief when the file is complete.

Three outcomes are possible. The visa is approved and the passport is returned with the immigrant visa. The case is refused under section 221(g) for a missing document, which is cured by supplying it. Or the officer finds a ground of inadmissibility, such as a prior unlawful presence bar or a misrepresentation, which requires a waiver on Form I-601 based on extreme hardship to a qualifying relative. That last outcome is why we review a parent's prior U.S. history before the I-130 is filed, not after the interview.

## Stage four: entry and the green card

The immigrant visa is valid for a limited period tied to the medical exam, usually up to six months, and your parent must enter the United States before it expires. Before traveling, your parent pays the USCIS immigrant fee online; the physical green card is not produced until it is paid. At the port of entry, the officer admits your parent as a permanent resident and endorses the visa, which serves as proof of status for a year while the card is mailed to the U.S. address you gave the NVC.

From that point your parent can work, travel and apply for a Social Security number. Residence begins on the date of entry, and naturalization eligibility follows after five years. For parents applying from Türkiye, our [Istanbul office](/locations/istanbul) prepares the consular stage directly.

## When consular processing is the better choice

Families sometimes ask whether the parent should instead visit the United States and apply here. If the parent is abroad and intends to immigrate, the answer is usually no: entering on a visitor visa with a plan to stay risks a misrepresentation finding that is difficult to waive. Adjustment of status fits a parent who is already here after a lawful entry, as described in [adjustment of status for parents](/adjustment-of-status-for-parents). The broader comparison, including the tax and travel questions families raise, is in [how to get a green card for your parents](/how-to-get-a-green-card-for-your-parents), and the other family categories are covered on our [family-based immigration](/practice-area/key-services/family-based-immigration) page.
