A DMCA takedown notice works because the platform, not the infringer, is the one who acts on it. Section 512 of the Copyright Act shields hosting providers from liability for their users' infringement if they remove material promptly when a copyright owner sends a notice that meets the statutory requirements. The platform's incentive is to comply, so a correct notice usually results in removal within days without any contact with the person who posted the content. That speed is the tool's strength. Its limits are that it covers copyright only, that the poster can send a counter-notice and force the content back up unless you sue, and that a careless notice can create liability for the sender. ## Who a DMCA takedown notice goes to

The notice goes to the service provider hosting the material: the website host, the video platform, the marketplace, the app store, or the social network. Providers that want the safe harbor must register a designated agent with the Copyright Office, and the Office maintains a searchable directory. Most large platforms also offer a web form that captures the required elements. Using the platform's form is fine; sending to a general support address is not, because a notice that does not reach the designated agent may not count.

The notice does not go to the person who posted the material, although the platform will typically forward it to them.

## The six required elements

A notice is effective under the statute only if it includes, in substance:

- A physical or electronic signature of the owner or a person authorized to act for the owner.
- Identification of the copyrighted work claimed to be infringed, or a representative list if many works on one site are involved.
- Identification of the infringing material and information reasonably sufficient to let the provider locate it, which in practice means URLs.
- Contact information for the complaining party.
- A statement that the complaining party has a good-faith belief that the use is not authorized by the owner, its agent, or the law.
- A statement that the information in the notice is accurate and, under penalty of perjury, that the complaining party is authorized to act on behalf of the owner.

A notice missing the identification of the work or the location of the material can be ignored by the provider without losing the safe harbor, and some providers will ask for a corrected notice. Precision in the URLs matters most: "all of their videos" is not a location.

## The fair use check before sending

The good-faith belief statement is not a formality. Courts have held that a sender must consider whether the use is authorized by law, including fair use, before sending a notice. A knowing, material misrepresentation that material is infringing exposes the sender to damages, including the poster's costs and attorney's fees, under Section 512(f). Commentary, criticism, parody, news reporting, and short quotations are the categories where fair use arguments are strongest and where a notice should be reviewed by counsel before it goes out. The question is not whether the use annoys you but whether a court would likely call it infringing.

Registration is not required to send a notice, but it is required to sue if the poster counter-notifies, so a work worth defending should be registered before enforcement begins, for the reasons in our post on [why registration matters when copyright is automatic](/is-copyright-automatic-why-register). Our [copyright registration](/practice-area/key-services/copyright-registration) page covers the filing.

## What the platform does

On receiving a compliant notice, the provider removes or disables access to the material expeditiously and takes reasonable steps to notify the poster. Providers must also adopt and reasonably implement a policy for terminating repeat infringers, which is why multiple notices against the same account can result in the account being closed, a consequence that often matters more to the infringer than the removal of any one item.

## The counter-notice and the restoration window

The poster may respond with a counter-notice stating, under penalty of perjury, a good-faith belief that the material was removed by mistake or misidentification. The counter-notice must include the poster's name, address, and phone number, identify the removed material and its former location, and consent to the jurisdiction of the federal court for the poster's address (or any district where the provider may be found, for posters outside the United States), along with an agreement to accept service of process from the complaining party.

Once the provider forwards a counter-notice, it must restore the material in not less than ten and not more than fourteen business days unless the complaining party notifies the provider that it has filed a lawsuit seeking a court order against the poster. That window is where the counter-notice gets its force. A copyright owner who is not prepared to file suit within roughly two weeks will see the content return. Decide before sending the notice whether you would sue, because the counter-notice forces that decision on a short clock. If the matter goes to court, the stages are described in our post on [what happens in an IP infringement lawsuit](/ip-infringement-lawsuit-process).

## What the DMCA does not cover

The notice-and-takedown process is a copyright tool. It does not apply to trademark infringement, counterfeit goods, defamation, privacy violations, or trade secret disclosure. Most platforms have separate complaint procedures for trademark and counterfeit claims, with their own requirements, and marketplaces run brand protection programs that usually require a trademark registration to enroll. Trade secret or defamatory content requires a demand to the poster, the platform's terms-of-service process, or a court order.

The process also does not reach platforms outside the United States that do not seek the U.S. safe harbor, although many foreign providers follow similar procedures voluntarily. And it does not stop a determined infringer from reposting under a new account, which is why high-volume infringement needs a monitoring service and a plan to escalate to litigation against the source rather than the symptoms.

## When a notice is the wrong first move

If the poster is a competitor you expect to litigate with, a notice tips them off and starts the counter-notice clock before you are ready. If the poster is a customer or partner, a direct request is cheaper and preserves the relationship. If the material is a borderline fair use, the 512(f) risk may outweigh the benefit. In those situations a demand letter, discussed in our post on [when to send a cease and desist letter](/when-to-send-cease-and-desist-letter), or a direct call may be the better opening.
