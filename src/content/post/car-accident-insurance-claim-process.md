A car accident insurance claim is really two or three claims running on different clocks: a property damage claim that resolves in weeks, a bodily injury claim that cannot be valued until your treatment is understood, and, when the at-fault driver is uninsured or underinsured, a claim against your own policy. Knowing which claim you are in, and who is on the other side of it, explains most of what the adjuster does. This post walks through the process in Virginia, Washington, DC, and New York from the first phone call to the settlement check.

## Which policies are in play

Start by identifying every policy that could pay. The at-fault driver's liability coverage is the obvious one, and it pays only up to the policy limit. Your own policy may include medical payments coverage, which pays early medical bills regardless of fault in Virginia and DC, and collision coverage for your vehicle. New York is a no-fault state: your own insurer pays basic economic losses such as medical bills and lost wages through personal injury protection, and you can sue the at-fault driver for pain and suffering only if your injury meets the state's serious injury threshold. All three jurisdictions require some form of uninsured motorist coverage, and underinsured coverage fills the gap when the other driver's limits are too low; our post on [uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage](/uninsured-underinsured-motorist-coverage-explained) explains how those claims work.

If the at-fault driver was working at the time, the employer's commercial policy may also apply. Our [car, truck, and motorcycle accident practice](/practice-area/key-services/car-truck-and-motorcycle-accidents) requests policy limits early because the limits define what is realistically recoverable.

## The property damage claim moves first

The vehicle claim is usually straightforward and quick. The insurer inspects the car, decides whether to repair or total it, and pays the repair cost or the actual cash value. You can run this through the other driver's liability carrier if fault is clear, or through your own collision coverage and let your insurer recover from the other side. Settle the property damage claim on its own; a property damage release should not touch the injury claim, and you should read it to confirm that it does not.

## The bodily injury claim waits for treatment

The injury claim cannot be valued until you know what the injury is. That means treating, following the plan, and reaching either full recovery or the point where your doctor can describe what treatment and limitations remain. Settling before that point means guessing, and the release ends the claim regardless of what is diagnosed later.

During this period, the adjuster may ask for a recorded statement, a medical authorization, and updates. You are not required to give the other side's insurer a recorded statement, and medical authorizations should be limited to records that relate to the crash and a reasonable period before it.

## The demand package

When treatment is complete or stable, the claim is presented in a demand package. It typically contains the police report and photographs, a narrative of the crash and the basis for fault, the complete medical records and itemized bills, documentation of lost wages, a description of ongoing limitations, and a demand figure. The quality of this package drives the first offer. Missing bills, gaps in records, and vague descriptions of pain all give the adjuster reasons to discount. Our post on [what damages you can recover in a personal injury case](/damages-in-personal-injury-case) lists the categories the demand should address.

## Negotiation and what adjusters weigh

The adjuster evaluates liability, the severity and permanence of the injury, the consistency of the medical records, your credibility, and the policy limits. In Virginia and DC, any evidence that you shared fault is weighed heavily because contributory negligence can defeat the claim entirely; in New York, comparative fault reduces the value proportionally. Expect the first offer to be low and expect several rounds. A claim with clean liability, consistent treatment, and documented losses settles near its value; a claim with gaps and disputes settles at a discount or not at all.

- The insurer's offer reflects what it expects a jury to award, discounted by the risk that you will not sue.
- Filing a lawsuit changes that calculation, and most filed cases still settle before trial.
- Policy limits are a ceiling; a serious injury against a minimum-limits policy will exhaust the policy and turn attention to underinsured coverage.

## Settlement, releases, and liens

A settlement is final once you sign the release. Before that, three things must be resolved. First, any health insurer, Medicare, Medicaid, or employer health plan that paid for treatment may have a right to reimbursement from the settlement. Second, medical providers may hold liens. Third, if you intend to make an underinsured motorist claim, your own insurer usually must be notified and given the chance to consent before you settle with the at-fault driver's carrier, or you risk forfeiting the coverage. Once these are resolved, the check is issued, liens and fees are paid, and the net amount is released to you.

## When the claim goes to litigation

If the insurer disputes liability, undervalues the injury, or runs out the clock, a lawsuit is filed before the statute of limitations expires: two years in Virginia, three in DC and New York. Litigation adds discovery, depositions, and often mediation, and it tends to produce more realistic offers because the insurer now faces trial risk.
