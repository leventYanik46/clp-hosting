Effective January 21, 2026, the State Department paused the issuance of immigrant visas to nationals of 75 countries, citing concern about applicants becoming a "public charge." Interviews can still be scheduled and held, but no immigrant visa is issued to a covered national while the pause is in effect. For families waiting on a green card through a consulate abroad, this can stop a case at the final step.

## What the 75-country visa pause covers

The pause applies to **immigrant visas**, the visas used by people becoming permanent residents through consular processing. That includes family-based immigrant visas for spouses, parents, children, and siblings, employment-based immigrant visas, and diversity visas, for nationals of the listed countries.

The State Department did not set an end date. It described the pause as lasting until it completes a review of screening and vetting procedures.

## What it does not cover

- **Nonimmigrant visas.** Visitor, student, and work visas such as H-1B, L-1, O-1, and E visas are not part of this pause, though separate travel restrictions may apply to some nationalities.
- **Visas already issued.** Valid immigrant visas were not revoked by the announcement.
- **Dual nationals.** Applicants who hold a valid passport from a country not on the list are reported to be exempt.
- **Adjustment of status inside the U.S.** The pause is a State Department action. USCIS processes adjustment of status, though other USCIS policies, including the May 2026 adjustment of status memo, affect those cases.

## What happens to cases in progress

For covered nationals, cases can move through USCIS approval of the petition, National Visa Center processing, document submission, and the interview. The final issuance is what stops. Some applicants have been refused under section 221(g) at the interview while the pause is in place.

Keep the case active. Respond to every NVC request and attend interviews. Abandoning the case or letting documents expire can create new problems once the pause lifts.

## Options for affected families

**Check dual nationality.** A second citizenship from a non-listed country may allow processing.

**Consider whether adjustment of status is possible.** If the beneficiary is lawfully in the U.S., adjustment may be available, subject to eligibility and current USCIS discretion policies. This is rarely an option for someone outside the U.S.

**Protect children who may age out.** The Child Status Protection Act can protect some children, but the rules require action within certain periods. A pause does not automatically freeze age calculations.

**Keep status valid for nonimmigrant alternatives.** A family member who qualifies for a temporary visa not covered by the pause may be able to come to the U.S. that way, if the visa's intent rules allow it.

**Watch for litigation and policy changes.** Pauses like this are often challenged in court and adjusted over time. Check the current status before making decisions.

## What not to do

- Do not withdraw an approved petition out of frustration. Rebuilding it costs time and may cost a priority date.
- Do not travel to the U.S. on a visitor visa with the intent to stay. Misrepresenting your purpose can lead to a permanent bar.
- Do not rely on informal social media lists. Confirm whether a country is covered on the State Department's official notice.

## Public charge and the bigger picture

The pause rests on public charge concerns, so strong evidence of financial support, including a well-documented Form I-864 Affidavit of Support and, when needed, a joint sponsor, will likely matter when issuance resumes.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I know if my country is on the list?
Check the State Department's official announcement and travel.state.gov for the current list. Lists circulated on social media may be outdated.

### Should I still attend my interview?
Yes. Attend and keep the case active so it is ready when issuance resumes.

### Will my fees be refunded?
Generally no. Fees already paid stay with the case.

### Can my U.S. citizen spouse sue to end the pause?
Some families have joined or filed lawsuits. Whether a suit makes sense depends on current litigation and your facts.

### Does the pause affect refugees or asylees?
The pause concerns immigrant visas issued by consulates. Refugee processing and asylum follow different procedures and policies.

### Can my relative visit on a tourist visa in the meantime?
Possibly, but a pending immigrant case makes it harder to show non-immigrant intent, and refusals are common.

### Does it affect K-1 fiancé visas?
K-1 visas are technically nonimmigrant visas, but they are processed through immigrant visa units. Check the State Department's current guidance for K visas specifically.
