Buying an existing business is one of the most common routes to an E-2 visa. An E-2 visa business for sale comes with customers, revenue, and often employees, which makes the investment easier to prove. It also comes with risks a startup doesn't have: inflated financials, hidden liabilities, and deal terms that leave your money stuck if the visa is refused.

## Why buy instead of start?

An operating business answers several E-2 questions at once. It shows a real, active enterprise. Existing payroll helps show the business is not marginal, meaning it does more than support you and your family. A track record of revenue makes the business plan more credible to a consular officer.

The downside is that the officer is now evaluating someone else's business as well as yours. Weak books, unpaid taxes, or a seller who overstated revenue all become your problem.

## Structuring the deal around the visa

The most important clause in an E-2 purchase is the one that protects your money if the visa is denied. The standard approach is an escrow arrangement:

- You sign a binding purchase agreement and fund escrow.
- The agreement says the funds release to the seller only when the E-2 visa is issued.
- If the visa is refused, the funds return to you, often minus agreed costs.

This works because the State Department accepts funds in escrow as "at risk" as long as the only condition for release is visa issuance. What doesn't work is a letter of intent, a refundable deposit with no binding agreement, or money still sitting in your personal account.

Expect to pay some funds outside escrow, such as legal fees, inventory, or a lease deposit. Those count toward your investment too.

## Due diligence: what to verify

Treat the purchase like any acquisition, with extra attention to what the consulate will see:

1. **Financial records.** Ask for at least three years of tax returns and bank statements, not just profit and loss summaries prepared by a broker. Revenue reported to the IRS is the number that matters.
2. **Payroll.** Confirm the actual employees on payroll, their hours, and whether they will stay after the sale.
3. **Lease.** Check the term remaining, renewal options, and whether the landlord must consent to the assignment.
4. **Liabilities.** Search for liens, judgments, and unpaid sales or payroll taxes. An asset purchase can limit your exposure compared with buying the seller's company shares.
5. **Valuation.** The price should be supportable by an independent valuation or by comparable sales. A purchase price far above market value raises questions about whether the investment is genuine.

## Franchises and the E-2 visa

Franchises appeal to E-2 investors because they offer a tested system and training. A franchise can be a strong E-2 vehicle, but be careful with marketing claims. There is no such thing as an "E-2 approved" franchise. Consular officers evaluate each investor and each business individually.

Before buying a franchise, read the Franchise Disclosure Document (FDD). It lists the full initial investment, ongoing royalties, litigation history, and the number of units that have closed. Item 19, if the franchisor includes it, contains financial performance representations. If the FDD shows that most units earn modest profits, plan for how your unit will hire enough staff to avoid a marginality finding.

When people search for the best franchise for an E-2 visa, the honest answer is the one whose numbers support hiring U.S. workers within a realistic timeframe and whose initial investment is substantial relative to the total cost of the business. A low-cost home-based franchise with no employees is usually a weak E-2 case.

## How much do you need to invest?

There is no fixed minimum for the E-2 visa. The investment must be substantial in proportion to the total cost of buying the business. For lower-priced businesses, officers expect you to fund a higher percentage of the price yourself rather than relying on seller financing. Seller notes secured by the business's own assets do not count toward your at-risk investment.

## Common mistakes in E-2 purchases

- Paying the full price to the seller before the visa is issued, with no protection if it's refused.
- Relying on broker-prepared financials that don't match tax returns.
- Buying a business so small that it cannot hire anyone beyond your family.
- Missing the lease assignment, then discovering the landlord won't approve the new owner.
- Using borrowed money secured by the business itself.

## Before you sign

Have the purchase agreement, escrow terms, and financial records reviewed together with your E-2 strategy. A deal that works commercially can still fail at the consulate if the structure or the numbers don't match what the officer needs to see.
