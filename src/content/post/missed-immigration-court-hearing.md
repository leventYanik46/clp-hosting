If you miss your immigration court hearing, the judge can order you removed that same day without hearing a word from you. This is called an in absentia order, and DHS only has to show that you were given written notice of the hearing and that the charges in the Notice to Appear are supported by the documents in the file. There is no second chance built into the hearing itself. The second chance comes from a motion to rescind the order, and the clock on that motion may already be running.

## How a missed immigration court hearing becomes an in absentia order

Under INA 240(b)(5), when a respondent does not appear, the immigration judge proceeds in absentia if DHS proves by clear, unequivocal and convincing evidence that written notice was provided and that the person is removable as charged. The judge does not reach any application for relief you had pending; asylum, cancellation of removal or adjustment applications are treated as abandoned.

An in absentia order is a final order of removal. ICE can act on it immediately, and it carries a separate penalty: if you received notice and had no exceptional circumstances, you are barred for ten years from voluntary departure, cancellation of removal, adjustment of status and change of status.

CLP's [immigration court defense team](/practice-area/key-services/removal-defense) sees two patterns behind most missed hearings. Either the hearing notice went to an old address, or the person believed the case had been dismissed, rescheduled or closed because of something a notario, a former lawyer or a friend told them. Both can be fixed, but they are fixed differently.

## The two routes to rescind the order

A motion to reopen to rescind an in absentia order is the remedy, and INA 240(b)(5)(C) gives you two paths.

### Within 180 days: exceptional circumstances

If you received notice but could not attend, you have 180 days from the order to file a motion showing exceptional circumstances. The statute defines these as circumstances beyond your control, and it gives examples: battery or extreme cruelty against you or your child, your own serious illness, or the death or serious illness of your spouse, child or parent. Less compelling reasons do not meet the standard. Traffic, a missed bus, a work shift or a misunderstanding about the date usually fail, though the Board looks at the totality of the circumstances, including whether you had a strong case for relief and whether you acted quickly after learning of the order.

### At any time: no notice

If you never received written notice of the hearing, there is no deadline. The same is true if you were in federal or state custody and the failure to appear was not your fault. Lack of notice is a factual question that turns on where the notice was mailed and whether you had told the court your current address. Notice mailed to the most recent address you gave the court is treated as proper notice even if you never saw it, although the presumption that regular mail was delivered can be rebutted with evidence, and that is why the address rule below matters so much.

One important limit: the Supreme Court held in 2024 that a hearing notice stating the time and place is sufficient for in absentia purposes even if the original Notice to Appear left those details blank. A defective NTA alone will not void the order if a later notice reached you.

## Filing the motion stays removal automatically

This is the single most important fact for anyone with an in absentia order. Under INA 240(b)(5)(C), filing a motion to rescind an in absentia order automatically stays your removal until the immigration judge rules on it. Most other motions to reopen do not carry an automatic stay. If ICE has detained you or scheduled your removal, getting that motion on file is the first priority, and the supporting evidence can be supplemented afterward if the deadline requires it.

The motion goes to the immigration court that entered the order, with a copy served on the DHS trial attorney. It should include a sworn declaration explaining what happened, documentary proof (medical records, death certificates, proof of your address at the time, the envelope or return receipt if you have it), and the applications for relief you would pursue if the case is reopened, so the judge can see that reopening would not be pointless.

## The address rule that causes most of these cases

Every respondent in removal proceedings must report a change of address to the immigration court on Form EOIR-33 within five days of moving. This is a separate obligation from updating your address with USCIS or ICE, and sending a change to one agency does not update the others. Courts treat notice mailed to your last reported address as proper notice. If you moved and did not file the EOIR-33, you will have a hard time arguing lack of notice, and your motion will likely need to fit the exceptional circumstances route instead.

If you are unsure whether a hearing was ever scheduled or missed, the EOIR automated case information system (by phone or online) will show your next hearing date and any order entered, using your A-number.

## What happens after the motion

If the judge grants the motion, the order is rescinded and your case is put back on the calendar, usually at a new master calendar hearing. You then proceed as if the missed hearing had not happened, including any relief application you were eligible for. The ten-year bar on relief falls away once the order is rescinded.

If the judge denies the motion, you can appeal to the Board of Immigration Appeals within 30 days, and the appeal carries its own automatic stay.
