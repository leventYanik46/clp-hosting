In a motorcycle accident claim, the rider starts behind. Insurers, and some jurors, assume motorcyclists speed, weave, and take risks, and in Virginia and DC that assumption is dangerous because any share of fault can bar recovery completely. The most common serious crash, a car turning left across a motorcycle's path, is usually the driver's fault, yet riders still have to prove it against a presumption that they were going too fast to be seen. This post explains how fault is actually decided, the helmet and lane rules in the three jurisdictions where we practice, and the evidence that counters bias.

## The left-turn crash and right of way

The majority of multi-vehicle motorcycle crashes involve a car turning left in front of an oncoming motorcycle at an intersection or driveway. The driver typically says the motorcycle "came out of nowhere." The law is on the rider's side: a driver turning left must yield to oncoming traffic, and a motorcycle traveling straight through an intersection with a green light has the right of way. The dispute is almost always about speed and visibility, because the driver's only defense is that the motorcycle was going so fast, or was so hard to see, that the turn was reasonable.

That defense is answered with physical evidence. Skid marks, the distance the motorcycle traveled after impact, the damage pattern on both vehicles, and the location of debris allow a reconstruction expert to calculate speed. Headlight status, lane position, and lighting conditions address visibility. Our [motorcycle accident team](/practice-area/key-services/car-truck-and-motorcycle-accidents) treats these cases as reconstruction cases from the start, because the rider's word alone rarely overcomes the stereotype.

## Why bias against riders changes the strategy

Adjusters price motorcycle claims lower than car claims with similar facts, on the theory that a jury will be less sympathetic. Counteracting that requires evidence of the rider's care rather than just the driver's fault: a valid motorcycle endorsement, a well-maintained bike, a helmet and protective gear, daytime running lights, and a record free of recent violations. None of these is legally required to recover, but all of them undercut the argument that the rider was reckless. In Virginia and DC, where the contributory negligence rule means a small finding of rider fault ends the case, this evidence is not decoration. Our post on [how contributory negligence works in Virginia and DC](/contributory-negligence-virginia-dc-explained) explains the stakes.

## Helmet laws and how helmet use affects the claim

Virginia, the District of Columbia, and New York each require every motorcycle operator and passenger to wear a helmet that meets applicable safety standards. Riding without one is a traffic violation in all three. In a claim, the question is whether the violation affects fault or damages.

Helmet non-use is not relevant to who caused the crash; a bare head does not make a car turn left. Where it matters is causation of a particular injury. If the rider suffered a head injury, the defense will argue that a helmet would have prevented or reduced it, and in a contributory negligence jurisdiction it may argue that the failure to wear one bars recovery for that injury. If the injuries are to the legs, pelvis, or spine, the helmet question is largely irrelevant and should be kept out of the case. Riders who were wearing a helmet should keep it unrepaired and unwashed as evidence.

- Wear a compliant helmet every ride; it is the law and it removes an entire category of defense.
- Keep the helmet, jacket, and gloves after a crash exactly as they are.
- Photograph your gear alongside your injuries to show what was and was not protected.

## Lane splitting, filtering, and lane position

Lane splitting, riding between lanes of slow or stopped traffic, is not permitted in Virginia, DC, or New York. A rider struck while splitting lanes will face a strong fault argument, and in Virginia or DC it will likely be a complete defense. Lane position within a single lane is different: motorcycles are entitled to the full width of a lane, and a driver who crowds or merges into a motorcycle's lane has violated the rider's right of way. Two motorcycles riding side by side in one lane is permitted in some jurisdictions and not others, which is one more fact the crash report should capture accurately.

## Evidence specific to motorcycle cases

Motorcycle crashes leave evidence that car crashes do not, and some of it is easily lost. The motorcycle itself should be preserved rather than repaired or sold, because the damage pattern establishes impact angle and speed. Many modern motorcycles record diagnostic data. The rider's gear shows abrasion patterns that confirm the slide direction and distance. Witness statements matter more than usual because drivers in these crashes often genuinely did not see the motorcycle and will testify to that belief sincerely. Nearby businesses with cameras should be identified in the first days. ## Damages in motorcycle claims

Because a rider has no steel frame around them, injuries are more severe at every speed: fractures, road rash requiring grafts, traumatic brain injury, and spinal injury are common. These claims frequently exceed the at-fault driver's policy limits, which makes the rider's own uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage central to recovery.
