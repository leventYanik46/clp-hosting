Once an agency misses the Freedom of Information Act's statutory deadline, you may file a FOIA lawsuit in federal court without appealing, without waiting further and without asking permission. That rule, called constructive exhaustion, is what gives immigration FOIA requests teeth. USCIS, ICE, CBP and EOIR hold the records that decide whether a naturalization application is safe, whether an old order exists, and whether a waiver is needed, and they often take far longer than the law allows to produce them. When a deadline in your own case cannot wait, the lawsuit is the tool. ## The deadlines that trigger a FOIA lawsuit

An agency must determine within 20 working days of receiving a proper request whether it will comply and notify you of its decision. In unusual circumstances, such as the need to search multiple offices or consult another agency, it may extend that period by 10 working days with written notice. An agency can also ask you to narrow the request or agree to a longer timeline. If it does neither and the period passes with no determination, you are deemed to have exhausted your administrative remedies and may sue.

There is a catch. If the agency responds, even late, before you file your complaint, constructive exhaustion ends and you must pursue the administrative appeal before suing. Timing therefore matters: once you decide to litigate, file promptly rather than waiting for a response that would reset the process. A response that merely acknowledges receipt and assigns a control number is not a determination; a letter releasing some records and withholding others is.

If the agency responded and you are challenging redactions or a "no records" finding, the path is the administrative appeal within the stated deadline, generally 90 days, and then a lawsuit if the appeal fails or goes unanswered within 20 working days.

## What a FOIA lawsuit can get you

The complaint is filed in U.S. district court in the district where you live, where the records are kept, or in the District of Columbia. The agency must answer within 30 days, shorter than the usual period for suits against the government. The court reviews the matter fresh, and the agency bears the burden of justifying any withholding.

In practice, most FOIA suits over immigration records do not end in a trial over exemptions. They end with the agency processing the request on a schedule, often negotiated between counsel and entered as a court order, with rolling productions and status reports. Where the agency withholds material, it must produce an index describing each withheld document and the exemption claimed, and the court can review the documents privately and order release of anything improperly withheld.

An agency can ask the court for more time by showing exceptional circumstances and due diligence in reducing its backlog, and courts sometimes grant such stays. But a predictable backlog alone is not an exceptional circumstance under the statute unless the agency shows reasonable progress in reducing it, and a requester who shows a concrete need, such as a scheduled hearing or an expiring deadline, is well placed to resist.

## Attorney fees

The Freedom of Information Act has its own fee-shifting provision, and it is more generous to plaintiffs than the Equal Access to Justice Act. A requester who substantially prevails may recover fees and costs, and since a 2007 amendment, substantially prevailing includes a voluntary or unilateral change in the agency's position caused by the lawsuit. If the agency produces the file only because you sued, fees are on the table. The court then weighs the public benefit of the disclosure, your personal interest in it and whether the agency's withholding had a reasonable basis. An individual seeking their own immigration file usually does well on that last point where the agency simply failed to respond. [Whether you can recover legal fees after beating USCIS](/eaja-fees-immigration-cases) contrasts this with the rule in delay suits.

## FOIA lawsuit versus mandamus

The two are often confused because both compel a federal agency to act. A FOIA suit compels release of records. A mandamus suit, described in [immigration mandamus lawsuits](/immigration-mandamus-lawsuits) and on our [writ of mandamus service](/practice-area/key-services/immigration-mandamus) page, compels a decision on a delayed application. They work in sequence more often than in parallel: the FOIA records show whether the underlying application is sound, and that determines whether forcing a decision is wise.

## Before you sue

- Confirm the request was proper: correct agency, sufficient identification, signed verification of identity, and consent for any third-party records.
- Calculate the deadline in working days from the agency's receipt, adding any extension the agency invoked in writing.
- Document your follow-up inquiries and the agency's non-responses.
- Consider a written request for expedited processing if a compelling need exists, which creates a separate reviewable decision.
- Decide the venue with counsel; [which federal court hears your immigration case](/which-federal-court-hears-immigration-case) explains the options.

A FOIA suit is a tool for a records request that is both overdue and urgent. Where nothing in your case depends on the records arriving soon, a patient follow-up usually costs less. Where a hearing, an interview or a filing deadline does depend on them, the statute gives you a court, and the court gives you a schedule.
