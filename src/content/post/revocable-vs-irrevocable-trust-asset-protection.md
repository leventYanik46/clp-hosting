A revocable trust protects your assets from probate. It does not protect them from anyone else. Because you can amend it, revoke it, and take the property back at any time, the law treats everything in it as still yours: your creditors can reach it, a judgment against you attaches to it, and Medicaid counts it. An irrevocable trust can put assets beyond those claims, but only because you have genuinely given up control. The choice between a revocable vs irrevocable trust is really a choice about how much control you are willing to surrender for how much protection.

## Why a revocable trust gives no creditor protection

Every jurisdiction, including Virginia, DC, and New York, follows the same principle: a person cannot shield assets from creditors by placing them in a trust they can undo. The settlor's power to revoke means the settlor's creditors can compel revocation or reach the assets directly, during life and after death. A revocable trust is still worth having for the reasons our post on [living trusts compared with wills](/revocable-living-trust-vs-will) describes, such as avoiding probate and managing incapacity. Just do not expect it to stand between you and a lawsuit, a business failure, or a nursing home bill. Clients who come to our [estate planning and asset protection](/practice-area/key-services/estate-planning-asset-protection) practice with a revocable trust and the belief that their house is protected are usually surprised to learn that it is not.

## What an irrevocable trust can do

When you transfer assets to an irrevocable trust, name someone else as trustee, and keep no power to take the property back, the assets are no longer yours. A creditor who sues you later generally cannot reach them, because you do not own them. Properly structured, the transfer also removes the assets from your taxable estate, which matters for families near the federal exemption and for New York and DC residents facing those jurisdictions' lower state estate tax thresholds.

Common uses include a trust for children or grandchildren that holds their inheritance under a spendthrift clause, so their creditors and divorcing spouses cannot reach it; an irrevocable life insurance trust that keeps policy proceeds out of the estate; and a Medicaid asset protection trust that holds a home or savings so they are not counted toward long-term care eligibility once the look-back period has passed.

## The price of protection

### You lose control

The trustee, not you, manages the assets and makes distributions according to the terms you wrote at the start. You can retain limited rights in some designs, such as the right to live in a house the trust owns or to receive income, but every retained right is a potential opening for a creditor or the IRS to argue the assets are still yours. The more you keep, the less the trust protects.

### Timing is everything

Protection works only for claims that arise after the transfer. Moving assets into a trust once a lawsuit is filed, a loan is in default, or a creditor is circling is a voidable transfer under the fraudulent transfer laws in all three of the firm's jurisdictions. A court can unwind it, and the attempt itself damages your credibility. For Medicaid, transfers made within the sixty-month look-back period before an application trigger a penalty period of ineligibility. Asset protection has to be done years in advance, while the weather is clear.

### Tax and basis consequences

Funding an irrevocable trust is usually a completed gift that requires a gift tax return if it exceeds the annual exclusion. Assets given away during life generally keep your original cost basis rather than receiving a step-up at death, so a highly appreciated asset placed in the wrong kind of irrevocable trust can leave your heirs with a capital gains bill they would have avoided by inheriting it. Some trust designs preserve the step-up by keeping the assets in the estate for income tax purposes while removing them for other purposes. This is where the drafting detail matters most, and it is addressed alongside the gift planning described in our post on [lifetime gifting and the taxable estate](/lifetime-gifting-reduce-taxable-estate).

## Trusts you create for yourself

Most states, including New York and the District of Columbia, will not protect assets in a trust you set up for your own benefit, no matter how irrevocable it is. Virginia is among the minority of states that permit a form of self-settled asset protection trust under specific statutory conditions, including an independent trustee and a period during which existing creditors can still challenge the transfer. Whether that structure fits, and how it holds up when the creditor is in another state, is a question for a consultation rather than a form.

## Modifying an irrevocable trust later

Irrevocable does not always mean frozen. Many trusts can be adjusted through a trust protector, through decanting into a new trust where state law permits, or by court petition or unanimous consent of the beneficiaries. These tools help when circumstances change, but relying on them undercuts the protection, since a trust that is easy to modify looks less like a completed transfer. Build the flexibility in deliberately, and do not count on it for things that should have been decided at the start.

## Revocable vs irrevocable trust: which one is right for you

A revocable trust for probate avoidance and an irrevocable trust for protection are not competing options; many clients have both. The question is whether you face a real risk that justifies giving up control of a portion of your assets: a profession with liability exposure, a business with personal guarantees, a family history of long-term care, or an estate large enough to face estate tax. If so, the irrevocable trust should hold only what you can afford to part with, drafted for your specific risk.
