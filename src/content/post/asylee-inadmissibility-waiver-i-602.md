Asylees apply for a green card under a different section of the law from most applicants, INA 209 rather than INA 245, and that changes which inadmissibility grounds apply and how they are waived. Three grounds that stop other applicants do not apply to asylees at all. Most of the rest can be waived on Form I-602 under INA 209(c) for humanitarian purposes, to assure family unity, or when it is in the public interest. A short list cannot be waived by anyone. Our [green card through asylum](/practice-area/key-services/green-card-through-asylum) team screens every I-485 for these grounds before filing, because an asylee inadmissibility waiver filed with the application is far easier to win than one filed in response to a notice of intent to deny. Here is how the three categories break down and what USCIS weighs.

## Grounds that do not apply to asylees

INA 209(c) states that three provisions of INA 212(a) are not applicable to an asylee seeking adjustment: the public charge ground in 212(a)(4), the labor certification ground in 212(a)(5), and the ground in 212(a)(7)(A) for arriving without a valid immigrant visa or entry document. In practice this means no affidavit of support, no income test, no sponsor, and no penalty for the manner in which you first arrived. Entering without inspection or overstaying a visa does not by itself block asylee adjustment, because the entry document ground is off the table and any related ground falls within the waiver's reach. The unlawful presence bars in 212(a)(9)(B) are triggered only by a departure from the United States after the unlawful presence accrued, which is one more reason asylees should not travel without advice.

## Grounds that can be waived on Form I-602

Everything in 212(a) that is not on the exempt list or the unwaivable list below can be waived under 209(c). The list is broader than the waivers available to other applicants, and that is deliberate: Congress wrote the asylee and refugee waiver generously because the alternative is returning someone to persecution. The grounds that come up most often are:

- Health-related grounds under 212(a)(1), including a communicable disease of public health significance, a physical or mental disorder with associated harmful behavior, and missing vaccinations that are medically contraindicated or objected to on religious or moral grounds.
- Criminal grounds under 212(a)(2)(A), (B), and (D): a crime involving moral turpitude, a controlled substance conviction, multiple convictions, and prostitution-related grounds. Unlike the waiver available to other applicants, 209(c) can reach a controlled substance conviction that is more than simple marijuana possession.
- Misrepresentation under 212(a)(6)(C)(i) and, in USCIS's reading, a false claim to U.S. citizenship under 212(a)(6)(C)(ii), which is unwaivable for almost every other category.
- Alien smuggling under 212(a)(6)(E), including bringing a family member across the border.
- Prior removal orders under 212(a)(9)(A) and unlawful presence under 212(a)(9)(B).

Note what the waiver does not fix. It cures inadmissibility; it does not restore asylum. A conviction serious enough to count as a particularly serious crime is a ground to terminate asylum, and a terminated asylee has nothing to adjust from. The criminal analysis therefore has two stages: first whether the conviction threatens the asylum grant, then whether it needs a waiver.

## Grounds that cannot be waived

INA 209(c) withholds the waiver for 212(a)(2)(C), which covers anyone the government knows or has reason to believe is or has been a controlled substance trafficker, and for four paragraphs of 212(a)(3): (A) espionage and sabotage, (B) terrorism-related grounds, (C) adverse foreign policy consequences, and (E) participation in Nazi persecution, genocide, torture, or extrajudicial killing. The reason-to-believe standard in the trafficking ground requires no conviction, so an arrest with a drug quantity that suggests sale can trigger it even if the charge was dismissed.

The terrorism-related ground deserves a separate note because it is drafted broadly enough to reach people who paid a ransom, were forced to provide food, or belonged to an armed opposition group. The 209(c) waiver cannot reach it, but a separate, discretionary exemption administered by DHS can apply in defined situations, including certain cases of support given under duress. That exemption is requested differently and decided on a different timeline, so a client with any contact with an armed group should raise it before the I-485 is filed.

## What USCIS weighs on an I-602

The waiver is discretionary, and the applicant must show that granting it serves one of three purposes: humanitarian reasons, family unity, or the public interest. For an asylee the humanitarian prong carries real weight, because denial means remaining in a status that can be terminated, with no path to permanence, rather than returning to the country of persecution. Family unity rests on U.S. citizen or permanent resident relatives, especially children. The public interest prong covers employment, community service, and the absence of any danger.

Against those, the adjudicator weighs the seriousness and recency of the ground, the number of incidents, rehabilitation, and candor. For crimes that are violent or dangerous, the standard rises sharply: under Matter of Jean, a decision of the Attorney General, a 209(c) waiver for such a crime requires extraordinary circumstances, such as a national security or foreign policy consideration or an exceptional and extremely unusual hardship, and is rarely granted. The evidence that persuades is concrete: certified dispositions, proof that every sentence and condition was completed, treatment records, employer and community letters, tax returns, and a personal statement that acknowledges the conduct rather than minimizing it. File the I-602 with the I-485 when the ground is known, pay the current fee or request a waiver of it, and expect USCIS to invite a waiver by request for evidence when it finds a ground you did not address.

## Look before you file

The criminal grounds are where self-filed asylee applications go wrong, because an arrest that seemed minor at the time can be a controlled substance ground, a moral turpitude ground, or evidence of a particularly serious crime depending on the statute of conviction. The way these categories interact with immigration law is explained in our post on [crimmigration](/what-is-crimmigration). Before filing, obtain certified records for every arrest, analyze each one under the current inadmissibility and termination rules, and consider whether post-conviction relief is realistic.
