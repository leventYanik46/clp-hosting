The choice between an LLC or a corporation usually turns on one question: will you raise money from institutional investors or give equity to employees in the next few years? If yes, a C corporation is almost always the right vehicle. If no, an LLC is cheaper to run and more flexible on taxes. Both protect your personal assets from business debts, so liability protection is rarely the deciding factor. What differs is how the entity is taxed, how it is managed, and how comfortable future investors will be with it.

## How an LLC and a corporation differ in practice

An LLC is governed by an operating agreement that the members write themselves. You can allocate profits unevenly, let members manage directly, and skip most of the formalities that corporations observe. By default the IRS treats a single-member LLC as a disregarded entity and a multi-member LLC as a partnership, so profits pass through to the owners' personal returns without a second layer of tax.

A corporation is governed by its state's corporation statute, bylaws, and a board of directors. Shareholders elect directors, directors appoint officers, and major decisions are recorded in resolutions. A C corporation pays its own income tax, and shareholders pay tax again on dividends. The tradeoff is a structure that investors, lenders, and acquirers understand without explanation.

Either entity can change its tax treatment by election. An LLC can elect to be taxed as an S corporation or a C corporation. A corporation can elect S corporation status if it meets the eligibility rules. Our [business formation and structuring](/practice-area/key-services/business-formation-structuring) practice walks founders through these elections, because a wrong default tax status is one of the more expensive mistakes to unwind.

## When a corporation is the better choice for a startup

Venture funds and most angel groups invest through preferred stock, and the standard financing documents assume a Delaware C corporation. Convertible notes and SAFEs convert into shares, which an LLC does not have. Employee stock option plans, incentive stock options, and the 409A valuations that support them are built around corporate stock. If any of these are in your two-year plan, forming an LLC now means paying to convert later, often under time pressure in the middle of a financing.

Corporate stock can also qualify for the Section 1202 qualified small business stock exclusion, which can exclude a large portion of the gain on a sale if the stock is held long enough and the company meets the asset tests. Those rules were revised in 2025, so confirm the current holding periods and caps with your tax advisor before you count on them. LLC interests do not qualify.

A corporation also handles turnover better. Shareholders come and go without rewriting the governing document, and the board structure gives investors a familiar seat at the table. A corporation's governance obligations can be managed with a short set of policies; CLP's [corporate governance and internal policies](/practice-area/key-services/corporate-governance-and-internal-policies) team handles that as the company grows.

## When an LLC fits better

Many profitable companies never raise outside equity. Consulting firms, agencies, restaurants, real estate holding companies, and professional practices typically distribute profits to owners every year. For them, the C corporation's double taxation is a real cost with no offsetting benefit. An LLC passes income through once, and the owners can agree to split profits in a way that reflects who contributed capital and who contributes labor.

An LLC is also lighter to administer. No annual shareholder meeting, no board minutes, fewer state filings in most jurisdictions. For a founder who wants to run the business and not a governance calendar, that matters. The document that does all the work is the operating agreement; we cover what it should contain in our post on [what a multi-member LLC operating agreement should include](/llc-operating-agreement-what-to-include).

## What foreign founders should consider

Many of our clients form U.S. companies from abroad or hold a visa while they build. Two rules shape their choice. First, an S corporation cannot have a nonresident alien as a shareholder, so the S election is off the table for most foreign-owned businesses. Second, an LLC with foreign members creates partnership withholding and reporting obligations that are easy to miss, and some countries tax an LLC's pass-through income in ways that defeat the purpose.

For founders who will apply for an investor visa, the entity must be real, operating, and documented. A corporation with clear share ownership often makes the ownership and control showing easier. Our [E-2 treaty investor visa](/practice-area/key-services/e-2-visa) page explains what that evidence looks like, and our post on [how much to invest for an E-2 visa](/e-2-visa-minimum-investment-2026) addresses the capital question.

## Can you change your mind later?

Yes, and the direction matters. Converting an LLC into a corporation is usually tax-free and can be done by statutory conversion in Delaware, Virginia, and many other states, or by merging the LLC into a new corporation. Investors require this routinely before a priced round. Converting a corporation into an LLC is treated as a liquidation of the corporation, which can trigger tax on appreciated assets, so it is rarely worth doing once the business has value.

Because the LLC-to-corporation path is open, some founders start as an LLC and convert when a financing becomes real. That works if the cap table is simple. It works badly if you have already promised equity to employees or advisors, because LLC profits interests do not map cleanly onto stock options. If equity grants are coming soon, form the corporation from the start.
