A trademark specimen refusal means the examining attorney looked at the image you submitted and did not see your mark being used in commerce on the goods or services listed in the application. The mark itself may be fine. The problem is proof, and it is fixable in most cases by substituting a specimen that shows real use and verifying that the use existed on the relevant date. Specimen problems are among the most common issues our [trademark office action responses](/practice-area/key-services/trademark-office-action-responses) page addresses for applicants who filed on their own, because the rules about what counts as use are stricter than they look.

## What a specimen is supposed to show

For goods, the specimen must show the mark on the goods themselves, on their packaging or labels, on tags, or on a display associated with the goods at the point of sale. A web page qualifies if it shows the mark near a picture or description of the goods and gives the buyer a way to order them, such as a price and an add-to-cart button. For services, the specimen must show the mark used in advertising or rendering the services, with a reference to the services themselves: a website, a brochure, a signed storefront, a screenshot of the service in operation.

Three rules apply to both. The mark on the specimen must match the mark in the drawing. The specimen must show the mark with the goods or services actually listed in the application, not something else the company sells. And the use must be real: the specimen must be something a customer actually saw, not an image prepared for the filing.

## The most common reasons for a trademark specimen refusal

- The specimen is a mock-up, a rendering, a printer's proof or a digitally altered image. Examiners look for signs of editing and ask for proof the item exists.
- The specimen is advertising for goods. An advertisement is acceptable for services but not for goods, where the mark must appear on the product, its packaging or a point-of-sale display.
- A web page specimen for goods shows the mark but no way to purchase, or omits the URL and the date the page was accessed or printed, both of which are required.
- The mark on the specimen differs from the drawing, for example a stylized version where a standard-character mark was filed with different wording, or a logo missing an element.
- The mark appears only as a trade name or company name in a footer, not as a brand for the listed goods or services.
- The use is ornamental: a slogan across the front of a shirt reads as decoration rather than as a source identifier.
- The specimen is an invoice, a business card, a press release or an internal document, none of which shows use in commerce for goods.

The office action states which of these the examiner found. Read it carefully, because the fix depends on the reason.

## How to fix it with a substitute specimen

A substitute specimen must have been in use in commerce on or before the relevant date: the filing date for a use-based application, or the date the Statement of Use or Amendment to Allege Use was filed for an intent-to-use application. You submit the new specimen with a verified statement that it was in use at least as early as that date. The verification is signed under penalty of perjury, so the date must be right.

That timing rule is why evidence gathering matters. Pull dated product photographs, archived captures of the website, shipping records that tie the packaging to the date, and screenshots of marketplace listings with their timestamps. A specimen that exists today but did not exist on the filing date cannot be used, no matter how good it looks. The deadline for the response is the same as for any office action, as explained in our post on [the trademark office action response deadline](/trademark-office-action-response-deadline), and gathering historical evidence is one reason to start early.

## When you cannot produce an acceptable specimen

If the mark simply was not in use on the relevant date, a substitute specimen is not available, and the honest options are limited. Before publication, a use-based application can be amended to an intent-to-use basis under Section 1(b), which removes the specimen requirement for now and lets you file the Statement of Use once real use begins; our post on [how intent-to-use trademark applications work](/intent-to-use-trademark-application-explained) covers that path. If the problem is confined to some of the listed goods, delete those goods and keep the rest. If the refusal is ornamental, the answers include a specimen showing the mark on a hang tag or label, proof that the mark already functions as a trademark for other goods, or amendment to the Supplemental Register. When none of these fits, a new application filed once use begins is cleaner than a response that stretches the facts, which can invalidate the registration later.

## Avoiding the refusal in the next filing

Capture the specimen from the live product or page, not from a design file. For web pages, include the full URL and the access date in the capture. Show the mark exactly as drawn, next to the goods, with a price or ordering option. Use one specimen per class and make sure it shows at least one of the goods in that class. Keep the originals, dated, in case a later examiner or an opponent asks for them.
