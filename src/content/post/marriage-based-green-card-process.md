A marriage-based green card is built on one question that USCIS or a consular officer will keep asking: is this a real marriage? Everything in the process, from the forms to the interview, is designed to answer it. Couples who prepare with that in mind tend to have smoother cases.

## Two routes: inside the United States or abroad

The route depends on where the foreign spouse is living and how they entered.

If the foreign spouse is in the United States and was inspected and admitted or paroled, the couple can often file the petition and the green card application together. The U.S. citizen files Form I-130, and the foreign spouse files Form I-485 at the same time. This is called concurrent filing. While the case is pending, the spouse can also request a work permit (Form I-765) and travel permission (Form I-131).

If the foreign spouse lives abroad, the case goes through consular processing. USCIS approves the I-130, the case moves to the National Visa Center, and the spouse completes Form DS-260 and attends an interview at a U.S. consulate.

A spouse of a U.S. citizen is an immediate relative, so there is no visa number backlog. A spouse of a permanent resident is in a preference category and may have to wait for a visa number.

## What you file

- Form I-130 with proof that the petitioner is a U.S. citizen
- Proof of a legal marriage, such as the marriage certificate, and proof that any prior marriage of either spouse ended
- Form I-485 or Form DS-260, depending on the route
- Form I-864, the affidavit of support, with income evidence
- Form I-693, the medical exam
- Passport photographs, identification, and civil documents

## Proving the marriage is genuine

USCIS looks for the kind of overlap that real couples accumulate without trying. Strong evidence includes:

- A joint lease, mortgage, or other proof you live together
- Joint bank accounts, credit cards, insurance, or tax returns
- Photos from different times and places, with family and friends
- Messages, call logs, and travel records
- Birth certificates of children you share
- Letters from people who know you as a couple

More paper is not always better. A clean set of documents that tells a consistent story beats a stack of random items. If your relationship is newer or you live apart for work, explain why and show how you stay connected.

## The interview

Many marriage-based cases end with an interview, and some are decided without one. If you are called in, both spouses usually attend. The officer may ask about how you met, daily routines, your home, family members, and recent events. Answer from real knowledge and do not rehearse a script. Small differences, such as which side of the bed each person sleeps on, are normal. Large, unexplained contradictions are what raise concerns.

Bring originals of your documents, updated evidence from the time since filing, and an interpreter if you need one. If the officer believes more proof is needed, you may receive a request for evidence rather than an immediate decision.

## How long it takes

Timelines vary by USCIS field office, consulate, and the details of the case. Plan in terms of months and, in some places, more than a year, and use the USCIS processing times page and the consulate's wait time information to get current estimates. Any gap, such as a missing document or a request for evidence, adds time.

## Conditional residence and removing the conditions

If you have been married for less than two years on the date the green card is approved, you receive a conditional green card valid for two years. To keep your status, you and your spouse file Form I-751 during the 90 days before the card expires. You submit new evidence that the marriage was real and continued. If the marriage ended or there was abuse, a waiver may let the foreign spouse file alone.

## Issues that deserve attention before you file

- Past overstays, unauthorized work, or unlawful entries
- Time spent in the United States unlawfully, which can trigger bars on returning after leaving
- Criminal arrests or charges, even old ones
- Prior marriages or previous immigration filings
- A sponsor who does not meet the income requirement

Many of these have solutions, but some are only available if you act before leaving the country or before you file. A review by an attorney early in the process is far easier than repairing a problem after a denial or a notice to appear.
