An I-130 petition for parents is approved on paper, not at an interview. USCIS looks at two things: that you are a U.S. citizen at least 21 years old, and that the person you are petitioning for is your parent under the immigration definition. For a mother, one document usually settles it. For a father, a stepparent or an adoptive parent, the proof gets longer, and the petitions that stall are almost always the ones where the birth certificate did not say what the family assumed it said. Here is what to gather for each situation.

## Proving your own eligibility for the I-130 petition for parents

You file a separate Form I-130 for each parent, each with its own fee and its own evidence, because immediate relative petitions carry no derivatives. For each petition, include proof of your citizenship: a U.S. birth certificate, a naturalization certificate, a certificate of citizenship or a valid U.S. passport. Include proof that you are at least 21, which your birth certificate supplies. If your name differs from the name on your birth certificate, add the marriage certificate, divorce decree or court order that explains the change. Only a citizen can file; a permanent resident cannot, as our [green card for parents](/practice-area/key-services/green-card-for-parents) page explains, and the fix is to naturalize first.

## Petitioning for your mother

Your birth certificate listing your mother's name is the core document. Add your mother's own birth certificate or passport biographic page for her identity. If your birth certificate was issued years after the birth, or was never issued, USCIS will want secondary evidence: baptismal or religious records made near the time of birth, school records, census records, or affidavits from people with personal knowledge of the birth, together with a letter from the civil registry explaining why no timely certificate exists.

## Petitioning for your father

A birth certificate naming your father is the start, and whether more is needed depends on whether your parents were married when you were born.

If they were married, add their marriage certificate, and the divorce decree or death certificate of any prior marriage that could affect its validity.

If they were not married, the law gives two routes. The first is legitimation: show that you were legitimated before you turned 18 under the law of your father's or your own residence or domicile, which in many countries happens automatically when the parents later marry or when the father acknowledges the child. The second is a bona fide parent-child relationship: evidence that your father had an actual relationship with you before you turned 21, such as financial support records, correspondence, photographs over the years, school or medical records naming him, and affidavits from relatives who observed it. The second route exists for families where the father was present but no legal formality ever happened, and it is proven with ordinary documents rather than legal ones.

A father not named on the birth certificate at all is the hardest version. A later-amended certificate, a court acknowledgment of paternity, and evidence of the relationship together can carry it; in some cases USCIS will suggest DNA testing through an accredited laboratory, which is voluntary but often decisive.

## Stepparents and adoptive parents

A stepparent qualifies if the marriage between your stepparent and your biological parent took place before you turned 18. File the marriage certificate, your birth certificate showing the biological parent, and proof that any earlier marriage of either spouse had ended. The step relationship can survive the biological parent's death or a divorce if the relationship continued in fact, and in that case add evidence of ongoing contact and support.

An adoptive parent qualifies if the adoption was finalized before you turned 16, and the parent had legal custody of you for two years and lived with you for two years. File the final adoption decree, the custody order if separate from the decree, and proof of the two years of residence, such as school records or leases showing the shared address. The custody and residence periods can be before or after the adoption, but both must be met.

## Translations, formats and the civil documents that follow

Every document in a language other than English needs a complete English translation with the translator's certification of competence and accuracy. Photocopies are acceptable for the I-130; keep originals for the interview. Check the Department of State's reciprocity schedule for your parent's country, which tells you what form of birth certificate and marriage certificate that country issues and whether a given document is considered available at all. Submitting the wrong format is a common cause of Requests for Evidence. The general structure of the petition is covered in [what Form I-130 is](/what-is-form-i-130-2025-update).

The I-130 is only the relationship stage. If your parent is in the United States after a lawful entry, the green card application can be filed at the same time, as described in [adjustment of status for parents](/adjustment-of-status-for-parents). If your parent is abroad, the same civil documents go to the National Visa Center later, along with your Form I-864.
