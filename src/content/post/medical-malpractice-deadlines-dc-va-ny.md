The medical malpractice statute of limitations is two years in Virginia, three years in Washington, DC, and two years and six months in New York. Those are the headline numbers, and they are wrong for a large share of real cases, because each jurisdiction starts the clock at a different moment and each has exceptions for patients who could not have known they were injured. A Virginia patient whose surgeon left a sponge inside has a different deadline from one whose cancer was misread on a scan, and both differ from a child's claim. CLP's [medical negligence practice](/practice-area/key-services/medical-negligence) calculates the deadline on the first call, because a review that takes months is pointless if the window closes during it.

## The basic medical malpractice statute of limitations in each jurisdiction

Virginia's two years run from the date of the injury, which in most cases means the date of the negligent act, not the date you discovered it. Virginia does not have a general discovery rule for medical malpractice, which makes it the harshest of the three.

DC's three years run from when the claim accrues, and DC applies a discovery rule: the clock starts when you knew, or with reasonable diligence should have known, of the injury, its cause, and some evidence of wrongdoing. A patient who had no reason to suspect negligence until a later provider explained it may get the benefit of that later date.

New York's two years and six months run from the act or omission, or from the end of a continuous course of treatment for the same condition. New York has no general discovery rule for malpractice either, but it has carved out specific exceptions described below.

## Exceptions that move the start date

Continuous treatment applies in Virginia and New York. If the same provider kept treating you for the same condition, the clock runs from the last treatment rather than from the original error, on the theory that a patient should not have to sue a doctor who is still trying to fix the problem.

Foreign objects left in the body get their own rule in both states: one year from the date the object was discovered or reasonably should have been. Virginia adds one year from discovery for cases where the provider fraudulently concealed the injury, and one year from the date the diagnosis is communicated for a negligent failure to diagnose a malignant tumor, with every Virginia extension capped at ten years from the original act.

New York's cancer exception, known as Lavern's Law, allows a claim for failure to diagnose cancer or a malignant tumor to be filed within two years and six months from when the patient discovered or should have discovered the error, capped at seven years from the act. The diagnosis cases that depend on these rules are discussed in [is a misdiagnosis or delayed diagnosis medical malpractice](/misdiagnosis-delayed-diagnosis-malpractice).

## Minors, incapacity, and death

Each jurisdiction extends the deadline for children, but differently. DC tolls the statute until the child turns 18. New York tolls it during minority but caps the extension at ten years from the act, so a birth injury claim cannot wait until the child is an adult. Virginia gives minors a limited extension that depends on the child's age at injury, and a parent's claim for the child's medical expenses has its own, shorter period. Mental incapacity tolls the clock in each jurisdiction while it lasts.

When the patient dies, the claim becomes a wrongful death action with a two-year deadline from the date of death in all three jurisdictions, and the two clocks interact: a malpractice claim that had already expired before death is generally not revived. The family's rights are covered on our [wrongful death](/practice-area/key-services/wrongful-death) page.

## Government and federal providers have earlier deadlines

Where the provider was public, a notice requirement sits in front of the statute of limitations. A claim against a New York public hospital requires a notice of claim within 90 days and suit within one year and 90 days. A claim against a District of Columbia agency requires written notice to the Mayor within six months. Veterans Affairs hospitals, military treatment facilities, and federally funded community health centers are sued under the Federal Tort Claims Act, which requires an administrative claim on Standard Form 95 within two years of accrual and allows suit only after the agency denies it or six months pass; once denied, you have six months to file. Active-duty service members face additional restrictions that require their own analysis.

## Pre-suit steps that consume the clock

Each jurisdiction requires work before a malpractice complaint can be filed, and that work takes time. Records take up to 30 days per provider under HIPAA, and expert review follows. Virginia requires a written expert certification in hand before the complaint is served. New York requires a certificate of merit with the complaint. DC requires 90 days' written notice to each defendant before filing, and if that notice is served within the last 90 days of the limitations period, the period is extended by 90 days from service, a provision that has rescued more than one late case. Those steps are explained in [how do you prove medical malpractice in court](/how-to-prove-medical-malpractice), and what follows the filing in [what happens in a medical malpractice case, step by step](/medical-malpractice-case-step-by-step). The practical rule is to start the review as soon as you suspect an error, not when the deadline approaches.
