Virginia and the District of Columbia are among the last few jurisdictions in the country that still follow pure contributory negligence. The rule is blunt: if your own carelessness contributed to your injury in any degree, even one percent, you recover nothing from the person who was mostly at fault. New York, by contrast, uses pure comparative negligence, which reduces your award by your share of fault but never zeroes it out. If you were hurt in the DC area, this rule shapes every decision from what you say at the scene to whether the case should go to a jury.

## What contributory negligence actually means

Under contributory negligence, a defendant who can prove you failed to use reasonable care for your own safety, and that your failure was a proximate cause of the injury, has a complete defense. The defendant carries the burden of proving your negligence, just as you carry the burden of proving theirs. But the consequence is asymmetric. You must prove every element of the defendant's negligence to win anything; the defendant only has to prove that you contributed to lose nothing.

In practice this means insurers in Virginia and DC investigate the injured person as thoroughly as the insured. Were you speeding slightly? Looking at your phone? Wearing dark clothing at night? Did you step onto a surface you could see was wet? Each of these becomes an argument that you share fault, and under this rule, shared fault is total defeat. ## The exceptions that keep claims alive

Contributory negligence is harsh, so courts and legislatures have carved out limits.

- Last clear chance: if the defendant had the last clear opportunity to avoid the accident and failed to take it, your earlier negligence may not bar recovery. The doctrine is narrow and fact-specific, but it is real.
- Willful or wanton conduct: ordinary contributory negligence is generally not a defense when the defendant's conduct went beyond carelessness into recklessness, such as drunk driving.
- DC's rule for pedestrians and cyclists: since the Motor Vehicle Collision Recovery Act of 2016, a pedestrian, cyclist, or other non-motorized road user injured by a motor vehicle in the District can recover unless their own negligence was greater than the driver's. That is a modified comparative rule, and it exists only for that category of claim. Our [pedestrian and bicycle accident](/practice-area/key-services/pedestrian-and-bicycle-accidents) page covers how it is applied.
- Children and incapacity: a young child is held to the standard of a child of similar age and experience, not an adult, and very young children may be incapable of contributory negligence as a matter of law.
- Safety statutes: where a statute was enacted specifically to protect a class of people from their own inability to protect themselves, the defense may not apply.

Assumption of risk is a separate but related defense: if you knowingly and voluntarily accepted a specific danger, the defendant may avoid liability on that ground too.

## How New York's comparative negligence differs

New York applies pure comparative negligence. A jury assigns a percentage of fault to each party, and your award is reduced by your share. If your damages are valued at a given amount and you are found 30 percent at fault, you receive 70 percent. Even a plaintiff found 90 percent at fault recovers 10 percent. The defense still argues your fault, but the argument changes the size of the recovery rather than its existence.

This difference affects settlement value directly. The same crash with the same injuries will generally settle for less in Virginia than in New York when there is any plausible argument about the plaintiff's conduct, because the Virginia insurer is pricing in the chance of a complete defense verdict.

## What the rule means for your evidence

Because the defense needs only a foothold, the plaintiff's own conduct has to be documented as carefully as the defendant's. Several steps help.

Say as little as possible about fault at the scene, and never apologize, because apologies are quoted back as admissions. Decline recorded statements to the other driver's insurer until you have advice. Preserve evidence that shows you were acting carefully: dashcam footage, phone records showing no activity, receipts placing you where you say you were, and witness names. In a crash case, the question of who had the right of way, and whether both drivers were obeying traffic rules, decides most contributory negligence disputes; our post on [how to prove negligence in a personal injury claim](/how-to-prove-negligence-injury-claim) explains how that proof is assembled. Motorcyclists face these arguments more than most, which is why we wrote separately about [how fault is decided in a motorcycle accident claim](/motorcycle-accident-claim-fault-helmet).

## Why cases in Virginia and DC still settle

A rule this severe cuts both ways. Juries in both jurisdictions understand that it is unfair to deny all compensation to someone who was slightly careless, and a defendant who overplays a thin contributory negligence argument can lose credibility on everything else. Insurers know this. The defense is strongest when the plaintiff's negligence is obvious and documented, and weakest when it rests on speculation. Well-prepared cases with clean facts settle on reasonable terms in both jurisdictions; thin cases with messy facts do not. Knowing which kind you have is the first thing to find out.
