The R-1 visa requirements are stricter than many congregations expect. It is not enough that a person is devout, or that a church wants to hire them. USCIS tests four things: that the position is a ministerial, religious vocation, or religious occupation role; that the worker has been a member of the same denomination for at least two years before the petition is filed; that the work will be at least 20 hours a week and compensated; and that the employer is a bona fide nonprofit religious organization in the United States or an affiliate of one. Miss any one and the petition fails, regardless of how the others look.

## Three kinds of religious worker under the R-1 visa requirements

The R-1 category covers three types of position, and the petition must state which one applies.

A minister is a person authorized by a religious denomination, and fully trained according to its standards, to conduct religious worship and perform the other duties usually carried out by its clergy. Ordination or its equivalent in the denomination is the usual proof, along with evidence of the training the denomination requires. Lay preachers who are not authorized to perform clergy functions do not qualify as ministers.

A religious vocation is a formal lifetime commitment, through vows, investiture, or a similar ceremony, to a religious way of life. Nuns, monks, and religious brothers and sisters are the standard examples. The denomination must have a class of persons whose lives are dedicated to religious practice and who are set apart from other members.

A religious occupation is a position whose duties primarily relate to a traditional religious function and are recognized as a religious occupation within the denomination. Liturgical workers, religious instructors, cantors, missionaries, religious translators, and religious broadcasters fit. Purely administrative or support positions do not: janitors, maintenance staff, clerical workers, fundraisers, and solicitors of donations are excluded, even when they work for a church. A role that mixes religious and administrative duties is judged by what the duties primarily are, which our [R-1 religious worker visa](/practice-area/key-services/r-1-visa) team reviews against the job description before filing.

## Two years in the denomination

The worker must have been a member of a religious denomination that has a bona fide nonprofit religious organization in the United States for at least two years immediately before the petition is filed. Two points trip people up. The membership must be in the same denomination that will employ them, so a recent convert or someone who changed denominations last year must wait. And denomination has its own definition: a religious group or community of believers governed or administered under a common type of ecclesiastical government, which also shares at least one further marker, such as a common creed or statement of faith, a common form of worship, a common code of doctrine and discipline, common religious services and ceremonies, common places of worship or congregations, or comparable indicia. Independent churches can qualify, but they have to document those features rather than assume them.

## At least 20 hours a week, and compensation

The position must average at least 20 hours of work per week. Part-time ministers and religious workers can qualify, but a few hours on Sunday cannot. The work must also be compensated, whether through salary, stipend, or non-salaried support such as housing, board, or a living allowance, and the employer must show it can pay. The only exception to compensation is for a missionary program established by the denomination that has traditionally sent uncompensated missionaries, and that exception comes with its own documentation of the program and the worker's means of support. Volunteer religious work with no support arrangement does not satisfy the rule.

## The employer: a bona fide nonprofit religious organization

The petitioner must be a nonprofit religious organization in the United States that holds, or is covered by, an IRS determination of tax-exempt status under section 501(c)(3), or an organization affiliated with a religious denomination. A church that has never applied for its own determination letter can sometimes rely on a group exemption through its denomination, but it must document that coverage. A religious school, hospital, or charity affiliated with a denomination can petition if it submits a religious denomination certification confirming the affiliation. The employer also signs a detailed attestation about the organization, the position, and the worker; the evidence list is in [R-1 visa documents the employer must provide](/r-1-visa-documents-employer-checklist).

## What R-1 is not

R-1 is a temporary classification with a five-year limit and a required period abroad before it can be used again; see [how long you can stay on an R-1 visa](/r-1-visa-maximum-stay-extensions). It is not the right vehicle for a secular job at a religious institution. A theology professor at a church-affiliated university may be better served by an H-1B, and the university is likely cap-exempt, as our post on [cap-exempt H-1B employers](/h-1b-cap-exempt-employers) explains. And it is not an immigrant visa, although the special immigrant religious worker green card follows a parallel set of rules; the [business and employment immigration overview](/practice-area/key-services/business-employment-immigration-solutions) covers the longer-term routes.
