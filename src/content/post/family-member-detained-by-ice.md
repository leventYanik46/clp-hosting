When a family member is detained by Immigration and Customs Enforcement, the first days matter more than any other part of the case. People can be transferred quickly, pressured to sign forms, and in some cases removed within days. Families who act quickly can locate their relative, prevent harmful signatures, and start the legal steps toward release.

## Step 1: Find your family member

Use the **ICE Online Detainee Locator System**. You need either:

- The person's A-number, or
- Their full name, country of birth, and date of birth.

New detentions may not appear immediately. Check again over the next day. You can also call the local ICE field office or the detention facility directly. Write down the facility name, address, and the detainee's booking number.

Detainees are often moved, sometimes to other states. Check the locator daily.

## Step 2: Tell them not to sign anything without a lawyer

ICE may present forms such as:

- **Voluntary departure** or **voluntary return** agreements.
- **Stipulated removal orders**, which agree to removal without a hearing.

Signing can end the chance to fight the case and can create bars to returning. Your family member should state that they want to speak with a lawyer before signing.

If they fear returning to their country, they should say so clearly to the officers. That can trigger a credible fear interview.

## Step 3: Gather documents

Collect copies of:

- Passport, birth certificate, and any visa or green card.
- Any pending applications, receipt notices, or prior court papers.
- Proof of time in the U.S.: leases, pay stubs, school records, tax returns.
- Evidence of family ties: marriage certificates, children's birth certificates.
- Medical records if the person has health conditions.

Proof of more than two years of continuous presence can matter in expedited removal cases.

## Step 4: Determine which process applies

The legal route depends on the person's history:

- **Removal proceedings in immigration court**, which allow applications for relief.
- **Expedited removal**, which can lead to removal without a hearing for people who cannot show two years of presence or lawful admission.
- **Reinstatement of a prior removal order**, for people previously removed.

A detention lawyer can find out which applies and what defenses exist.

## Step 5: Bond or habeas

**Bond.** Some detainees can ask an immigration judge for a bond hearing. The judge considers flight risk and danger to the community. Letters of support, proof of a stable address, and family ties help.

**Limits on bond.** A 2025 Board of Immigration Appeals decision treats many people who entered without inspection as ineligible for a bond hearing before an immigration judge. That has pushed more cases into federal court.

**Habeas corpus.** When the government refuses a bond hearing or holds someone unlawfully or for a prolonged period, a habeas petition in federal district court may be the remedy.

## Step 6: Arrange contact and money

Detainees can make calls and receive commissary funds through facility systems. Ask the facility about phone accounts and visiting rules. Keep conversations about the case limited; calls are often recorded.

## What families should avoid

- Paying people who promise release for a fee without being lawyers.
- Contacting ICE with information that could harm the case before speaking with counsel.
- Waiting. Deadlines and transfers move quickly.

## Frequently asked questions

### How fast can someone be deported after detention?
It depends on the process. A person with a prior removal order or in expedited removal can be removed quickly. People in regular proceedings usually have hearings first.

### Can I visit a detained family member?
Most facilities allow visits under set rules and schedules. Check the facility's visitation policy and bring identification.

### Is it safe for undocumented family members to visit?
Visiting a detention facility can carry risk for people without status. Get advice before going in person.

### How much is an immigration bond?
Bond amounts are set by ICE or an immigration judge based on the case. There is a legal minimum, but amounts vary widely.

### Can we pay the bond online?
ICE has procedures for posting bonds, including through certain online systems and at ICE offices. The obligor must provide identification and follow ICE's process.

### What if my family member has a medical condition?
Tell the facility and ICE in writing and keep copies. Medical needs can also support a request for release.

### What is an immigration detention lawyer's first step?
Usually confirming where the person is held, which process applies, and whether bond, parole, or habeas is available.

### Can a detained person apply for asylum?
Yes. Detention does not prevent an asylum claim, and expressing fear early is important.

### Should family members talk to ICE about the case?
Speak with counsel first. Information given to ICE can affect the case.
