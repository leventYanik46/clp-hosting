An A-Number, short for Alien Registration Number, is a unique identifier that the Department of Homeland Security assigns to a noncitizen's immigration file. It usually looks like the letter A followed by nine digits, and it stays with you for life. Forms, interviews and court filings ask for it often, so knowing where to find it saves time.

## What an A-Number is

The A-Number is a file number. It links everything the government has on you: applications, approvals, denials, benefits and, if applicable, court proceedings. It is different from your Social Security number, your passport number and your USCIS online account number. In the U.S. immigration system, the word "alien" is the statutory term for a noncitizen, which is why it appears in the name.

Not every noncitizen has one. People who have applied for a green card, a work permit, certain humanitarian benefits, or who were placed in removal proceedings generally have an A-Number. Many people on temporary visas, such as tourists, usually do not have one unless they have applied for another benefit.

## How an A-Number is assigned

You do not request one directly. The government assigns an A-Number when you file certain applications or when an agency opens a file on you, such as in an immigration enforcement action. Once assigned, you keep the same number for all future applications. If you have more than one, tell a lawyer, because duplicates can cause confusion.

## Where to find your A-Number

Check these documents:

- **Permanent resident card (green card).** On the front, the number appears next to a label that says "USCIS#." For many cards, that is the A-Number, and it may have a different format on older cards.
- **Employment authorization document (work permit).** The "USCIS#" field on the card.
- **USCIS notices,** such as receipt notices, approval notices, biometrics appointments and interview notices. The A-Number often appears near the top, labeled "A-Number."
- **Immigrant visa.** The visa label from a U.S. consulate may include it, and the immigrant visa packet usually does.
- **Naturalization certificate,** which lists it.
- **Notice to Appear** and other immigration court documents.
- **Past applications and receipts** you or a lawyer kept.

Do not confuse it with the receipt number, which starts with three letters and has ten digits, or with your visa or passport number.

## If you cannot find it

- **Ask your attorney or the sponsor,** who may have it in their file.
- **Look through old mail,** emails and your USCIS online account.
- **Check old forms,** such as the I-485, I-130 or I-765 copies.
- **Request your file.** You can submit a Freedom of Information Act request to USCIS for your records, though it takes time, and you may need to provide identifying information.
- **Contact USCIS** through its official channels, though staff may not give out the number without verification.

## Protect it

Treat your A-Number like a sensitive identifier. Do not post it online, and share it only with people and agencies that need it. Be careful with anyone who asks for it by phone or text, since scammers pose as immigration officials. USCIS will not ask for payment by gift card or wire.

## When it matters

You will need your A-Number to file forms, check some case statuses, apply for a green card renewal, apply for citizenship and respond to notices. If you are unsure whether you have one, a lawyer can help you find it.
