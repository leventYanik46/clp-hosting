The EB-1A approval rate is one of the most quoted numbers in immigration, and one of the easiest to misread. A published percentage tells you how USCIS decided past cases, not whether yours will be approved. Two petitions with the same label can look nothing alike on the evidence.

## Where the numbers come from

USCIS publishes data on how many Form I-140 petitions it receives, approves and denies, broken out by classification. Law firms, advocacy groups and researchers then repackage those figures. Looking at the source data is better than relying on an article that quotes someone else's summary, because the numbers shift from year to year and by service center.

## Why a single percentage can mislead

- **Self-selection.** People who choose EB-1A often have strong records, and many get advice before filing. Weak cases may never be filed. So an approval figure partly reflects who decided to apply.
- **Quality varies.** The statistic mixes carefully built petitions with poorly prepared ones. Your own odds depend much more on the quality of your evidence and the way it is presented.
- **Changes over time.** Policy guidance and adjudication trends affect results. A rate from a prior year may not reflect today.
- **Denials are only part of the story.** Requests for Evidence and long processing times affect outcomes but do not show up in an approval percentage.
- **Premium processing and category mix** can also change what the numbers mean.

## How USCIS actually decides

An EB-1A petition has two steps. First, you must either show a one-time major internationally recognized award or meet at least three of ten listed criteria, such as awards, memberships in selective associations, published material about you, judging the work of others, original contributions, scholarly articles, display of your work, a leading role at distinguished organizations, high pay, or commercial success in the performing arts.

Second, USCIS makes a final merits determination. It asks whether the record as a whole shows sustained national or international acclaim and that you are among the small percentage at the top of the field. Plenty of denials come from petitioners who check the boxes in step one but fail the overall picture in step two.

## What strengthens a petition

1. **Independent evidence.** Third-party coverage, citations by researchers you do not know, and invitations to judge or speak carry more weight than self-reported claims.
2. **Context for each exhibit.** Show why an award is selective, how many people compete, and why a publication is major.
3. **A coherent field definition.** A petition that clearly defines what field you are in, and where you rank in it, is easier to evaluate.
4. **Strong recommendation letters** that explain your contribution specifically and concretely, not just praise.
5. **Consistency.** Resume, letters, exhibits and the petition letter should all tell the same story.

## What to do with the statistics

Use the approval rate as a rough background fact, not a prediction. A better question is whether your own record would persuade an officer who has seen many petitions and is not inclined to approve based on enthusiasm alone.
