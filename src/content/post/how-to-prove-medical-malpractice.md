You cannot prove medical malpractice with your own account of what happened, however accurate it is. Courts in Washington, DC, Virginia, and New York require a qualified medical expert to testify that the provider fell below the standard of care and that the failure caused the injury, and each jurisdiction adds paperwork that has to be in place before or at the moment you file. The proof is built in four steps, and CLP's [medical negligence team](/practice-area/key-services/medical-negligence) follows them in order on every case, because a shortcut at step one usually surfaces as a problem at step four.

## Step one: the complete record, including the audit trail

Under HIPAA you have a right to a copy of your medical records, and a provider generally has 30 days to produce them, with one extension. Request them from every provider involved: the hospital, each physician practice, the lab, the imaging center, the pharmacy, and the ambulance service. Ask specifically for the complete chart, including nursing notes, medication administration records, imaging files rather than just reports, fetal monitoring strips in a birth case, and the audit trail.

The audit trail is the metadata from the electronic health record showing who opened, created, or edited each entry and when. It is the only way to see whether a note describing a careful examination was written at the time or added after the complication. Build your own timeline alongside the record: symptoms, calls, visits, what you were told. Gaps between what you remember and what the chart says are often where the case lives.

## Step two: a qualified expert in the right specialty

The expert is the case. Each jurisdiction sets rules on who qualifies. Virginia requires that the expert have had an active clinical practice in the defendant's specialty, or a related field, within one year of the alleged negligence, and that the expert know the standard of care in Virginia. New York and DC ask whether the witness is qualified by training and experience to testify about that specialty, and a surgeon will generally not be allowed to testify about what a radiologist should have seen.

The expert reviews the full record and identifies each specific departure from the standard of care, in the language of the specialty, and then explains what should have happened instead. That written opinion is the foundation for the pre-suit filings described below, and later for the expert's testimony. A plaintiff whose expert is excluded at trial for lack of qualification usually loses the case on the spot, which is why the expert's credentials are vetted before the first filing. What the expert is looking for is explained in [what counts as medical negligence and what is a bad outcome](/medical-negligence-vs-bad-outcome).

## Step three: the pre-suit requirements in each jurisdiction

Virginia requires that the plaintiff have obtained a written expert opinion, before serving the complaint, stating that the defendant deviated from the standard of care and caused the injury. The defendant can demand proof that the certification existed, and a case served without it can be dismissed. Virginia also lets either side request a medical malpractice review panel after the case is filed, a non-binding review whose findings are admissible at trial.

DC requires a written notice of intention to sue, served on each defendant at least 90 days before filing, and the court then sends the case to mandatory mediation early in the litigation. New York requires a certificate of merit filed with the complaint, in which the attorney certifies having consulted a physician who believes there is a reasonable basis for the claim.

Government providers add a layer. A claim against a Veterans Affairs hospital, a military hospital, or a federally funded community health center is a claim against the United States under the Federal Tort Claims Act, which requires an administrative claim on Standard Form 95 within two years and a waiting period before suit. A claim against a New York public hospital requires a notice of claim within 90 days. These deadlines, and the ordinary ones, are collected in [medical malpractice statute of limitations in DC, VA, and NY](/medical-malpractice-deadlines-dc-va-ny).

## Step four: causation, more likely than not

The last element is the one the defense fights hardest. Its experts will say the outcome was already determined by the disease, that the patient's own conduct caused the delay, or that the alleged breach made no difference. Your expert has to show that, more likely than not, proper care would have produced a materially better result, and has to say what that result would have been. A second expert often covers damages: a life care planner for future medical needs, an economist for lost earnings. Jurisdictions differ on whether a reduced chance of recovery, short of a probable one, is compensable, and that difference can decide a delayed diagnosis case.

## Where attempts to prove medical malpractice break down

Cases fail at predictable points. Records that were never requested from a secondary provider. An expert in the wrong specialty. A patient who missed follow-up appointments, which supports a contributory negligence defense in Virginia and DC and a comparative fault reduction in New York. A hospital that turns out to have no employment relationship with the emergency physician, so the wrong defendant was named. And deadlines, which run while the review is underway.
