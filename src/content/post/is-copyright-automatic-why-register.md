Copyright protects your work from the moment you fix it in a tangible form, with no filing, no fee, and no notice required. That part is true, and it is why so many creators and companies never register. The part they miss is that an unregistered copyright is a right you cannot enforce in a U.S. court, and even once you register, the timing of that registration decides whether an infringer owes you a court-set amount per work or only whatever losses you can prove. Copyright registration is less about creating the right and more about making the right usable. Our [copyright registration service](/practice-area/key-services/copyright-registration) exists because the difference shows up precisely when a client needs it most.

## Copyright registration is the ticket to the courthouse

For works first published in the United States, the Copyright Act requires registration (or a refusal of registration) before an infringement suit can be filed. The Supreme Court settled in 2019 that "registration" means the Copyright Office has actually acted on the application, not merely received it. An applicant who discovers infringement and files that day will wait for the Office to examine the claim before suing, unless they pay for special handling, which is an expedited track with its own fee. Foreign works protected under the Berne Convention are exempt from this requirement, but they still need registration to claim the remedies described next.

The practical consequence is that a company with unregistered works has no immediate legal response to copying. It can send a demand letter and a DMCA notice, both of which are covered in our post on [how a DMCA takedown works](/dmca-takedown-notice-process), but it cannot file a complaint or seek an injunction until the certificate issues.

## Statutory damages and attorney's fees depend on timing

The most valuable benefit is tied to when you register, not just whether. Under Section 412 of the Copyright Act, statutory damages and attorney's fees are unavailable for any infringement that began before the effective date of registration, with one exception: if the work was registered within three months after first publication, those remedies are available for infringements that began during that window.

Why that matters: actual damages in copyright cases are often hard to prove. A small business whose product photos were copied by a competitor may struggle to show lost sales in dollars. Statutory damages let a court award an amount per work infringed, within a range set by statute and increased for willful infringement, without that proof. Attorney's fees can exceed the damages in a modest case and are what makes enforcement economically rational. An infringer who knows the work was registered before they copied it faces both exposures, which is why a registration certificate changes the tone of settlement negotiations.

The rule produces a simple habit: register within three months of publishing anything you would be willing to sue over. For a steady stream of content, group registration options let photographers, writers, and publishers cover many works in one application.

## A presumption of validity and ownership

A certificate issued before or within five years of first publication is prima facie evidence that the copyright is valid and that the facts stated in the certificate (author, claimant, date of creation, work-for-hire status) are true. The burden then shifts to the defendant to prove otherwise. Without a certificate, you prove authorship and ownership from scratch, which can mean producing drafts, metadata, contracts, and witnesses. For a company that acquired the work from a contractor, the certificate also records the chain of title in a public register.

## Customs, platforms, and the Copyright Claims Board

Registered copyrights can be recorded with U.S. Customs and Border Protection, which can then detain imported goods that infringe. Online marketplaces and app stores often ask for a registration number before acting on a complaint beyond a basic DMCA notice, especially for repeat enforcement programs. The Copyright Claims Board, a small-claims tribunal at the Copyright Office for lower-value disputes, requires a registration or a pending application before it will proceed. Each of these doors opens only with a certificate.

## Deterrence and the public record

Registration places the work, the owner, and the date in a searchable public database. Potential licensees can find you, and potential infringers can see that the work is owned by someone who took the step. A copyright notice on the work itself is no longer required, but it still defeats an "innocent infringement" argument that can reduce damages. Our [IP litigation and dispute resolution](/practice-area/key-services/ip-litigation-and-dispute-resolution) page describes how a clean registration record shortens the path from discovery to resolution.

## What registration does not do

Registration does not extend protection to ideas, methods, facts, or functionality. It does not protect a title, a name, or a short phrase, which belong to trademark law if they are protectable at all. It does not stop someone from independently creating a similar work. And it does not substitute for an assignment: if a contractor created the work, registering it in the company's name without a signed assignment produces a certificate with a false statement of ownership, which is worse than no certificate.
