Someone has registered yourbrand.com, or a misspelling of it, and is parking ads on it or pointing it at a competitor. You do not need to sue. A UDRP complaint, filed under the Uniform Domain-Name Dispute-Resolution Policy that every registrar of .com, .net, .org and most other generic top-level domains agrees to, can order the domain transferred to you within a few months on a written record. The catch is the burden: you must prove all three elements of the Policy, and the third, bad faith, is where most complaints fail. CLP's [trademark enforcement and domain dispute](/practice-area/key-services/trademark-enforcement-and-ttab-proceedings) practice treats the evidence file as the whole case, because it is.

## What a UDRP complaint can get you

Two remedies only: transfer of the domain to you, or cancellation of the registration. No damages, no costs, no injunction against future conduct. The decision binds the registrar, which implements it by moving the domain, so you do not need to chase the registrant.

The Policy covers generic top-level domains. Country-code domains such as .us, .uk or .tr have their own dispute policies, some modeled on the UDRP and some not, so check the rules for the specific extension before you file.

## The three elements you must prove

Paragraph 4(a) of the Policy requires you to show that:

1. The domain name is identical or confusingly similar to a trademark in which you have rights.
2. The registrant has no rights or legitimate interests in the domain name.
3. The domain name was registered and is being used in bad faith.

The first element is usually easy. A registered trademark settles the question of rights, and panels compare the mark to the domain's second-level string, ignoring the extension. Common-law rights can suffice, but you will have to prove them with evidence of use and recognition, which is one more reason to hold a registration from our [trademark registration](/practice-area/key-services/trademark-and-intellectual-property-registration) practice before a dispute arises.

The second element is proven by showing the absence of the usual legitimate interests: the registrant is not known by the name, is not making a bona fide offering of goods under it, and is not making a legitimate noncommercial or fair use. Once you make that showing, the burden shifts to the registrant to prove otherwise.

The third element is the fight. Bad faith must exist both at registration and in use. The Policy lists examples: registering the domain mainly to sell it to you for more than out-of-pocket costs, registering it to block you from reflecting your mark, registering it to disrupt a competitor, or using it to attract users for commercial gain through confusion with your mark. Timing matters a great deal. If the domain was registered before your trademark rights existed, panels will rarely find bad-faith registration, and the complaint will fail no matter how the domain is used today.

## Evidence that wins or loses

Build the file before you file:

- Your registration certificate, or proof of common-law rights with dates.
- Screenshots of the domain's current content and archived versions over time.
- Registration history showing when the registrant acquired the domain relative to your first use.
- Any offer to sell the domain, and any correspondence.
- Evidence of a pattern: other domains the registrant holds that copy known brands.
- For a parked or inactive domain, facts supporting passive holding in bad faith, such as the distinctiveness of your mark and the registrant's use of a privacy service to hide its identity.

A complaint with twenty exhibits and a clear timeline tends to succeed. A complaint that recites the three elements and attaches a registration certificate tends not to.

## How the process runs

You file with an approved provider, most often the WIPO Arbitration and Mediation Center or Forum, and pay the provider's fee, which depends on the number of domains and whether you choose a one-member or three-member panel. The provider checks the complaint for compliance and the registrar locks the domain so it cannot be transferred away. The registrant then has 20 days to respond. Many never do, but a default does not win the case automatically; the panel still reviews your evidence against the three elements.

The panel issues a written decision shortly after appointment. If it orders transfer, the registrar waits 10 business days before implementing it, so that the registrant can file a court action if it wishes. If no suit is filed, the domain moves to you.

## When URS or a federal lawsuit fits better

The Uniform Rapid Suspension system is a faster, cheaper procedure available for newer generic top-level domains, not for .com. It requires clear and convincing evidence, and the only remedy is suspension of the domain for the rest of its registration term. It fits blatant cases where you simply want the site offline.

The Anticybersquatting Consumer Protection Act lets you sue in federal court for registering, trafficking in or using a domain confusingly similar to your mark with a bad-faith intent to profit. Court is slower and more expensive, but it offers damages, including statutory damages per domain, attorney's fees in exceptional cases, and an in rem action against the domain itself when the registrant cannot be found or is outside U.S. jurisdiction. ## Before you file

Think about whether a demand letter helps or hurts. A letter can prompt a cooperative registrant to hand over the domain, but it can also prompt a cybersquatter to move the domain to a new registrar or a new shell owner before the lock takes effect. We weigh that risk in our post on [trademark cease and desist letters](/trademark-cease-and-desist-letter-risks). For a serial squatter, filing first and letting the registrar lock speak for you is often the better order. Also check whether buying the domain quietly, through an intermediary, would cost less than the proceeding; sometimes it does, and the Policy does not require you to try.
