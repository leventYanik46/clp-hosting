A NOID, or Notice of Intent to Deny, is USCIS telling you that based on the record it has, it plans to deny your petition or application. It is not a denial yet. It is your last formal chance to fix the problem before a decision. Approval after a NOID happens regularly, but only when the response directly answers what the officer found wrong.

## What is a NOID?

USCIS issues a NOID when it believes the evidence does not support approval, often because of derogatory information or a legal deficiency that more of the same evidence will not cure. The notice should explain the reasons and give you a deadline to respond.

Common triggers include:

- Information from a site visit that does not match the petition
- Inconsistencies between this filing and earlier ones
- Doubts about whether a job, business, or relationship is genuine
- A legal finding, such as ineligibility based on prior immigration history
- Evidence the officer found on their own, which the regulations generally require USCIS to disclose so you can rebut it

## NOID vs. RFE

A Request for Evidence asks for something missing. A NOID signals that the officer has already reached a tentative negative conclusion. The practical differences:

- **Tone and burden.** An RFE asks you to complete the record. A NOID asks you to change the officer's mind.
- **Time.** RFE deadlines are often up to 87 days. NOID responses are capped at 30 days by regulation, plus three days if the notice was mailed. USCIS cannot extend this.
- **Stakes.** If you do not respond to a NOID, denial is close to certain.

## How to respond to a NOID

### Read the stated reasons carefully

List every ground the officer raised. A response that addresses three out of four reasons will likely be denied on the fourth.

### Get your full record

If the NOID relies on something you have never seen, such as a site visit report or an earlier file, ask for it. A FOIA request may take longer than your deadline, so do not wait on it. Respond with what you have and note what was not disclosed.

### Answer with evidence, not argument alone

Legal argument matters, but it works best paired with documents that directly contradict the officer's finding: payroll records, signed declarations, photographs, independent third-party evidence.

### Explain inconsistencies honestly

If the record contains an error, explain it and document the correct facts. Unexplained contradictions are what turn a NOID into a denial.

### Send it on time

Use a trackable method and keep proof of delivery. USCIS must receive the response by the deadline.

## What happens after you respond

USCIS reviews the response and issues a decision. Possible outcomes are approval, denial, or occasionally a second request. If the case is denied, options may include a motion to reopen or reconsider on Form I-290B, an appeal to the Administrative Appeals Office where available, refiling, or in some cases federal court review.

## Approval after a NOID

Officers do change their minds when a response is specific and well documented. The cases that recover usually share three traits: the response is organized by each ground in the NOID, it includes new evidence rather than repeating old submissions, and it addresses credibility concerns head on.


## Frequently asked questions

### Can I withdraw my case after a NOID?

Yes, but withdrawal is not always the best choice. A withdrawal may avoid a formal denial, but it can still raise questions in later filings. Get advice before deciding.

### Does a NOID mean I am in trouble with immigration enforcement?

Not by itself. A NOID is a benefits decision. If the case is denied and you have no other status, though, a denial can lead to removal proceedings in some cases. That is one more reason to respond carefully.

### Can I submit evidence after the deadline?

Generally no. USCIS decides based on the record at the deadline. Late evidence usually must come in through a motion or a new filing.
