If you are laid off on an H-1B, you generally have up to 60 days, or until your authorized stay ends if that comes sooner, to do something about your status. That window is called the grace period, and what you do inside it decides whether you stay in the United States lawfully or start accruing problems.

## How the H-1B layoff grace period works

When employment ends, H-1B status does not vanish on the last day. Federal rules allow a period of up to 60 consecutive days after the job ends, or until the end of your approved validity period (the date on your I-94 record), whichever is shorter. You are not authorized to work during the grace period unless a new petition is filed, but you are not accruing unlawful presence while it runs.

Two points trip people up. First, the 60 days is counted from the last day of employment, not from the day you were told about the layoff. Second, the grace period is not renewable. If you use it up and nothing is on file, you are out of status.

## Your main options

There are four common paths. Which one fits depends on how fast you can act and what you want next.

1. **Find a new H-1B employer.** A new employer can file a change of employer petition. Under portability, you may be able to start work once USCIS receives the filing. The filing must be made during your grace period.
2. **Change to another status.** If you cannot find a job in time, you may be able to request a change of status, for example to B-2 visitor, or to F-1 student if you enroll in a qualifying program. These have their own eligibility rules and timing issues.
3. **Depart the United States.** Leaving before the grace period ends avoids any gap in status. You can often return later on a new H-1B with a new employer.
4. **Pursue another basis to stay.** A pending green card application, a change to a different work visa, or a family-based option may be available depending on your circumstances.

## What employers must do

Your former employer has obligations too. It is expected to notify USCIS that your employment ended, and to offer to pay reasonable return transportation costs. Asking about these items in writing can help protect you. If you have a pending green card process through that employer, ask what happens to it and whether your priority date is preserved.

## What happens if you miss the window

If the grace period closes and you have no pending petition or status change, you fall out of status. USCIS may later deny a new filing, and if the government decides to place you in removal proceedings it starts that by issuing a Notice to Appear (NTA). An NTA does not mean you will be deported, but it begins a court process in which you need to be represented.

## Practical steps for the first week

- Write down your last day of employment and count 60 days from it.
- Collect your I-94 record, pay stubs, approval notices and your last LCA.
- Tell any prospective employer about the clock early, since they need time to prepare a transfer petition.
- Speak to an immigration lawyer before you decide between a transfer, a change of status and departure.

Time is the whole problem here, so the sooner the strategy conversation happens, the more options stay open.
