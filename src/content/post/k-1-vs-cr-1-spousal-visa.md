A U.S. citizen engaged to someone abroad has two ways to bring that person here permanently. The K-1 fiancé visa brings the fiancé to the United States first; the couple marries within 90 days and the new spouse applies for a green card from inside the country. The CR-1 spousal visa reverses the order: the couple marries first, wherever they can, the citizen files Form I-130, and the spouse enters the United States as a permanent resident. The K-1 or CR-1 decision turns on where you can marry, how soon the fiancé needs to work, how much the second application matters to you, and the fiancé's immigration history.

## K-1 or CR-1: what each route involves

The K-1 starts with Form I-129F at USCIS, the petition our [K-1 fiancé visa](/practice-area/key-services/k-1-fiance-visa) practice prepares. After approval, the fiancé completes a DS-160, a medical exam and a consular interview, enters on the K-1, marries within 90 days, and then files Form I-485 with Form I-864, Form I-765 and Form I-131. The green card, usually conditional, comes at the end of that second case. The full sequence is in [the K-1 fiancé visa process](/k-1-fiance-visa-process-steps).

The CR-1 starts with a marriage and Form I-130. After USCIS approval, the National Visa Center collects the DS-260, civil documents and the I-864, and the consulate interviews the spouse. The spouse enters on an immigrant visa and is a permanent resident on arrival. If the marriage is under two years old at entry the visa is a CR-1 with a conditional card; at two years or more it is an IR-1 with a ten-year card. Our [marriage-based green card](/practice-area/key-services/marriage-based-green-card) practice handles the consular track, and the choice between a consulate and adjustment for an already-married couple is covered in [adjustment of status or consular processing](/spouse-adjustment-vs-consular-processing).

## Where the K-1 wins

Marrying in the United States. Some couples cannot marry abroad: the fiancé's country does not recognize their marriage, the citizen cannot travel, or the families are here. The K-1 is the only route that puts the wedding in the United States without the fiancé entering on a visitor visa with a plan to stay, which risks a misrepresentation finding.

Earlier arrival, often. The K-1 frequently brings the fiancé to the United States sooner than a CR-1 brings a spouse, because the immigrant visa route adds the NVC stage and a longer consular review. Whether that holds at any given time depends on current USCIS and consular processing, which both agencies publish.

Testing the relationship on the ground. Some couples want to live in the same country before marrying. The K-1 permits that, within the 90 days.

## Where the CR-1 wins

Status on arrival. The CR-1 spouse is a permanent resident the day they enter. They can work immediately, get a Social Security number, travel, and sign a lease with a resident's documents. The K-1 spouse arrives with a 90-day admission, cannot work until the adjustment-based work permit is issued, and cannot leave the country until advance parole is approved.

One application, one set of fees. The CR-1 is a single case; the K-1 is two, each with its own USCIS fees and its own interview. For couples on a budget the difference is real.

Fewer traps. The K-1 is a single-purpose visa with hard edges: the 90-day deadline, no extension, no change of status, and adjustment only through the petitioner. The consequences when the wedding does not happen are described in [what happens if you do not marry within 90 days](/k-1-visa-90-day-marriage-deadline). A CR-1 spouse has none of those constraints.

## Facts that decide the question

A permanent resident petitioner has no K-1 option; the couple must marry and file an I-130 in the F2A category.

A fiancé with a prior overstay, a criminal record or a past misrepresentation faces an admissibility review on either route. Where a waiver is available, it is judged under the immigrant hardship standard in both cases, but the point in the process where it is decided differs, and so does the fallback if it is denied. We work through that before recommending a route.

A couple already married has no K-1 option. A K-3 spousal visa exists on paper for married couples waiting on an I-130, but in practice the I-130 is usually approved before the K-3 would help, and the Department of State closes the K-3 case when that happens.

A citizen whose income is below the I-864 threshold should resolve that before either route, since the affidavit is required at the CR-1 interview and again at K-1 adjustment, and a joint sponsor must be lined up.

## A practical way to decide

Ask three questions in order. Can you marry abroad, and are you willing to? If not, the K-1 is your route. If yes, does the fiancé need to work or travel soon after arrival? If so, the CR-1 is stronger. If neither consideration is decisive, compare current processing times for the I-129F and I-130 and the consulate's interview availability, and choose the route that gets you living together, with status, soonest. Whichever you choose, we prepare the file so that nothing has to be redone at the next stage.
