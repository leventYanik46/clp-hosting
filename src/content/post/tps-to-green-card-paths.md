Temporary Protected Status does not lead to a green card by itself. It protects you from removal and lets you work while your country's designation lasts. But many TPS holders qualify for permanent residence through another route, usually family or employment. With several TPS designations terminated or under legal challenge, now is the time to check whether one of those routes is open to you.

## Why TPS alone is not enough

TPS is temporary by design. When a country's designation ends, holders return to whatever status they had before, or to no status. There is no TPS-to-green-card application. To get permanent residence, you need an independent basis, such as a qualifying relative or employer.

## The admission problem

To adjust status inside the U.S., most applicants must have been "inspected and admitted or paroled." In 2021, the Supreme Court held in Sanchez v. Mayorkas that a TPS grant by itself does not count as an admission. That means TPS holders who originally entered without inspection generally cannot adjust status based on TPS alone, even if they marry a U.S. citizen.

However:

- If you entered with a visa and later overstayed, you were admitted, and adjustment may be possible
- Travel abroad on authorized TPS-related travel documents can affect whether you are treated as inspected and admitted on return; USCIS policy on this has changed over time and should be checked for your exact situation
- Some TPS holders can use consular processing abroad, though departing can trigger unlawful presence bars that may need a waiver

## Path 1: Family-based green cards

- **Spouse, parent, or unmarried child under 21 of a U.S. citizen.** Immediate relatives face no annual visa limits. If you were admitted, you may be able to adjust status even after an overstay.
- **Other relatives.** Siblings of U.S. citizens and spouses of green card holders fall into preference categories with waiting lines.

## Path 2: Employment-based green cards

An employer can sponsor you through PERM and an I-140. Employment-based adjustment usually also requires maintaining lawful status, which can be a barrier for TPS holders without a lawful status history. Talk to counsel about whether an exception applies.

## Path 3: Humanitarian and court-based options

Depending on your history, options may include asylum, a U visa or T visa, VAWA, or cancellation of removal in immigration court. These have their own eligibility rules.

## Check your country's designation

TPS designations are announced in the Federal Register and summarized on the USCIS TPS page. Check:

- Whether your country's designation has been extended, terminated, or is subject to a court order
- Re-registration windows and deadlines
- Whether your EAD has been automatically extended by a Federal Register notice, which is one of the few exceptions to the end of automatic EAD extensions

Missing a re-registration deadline can cost you TPS even if your country remains designated.

## What to gather

- Your I-94 or other entry records
- Every TPS approval notice and EAD
- Travel authorization documents and passport stamps from any trips
- Marriage, birth, and other family records


## Frequently asked questions

### Can I keep TPS while my green card case is pending?

Yes. You can hold TPS and pursue a green card at the same time. Keep re-registering for TPS on time until the green card is approved.

### Does marriage to a U.S. citizen fix everything?

Not always. If you entered without inspection and have no qualifying admission or parole, marriage alone may not allow you to adjust in the U.S. You may need consular processing and a waiver.

### What if my country's TPS ends?

You return to whatever status you held before TPS, or no status. Start reviewing other options before the termination date.

### Can my U.S. citizen child sponsor me?

Yes, once the child is 21. A parent of an adult citizen is an immediate relative, though the same admission and entry history questions apply to whether you can adjust in the U.S.
