You can apply for citizenship with an expired green card, and you can apply with a dismissed charge in your past, but each situation needs care. The expired card is mostly a paperwork problem, and the charge is a disclosure question. Both are easier to handle if you understand how USCIS looks at them before you file.

## Can I apply for citizenship with an expired green card

Your permanent resident status does not end when the card expires. The card is proof of status, not the status itself. You can generally file Form N-400 with an expired card, and you include a copy of the card with your application.

Even so, an expired card can create practical problems, such as proving your status to employers, banks or at the border. Many people renew the card with Form I-90 first or at the same time. Whether it makes sense to renew before filing depends on how soon you plan to naturalize and how long renewal is taking, so it is worth asking an attorney or checking current USCIS guidance. Always check the current rules, because USCIS practices on this change.

If you have been abroad for long periods, if you have a conditional card that expired, or if you are not certain whether you remain a permanent resident, speak to a lawyer before filing. A conditional resident whose card expired without a petition to remove conditions faces a different problem that cannot be solved through the N-400.

## Can I apply with dismissed charges

A dismissed charge does not automatically bar you from citizenship, but it does matter. The N-400 asks about arrests, citations and charges, and it generally asks about all of them, including those that were dismissed, expunged, sealed, or happened when you were a minor. Hiding an arrest can lead to a denial or worse, since a false statement on the application can be treated as a lack of good moral character or as misrepresentation.

What to do:

- **Disclose everything the form asks,** even if you think it is minor or old.
- **Gather certified court records,** known as dispositions, showing what happened.
- **Bring the documents to your interview.**
- **Be ready to explain** what happened, truthfully and briefly.

USCIS looks at your conduct during the statutory period and may look beyond it. Some offenses permanently bar a person from showing good moral character. Others create a temporary bar. A dismissal usually helps, but the underlying conduct can still be relevant. For example, a dismissed charge for a drug offense may raise questions about the behavior itself. An attorney can review your records and tell you whether to apply now or wait.

## How long after a green card can you apply

- **Five years** as a permanent resident is the general rule.
- **Three years** if you have been married to and living with a U.S. citizen for those three years, and your spouse has been a citizen for that time.
- **Up to 90 days early.** You can file within 90 days before reaching the required period.
- **Special rules** apply to some military applicants.

You also have to meet the continuous residence and physical presence requirements, so a long trip can delay eligibility even if the calendar years have passed.

## Before you file

1. Make sure you meet the time and presence requirements.
2. Collect your documents, including your green card, tax records and travel history.
3. Gather court records for any arrest.
4. Decide whether to renew an expired card.
5. Consider a consultation if anything on your record concerns you.

A short legal review can keep a simple application from becoming a complicated one.
