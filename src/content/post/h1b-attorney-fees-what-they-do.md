An H-1B attorney prepares the petition your employer files with USCIS, makes sure the job and the worker meet the legal definition of a specialty occupation, and steps in when the government asks questions. The fee you are quoted depends mostly on whether the case is a simple filing or one that is likely to draw scrutiny.

## What an H-1B attorney handles

The H-1B petition is built from several pieces that have to agree with each other. The attorney drafts the Labor Condition Application (LCA) for the Department of Labor, prepares Form I-129 and its support letter, and explains why the position requires a bachelor's degree or higher in a specific field. For cap-subject cases, the attorney also handles the registration during the lottery period and the petition filing that follows a selection.

Much of the real work is invisible. A good H-1B immigration lawyer checks the job duties against the offered wage level, confirms the beneficiary's degree matches the role, and spots problems before USCIS does. Third-party worksite placements, part-time roles and degrees in a field that only loosely relates to the job all deserve a closer look.

## Where the attorney helps after filing

If USCIS issues a Request for Evidence, the attorney reads the notice, identifies what the officer actually doubts and assembles a response. The same lawyer usually handles extensions, amendments after a job change, transfers to a new employer, and the move toward a green card when the time comes.

## How H-1B legal fees are usually structured

Most firms quote H-1B work one of three ways:

- **A flat fee per petition.** This is the most common model for straightforward cases. It typically covers the LCA, the petition and the support letter.
- **Separate fees for add-ons.** Responding to an RFE, filing a premium processing request, or preparing dependents' applications are often billed on top of the base fee.
- **Hourly billing.** More common for complicated matters, such as a case already in trouble.

Ask for the fee agreement in writing and ask what is excluded. A low headline price that leaves out the RFE response is not necessarily a bargain.

## Who pays what

Legal fees and government fees are separate line items, and the law limits what an employer can push onto the worker. Employers generally carry the cost of the petition itself, and the ACWIA training fee cannot be passed to the H-1B worker. Costs tied to a spouse's or child's own applications are often handled differently, and some employers cover them anyway. If the cost split matters to you, settle it with your employer before the petition is filed.

## When a consultation is worth it

You do not always need outside counsel. Many larger employers have in-house immigration teams. But if you are a small company filing for the first time, if the job sits at a client site, or if you have received an RFE or a denial, an experienced attorney usually pays for themselves in avoided mistakes.

Questions worth asking at the first meeting: how many H-1B cases the attorney files each year, who will actually do the drafting, and what happens to the fee if the registration is not selected in the lottery.
