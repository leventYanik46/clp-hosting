People often use "visa" and "passport" interchangeably, but they're different documents with different purposes. What's the difference between a visa and a passport? A passport proves your identity and citizenship. A visa gives you permission to travel to a country and ask to be admitted. For the U.S., there's also a third document that matters: the I-94.

## What a passport is

A passport is issued by your country of citizenship. It:

- Proves your identity
- Proves your citizenship
- Lets you travel internationally and return home

## What a visa is

A visa is issued by the country you want to visit. For the U.S., visas are issued by the State Department at U.S. embassies and consulates. A U.S. visa:

- Lets you travel to a U.S. port of entry
- Lets you ask CBP to admit you
- Shows the type of visa you have, such as B-2 for tourism or F-1 for study

A visa doesn't guarantee entry. CBP decides whether to admit you.

## The I-94: the record that controls your stay

When you're admitted, CBP creates an I-94 record showing:

- Your class of admission
- The date you're allowed to stay until

The I-94, not the visa, controls how long you can stay. You can download your I-94 from CBP's website.

## Passport vs visa: key differences

| | Passport | Visa |
|---|---|---|
| Issued by | Your country | The country you're visiting |
| Purpose | Identity and citizenship | Permission to seek entry |
| Required for U.S. travel | Yes, for most travelers | Yes, unless visa-exempt |
| Controls length of stay | No | No, the I-94 does |

## If your visa expires

You can stay in the U.S. as long as your I-94 is valid, even if your visa expires. But you'll need a new visa before traveling back to the U.S. after you leave.

## If your passport expires

Your passport should be valid for at least six months beyond your stay for many nationalities, though some countries are exempt. If your passport expires, your visa may still be valid, and you can travel with both the old and new passport.

## Visa-exempt travel

Citizens of Visa Waiver Program countries can travel to the U.S. without a visa for short visits, using ESTA. Canadians generally don't need visas for most visits.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking a valid visa means you can stay
- Overstaying an I-94 because the visa is valid longer
- Traveling with an expired visa
- Forgetting passport validity requirements

## Practical example

A student enters on an F-1 visa valid for five years. Her I-94 says "D/S" (duration of status), meaning she can stay as long as she maintains student status. Her visa later expires, but she can continue studying because her I-94 and status remain valid. If she travels abroad, she'll need a new F-1 visa to return. Another traveler enters on a B-2 visa valid for 10 years, but his I-94 allows only a six-month stay. If he stays longer without an extension, he is overstaying, even though his visa is still valid.

## Embassy vs consulate

A U.S. embassy is the main diplomatic mission in a country's capital. U.S. consulates are branch offices in other cities. Both can issue visas, but some consulates handle only certain visa types.

## Visa number vs passport number

The visa itself has a number, sometimes called the foil number or control number, printed in red on many U.S. visas. That number is different from your passport number. Forms and online systems may ask for one or the other, so check which one is requested. Using the wrong number is a common cause of errors on forms like the DS-160 and ESTA applications.
