The asylum interview is the one occasion when a USCIS officer hears your claim from you, under oath, and tests it against everything in your file. It is non-adversarial, which means no government lawyer cross-examines you, but it is decisive: an interview that is vague, inconsistent with your Form I-589, or unprepared sends the case to immigration court. Our [affirmative asylum application practice](/practice-area/key-services/affirmative-asylum-applications) treats interview preparation as the second half of the application, not an afterthought. Here is how the day unfolds and how to be ready for it.

## Before the asylum interview

USCIS schedules interviews from the newest filings backward, as of this writing, so a recent applicant may be called within months while older cases wait. The notice tells you the date and the asylum office. Applicants in Washington, DC, and Northern Virginia are interviewed by the Arlington office; New York applicants are assigned by address.

Reschedule only for a real reason. A reschedule requested by the applicant stops the 180-day asylum clock that controls work authorization, and a second request is scrutinized. If you must reschedule, do it in writing before the date.

Use the weeks before the interview to update the file. New evidence should be submitted ahead of time in the manner the notice specifies rather than handed across the table. Review the I-589 line by line and prepare a written list of corrections: a new address, a new job, a child born since filing, a date you now realize was wrong. Officers expect corrections and respect them; they do not respect an applicant who confirms an answer the file contradicts.

## Who comes with you

You must bring your own interpreter if you are not fluent in English. As of this writing the regulation requires an interpreter who is at least 18, fluent in English and your language, and not your attorney, not a witness in your case, and not a representative of your home government. USCIS may put its own contract interpreter on the phone to monitor the interpretation, and it will stop the interview if the monitor reports problems. Choose a trained interpreter, not a relative who will soften what you say.

Your attorney may attend, sit beside you, and make a statement at the end, but may not answer questions for you. A spouse or child included on your application must attend, because they are applicants too and may be interviewed separately. Witnesses may attend if arranged in advance.

## What the officer asks

The interview follows a fixed order. The officer places you under oath and confirms your identity, then walks through the biographic sections of the I-589: every name you have used, every address, every job, every entry to and exit from the United States, every prior application or visa, your family members and where they are. Expect this to take a long time; it is where inconsistencies between your form and your visa applications surface.

The officer then turns to the claim. What happened, when, where, who did it, what was said, what injuries resulted, whether you reported it and what the authorities did, why you believe you were targeted, whether you could live safely elsewhere in your country, and what you fear now. The officer will ask the same question more than once in different words, and will ask for details that seem small: the layout of a room, the uniform someone wore, the month of a second visit. Those details are how credibility is measured. The last section covers the bars: criminal history, any harm you caused to others, time spent in other countries, and the one-year filing deadline. Your attorney may then summarize the legal theory and address anything that needs context.

## How credibility is judged

Under the REAL ID Act the officer weighs the totality of the circumstances: demeanor, candor, responsiveness, the inherent plausibility of the account, consistency between what you say and what you wrote, and consistency with country conditions. An inconsistency need not go to the heart of the claim to count against you. Three habits protect you. Say that you do not remember when it is true instead of guessing; a guessed date that contradicts a medical record ends a case. Explain discrepancies yourself rather than waiting to be caught; if a visa application said you were traveling for tourism, say why. And know your prior statements: the DS-160, any credible fear interview record, any police report, and your own [asylum declaration](/how-to-write-asylum-declaration), which the officer will have read closely. If trauma affects your memory, say so, and consider a psychological evaluation that explains the gaps before the interview rather than after.

Preparation means a full mock interview with your attorney, in the order above, with an interpreter if you will use one. It does not mean memorizing answers. Memorized testimony sounds memorized, and an officer who hears it will probe until the script runs out.

## What to bring

- The interview notice and a government photo ID, plus your passport and every travel document, even expired ones.
- Originals of every document you submitted with the I-589, with the certified translations, so the officer can compare them to the copies.
- Any new evidence, in duplicate, if it could not be sent ahead.
- Your interpreter and their identification.
- The passports, birth certificates, and marriage certificate for any spouse or child on the application.
- Your written list of corrections to the I-589.

## After the interview

Decisions are usually mailed rather than delivered on the spot. The three possible outcomes, a grant, a referral to immigration court, or a notice of intent to deny for applicants still in lawful status, are explained in our post on [what happens after the asylum interview](/what-happens-after-asylum-interview). If the case is referred, the I-589 continues before an immigration judge, and the testimony you gave at the interview becomes part of the record the judge compares against, which is one more reason to get it right the first time.
