Joining the U.S. military does not by itself give someone a green card. Most branches require recruits to be U.S. citizens or lawful permanent residents before enlisting. Where military service helps most is after you have a green card: it offers a faster path to citizenship. Separate programs can also help family members of service members who are undocumented.

## Can an undocumented immigrant join the U.S. military?

Generally no. Enlistment typically requires U.S. citizenship or a green card. The Military Accessions Vital to the National Interest program, known as MAVNI, once let certain nonimmigrants with critical skills enlist, but it has been suspended for years and is not accepting applicants.

## Citizenship through military service

Green card holders who serve can naturalize under special rules:

- **Peacetime service (INA 328):** One year of honorable service can qualify a permanent resident to naturalize without the usual residence requirements, if they apply while serving or within six months of separation.
- **Service during designated periods of hostilities (INA 329):** Honorable service during a designated period, including the period that began on September 11, 2001, can allow naturalization even without a green card in some situations, and without residence requirements.

USCIS has staff who handle military naturalization, and there is generally no filing fee for these applications. Applicants still must meet good moral character, English, and civics requirements.

## Parole in place for military families

Parole in place lets certain undocumented spouses, parents, and children of active-duty service members, reservists, or veterans who entered without inspection be paroled while remaining in the U.S. Parole can make them eligible to adjust status to a green card through a qualifying family relationship, because adjustment usually requires inspection and admission or parole.

It is discretionary and is granted case by case. Applicants request it from USCIS with Form I-131 and proof of the military relationship.

## Deferred action for military families

Deferred action is a discretionary decision not to pursue removal for a period. USCIS has considered deferred action requests for family members of service members and veterans in some circumstances, which can bring work authorization. Availability and policy have shifted over time, so confirm the current approach.

## Posthumous benefits

If a service member dies from combat-related injury, the service member may be granted posthumous citizenship, and certain surviving family members may be able to pursue green cards.

## What to gather

For service members:

- Form N-426, Request for Certification of Military or Naval Service
- Proof of honorable service, such as a DD-214 for veterans
- Your green card, if you have one

For family members:

- Proof of the family relationship
- Proof of the service member's status
- Your entry history and any immigration records


## Frequently asked questions

### Can my spouse get a green card faster because I serve?

Military status does not create a separate green card category for spouses, but naturalization for the service member, parole in place for family members, and expedited processing requests can shorten the path.

### Can I naturalize while stationed abroad?

Yes. USCIS can conduct naturalization interviews and oath ceremonies at certain military installations overseas.

### Does military naturalization require the English test?

Yes. Service members must meet the English and civics requirements, though interviews and testing can be arranged on base.
