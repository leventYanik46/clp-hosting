The K-1 fiancé visa process runs through three agencies in a fixed order: USCIS decides the petition, the National Visa Center routes it, and a U.S. embassy or consulate interviews the fiancé and issues the visa. Nothing can be done out of sequence, and the couple's main job between stages is to keep documents current and the relationship evidence growing. Here is each stage, what you file, and where cases stall.

## Stage one of the K-1 fiancé visa process: Form I-129F

The U.S. citizen files Form I-129F, Petition for Alien Fiancé(e), with USCIS. The petition does three things: proves the petitioner's citizenship, establishes that the couple meets the eligibility rules, and makes the IMBRA disclosures. The requirements themselves are covered in [K-1 visa requirements](/k-1-visa-requirements-who-qualifies); the evidence that proves them goes in here, and our [K-1 fiancé visa petition](/practice-area/key-services/k-1-fiance-visa) practice assembles it.

A complete I-129F package contains proof of citizenship, proof that any prior marriages have ended, a passport-style photograph of each partner, signed statements of intent to marry from both, proof of the in-person meeting within the past two years, evidence of the relationship over time, and the IMBRA disclosures and any criminal records. Children who will follow on K-2 visas are listed on the form. Pay the current USCIS filing fee shown on the form page.

USCIS issues a receipt notice and, after review, an approval notice or a Request for Evidence. An approved I-129F is valid for four months, though the consulate can revalidate it. Processing times for the I-129F vary and are published by USCIS; if the petition sits far past the posted time with no action, our [immigration mandamus](/practice-area/key-services/immigration-mandamus) team can ask a federal court to compel a decision, as explained in [immigration mandamus lawsuits](/immigration-mandamus-lawsuits).

## Stage two: the National Visa Center

USCIS sends the approved petition to the National Visa Center, which assigns a case number and forwards the file to the embassy or consulate with jurisdiction over the fiancé's residence. For a K-1 the NVC stage is brief; the substantive document collection happens at the consulate rather than through the NVC's online portal, which is the main procedural difference from an immigrant visa case. Keep the NVC case number; everything that follows references it.

## Stage three: the consular stage

The fiancé completes Form DS-160, the online nonimmigrant visa application, pays the visa application fee and schedules the interview following the specific consulate's instructions. Consulates differ in how they schedule K visas, and the embassy website for the fiancé's country is the authoritative source.

Before the interview, the fiancé gathers:

- A passport valid for travel to the United States.
- A birth certificate and, where applicable, divorce decrees or death certificates ending prior marriages.
- Police certificates from the countries where the fiancé has lived for the required periods since age 16.
- The medical examination report from a panel physician approved by the embassy, completed shortly before the interview.
- Form I-134, the petitioner's declaration of financial support, with the petitioner's most recent tax return and proof of current income.
- Updated evidence of the relationship since the petition was filed: messages, visits, photographs.

The consular officer interviews the fiancé, usually alone, about the relationship, the petitioner, the wedding plans and the fiancé's background. The officer has the petitioner's IMBRA disclosures and shares them with the fiancé. If the visa is approved, the passport is returned with the K-1 visa foil, normally within days. If the officer needs more, the case is refused under section 221(g) pending documents, which is common and usually curable. A refusal on an inadmissibility ground is different and may require a waiver.

## Stage four: entry and the 90-day clock

The K-1 visa is valid for a single entry within six months of issuance. At the port of entry, Customs and Border Protection admits the fiancé for 90 days and issues an electronic I-94. The couple must marry within that period; the K-1 cannot be extended. We recommend having the wedding planned, and the marriage license requirements of your state checked, before the fiancé lands.

The marriage can be a courthouse ceremony. What matters is a civil marriage certificate, because the next filing cannot go in without it. A K-1 holder may apply for a work permit on the basis of K-1 status, but it expires with the 90 days and rarely arrives in time to be useful; the practical work authorization comes with the adjustment filing.

## What comes after the wedding

The K-1 is only half of the case. After the marriage, the new spouse files Form I-485 with USCIS to adjust status, along with Form I-864, Form I-765 and Form I-131. No separate I-130 is needed, because the approved I-129F and the marriage to the petitioner are the basis. That stage, including the interview and conditional residence, is covered in [how K-1 adjustment of status works](/k-1-adjustment-of-status-after-marriage).

Two warnings for the gap between entry and adjustment. Do not leave the United States after the 90 days until advance parole is approved; the K-1 is a single-entry visa, and a departing spouse may not be able to return. And file the I-485 as soon as the marriage certificate is issued: K-1 status ends when the 90 days do, and while a marriage to the petitioner after that point still supports adjustment, the delay must be explained.
