Where your spouse is standing on the day you file decides more than any other fact in a spousal green card case. A spouse in the United States after a lawful admission can usually adjust status here. A spouse abroad goes through consular processing and arrives as a permanent resident. Some couples have a real choice between the two, and for them the question of adjustment of status or consular processing comes down to four things: where the spouse wants to wait, how soon they need to work, how much travel the family needs, and whether an old immigration problem makes one route unavailable.

## Adjustment of status or consular processing: how each route runs

Adjustment of status is a single case with USCIS. The citizen spouse files Form I-130, and the foreign spouse files Form I-485 at the same time, with Form I-765 for a work permit and Form I-131 for advance parole. The spouse stays in the United States, attends biometrics and an interview at the local field office, and receives the green card by mail. Our step-by-step post on [marriage-based adjustment of status](/marriage-based-adjustment-of-status-steps) walks through the forms.

Consular processing is two agencies in sequence. USCIS approves the I-130, then sends it to the National Visa Center, which collects fees, the DS-260 immigrant visa application, civil documents and the Form I-864 affidavit of support. The NVC schedules an interview at the U.S. embassy or consulate in the spouse's country. If the visa is issued, the spouse enters the United States as a permanent resident and the physical card follows. Our [marriage-based green card](/practice-area/key-services/marriage-based-green-card) practice handles both tracks and often runs them in parallel for couples who are not yet sure where they will be.

## When the choice is made for you

Several facts remove the choice entirely.

A spouse who entered the United States without inspection cannot adjust status, even when married to a citizen, unless a petition or labor certification filed on or before April 30, 2001 protects them under section 245(i). That spouse goes through the consulate, usually with a Form I-601A waiver decided first; see [how the I-601A provisional waiver works](/i-601a-provisional-waiver-explained).

A spouse of a permanent resident, in the F2A category, can adjust only when the priority date is current and only if they have kept lawful status. A spouse who fell out of status waits for consular processing or for the petitioner to naturalize. The two-chart system that governs the wait is explained in [priority date vs filing date](/priority-date-vs-filing-date).

A spouse who is physically outside the United States has no adjustment option. Entering on a visitor visa in order to adjust risks a misrepresentation finding; the Department of State's 90-day rule treats a marriage or filing soon after a visitor entry as presumptive evidence of a misrepresented intent, and USCIS examines the same facts.

## Comparing the two when you do have a choice

Work authorization. An adjusting spouse cannot work until the work permit arrives, and the wait depends on current USCIS processing. A consular spouse is authorized to work the day they enter as a resident; the endorsed immigrant visa in the passport serves as proof of residence for a year while the card is produced.

Travel. An adjusting spouse is grounded until advance parole is approved, and even then travel is a risk if the trip overlaps an interview notice. A consular spouse waits abroad, can travel freely while waiting, and may visit the United States on a valid visitor visa only if they will actually return home for the interview.

Being together. Adjustment keeps the couple together in the United States during the wait. Consular processing means separation, or the citizen spouse living abroad for the duration, unless the couple already lives abroad together.

Timing. Neither route is reliably faster. Adjustment depends on the field office; consular cases depend on USCIS, the NVC and the consulate's interview capacity. Check the USCIS processing times for the I-130 and I-485 and the NVC's published timeframes for the current picture.

Cost and risk of error. Adjustment involves more forms and fees at once. Consular processing has a harder stop: a problem the consular officer finds at the interview leaves the spouse outside the United States while it is fixed, and a consular refusal has no formal appeal in the way a USCIS denial does.

## The CR-1 and IR-1 distinction

A consular spouse receives either a CR-1 or an IR-1 immigrant visa. If the marriage is less than two years old on the day of entry, the visa is a CR-1 and the green card is conditional, with Form I-751 due in the 90 days before the two-year card expires. If the marriage is two years old or more at entry, the visa is an IR-1 and the card is valid for ten years. An adjusting spouse faces the same two-year test, measured on the day the I-485 is approved. For a couple whose second anniversary falls during the case, that timing can mean skipping the I-751 altogether.

## Couples who are not yet married

If you are engaged rather than married, the comparison changes. A U.S. citizen can bring a fiancé on a [K-1 fiancé visa](/practice-area/key-services/k-1-fiance-visa), marry here within 90 days, and then adjust status; or the couple can marry abroad and file for a CR-1. That decision has its own tradeoffs, covered in [K-1 or CR-1](/k-1-vs-cr-1-spousal-visa). Married couples with a spouse abroad should start the I-130 now in either case; the petition is the same whichever route finishes the case.
