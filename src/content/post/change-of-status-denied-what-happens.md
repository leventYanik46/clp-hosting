If USCIS denies your change of status after your I-94 date has passed, you are out of status on the day of the decision and unlawful presence starts counting that same day. That single fact drives everything that follows: the 180-day deadline that separates a clean departure from a three-year bar, the 30-day window for a motion, and the choice between fighting from inside the country and applying for a visa from outside it. This post explains why change of status applications are denied, what the denial triggers, and the options that remain.

## Why USCIS denies change and extension requests

The common grounds are predictable. The application was received after the I-94 date and no extraordinary circumstance excused it. The applicant had already broken status by working, studying, or overstaying, which makes the request ineligible under INA 248 regardless of its merits. The applicant belonged to a group that cannot change status at all, such as a Visa Waiver entrant or a J-1 subject to the two-year home residency rule. The evidence of funds or ties was thin. The new activity had already begun, which is the classic B-2 to F-1 denial. Or a request for evidence went unanswered. Our [change of status and extension](/practice-area/key-services/change-and-extension-of-status) team sees a further pattern in dependents whose I-539 was filed on a different schedule from the principal's I-129, so that the two decisions did not line up.

## The day of the denial: status and unlawful presence

Two situations need to be separated. If your original I-94 is still valid on the denial date, you keep your original status until that date and nothing has changed except the plan. If your I-94 has already expired, you were protected only by the pending application. The denial ends that protection: you are out of status immediately and unlawful presence accrues from the denial date forward. The days while the timely application was pending do not count. Our post on [unlawful presence versus being out of status](/unlawful-presence-vs-out-of-status) explains why those two labels matter separately.

## The 180-day clock and the visa in your passport

From the denial date, you have fewer than 180 days before a departure triggers the three-year bar under INA 212(a)(9)(B), and a full year of unlawful presence triggers the ten-year bar. Leaving promptly keeps both off the record. Remaining past the denial also brings INA 222(g) into play: a visa in your passport is treated as void once you have overstayed, and future visa applications must generally be made at a consulate in your country of nationality rather than wherever is convenient. USCIS may also issue a Notice to Appear after a denial that leaves you without status, which places the case before an immigration judge; if that happens, speak to our [removal defense](/practice-area/key-services/removal-defense) team before making any travel decision.

## Form I-290B: motions within 30 days

A denied Form I-539 cannot be appealed to the Administrative Appeals Office. What you can file is a motion to reopen, based on new facts supported by evidence, or a motion to reconsider, based on a legal or policy error in the decision, on Form I-290B within 30 days of the decision date (33 if the decision was mailed). The motion goes back to the office that decided the case. Two cautions: filing a motion does not restore your status or stop unlawful presence from accruing while it is pending, and a motion that simply resubmits the same evidence is denied. A denied Form I-129 is different, because the employer as petitioner may appeal to the AAO as well as file a motion. In rare cases involving a clear legal error, review under the Administrative Procedure Act in federal court is possible, and our [immigration appeals and federal court](/practice-area/key-services/immigration-appeals-delays-and-federal-court) page describes when that route is realistic.

## Refile, reinstate, or go to the consulate

If your I-94 is still valid, the simplest fix is often a new, complete application that cures the defect, filed before the date passes. F-1 and M-1 students who fell out of status may ask for reinstatement on Form I-539 if the violation was outside their control or a technical lapse, and they request it within five months of the violation. For everyone else, the alternative is consular processing: leave, apply for the visa in the new category at a U.S. consulate, and re-enter. The consular route has no maintenance-of-status requirement, so a status violation that doomed the change of status does not by itself prevent a visa, but the applicant faces the consular officer's intent review and any refusal under INA 214(b). Our post on [what to do when a visa application is denied](/my-visa-application-was-denied-what-should-i-do) covers that side. Visa Waiver entrants have only the consular route, as explained in our post on [changing status after a Visa Waiver entry](/change-status-after-visa-waiver-entry).

## Reducing the risk before the next filing

Most denials were foreseeable from the record. Before filing again, retrieve your I-94 history, confirm no work or study happened outside the terms of your status, assemble financial evidence with a paper trail, write a statement that explains the timing honestly, and file with enough margin that an RFE can be answered before anything expires. Where the facts are weak, choosing the consulate from the start is sometimes the better bet than filing an application that is likely to be denied and then leaving with a denial on file.
