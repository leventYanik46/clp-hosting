To become a U.S. citizen, you must show good moral character during the statutory period before you apply, usually five years, or three years if you are applying as the spouse of a U.S. citizen. Citizenship denied due to good moral character is one of the more common N-400 outcomes that applicants do not see coming, because the standard covers more than criminal history.

## What good moral character means

USCIS looks at your conduct during the statutory period and up to the oath ceremony. Some conduct is a permanent or automatic bar. Other conduct is weighed case by case. Officers can also consider conduct from before the statutory period if it bears on your present character.

USCIS guidance issued in 2025 directs officers to take a more holistic look, weighing positive attributes and contributions alongside any negative factors, rather than treating good moral character as simply the absence of misconduct.

## Permanent and automatic bars

- Murder convictions
- Aggravated felony convictions on or after November 29, 1990
- Certain other serious crimes during the statutory period
- Confinement in jail for 180 days or more during the statutory period
- False testimony under oath to obtain an immigration benefit
- Habitual drunkenness
- Certain controlled substance offenses

## Common reasons for discretionary denial

These are not automatic bars but often lead to denial:

- **Unpaid taxes or failure to file returns.** Evidence of a payment plan with the IRS and compliance can help.
- **Failure to pay court-ordered child support.**
- **DUI arrests,** especially more than one.
- **Unlawful voting or false claims to U.S. citizenship.**
- **Failure to register for Selective Service** for men who lived in the U.S. between ages 18 and 26, unless you can show it was not knowing or willful.
- **Lying on the N-400 or at the interview,** including omitting arrests that were dismissed or expunged. Expunged records still need to be disclosed.

## Will bankruptcy affect my citizenship application?

Filing for bankruptcy is not, by itself, a good moral character problem. It is a legal process available to anyone. Bankruptcy can become an issue only if there was fraud in the filing, such as hiding assets or lying under oath. If you filed for bankruptcy, list it accurately where asked and bring your discharge paperwork.

## Good moral character examples that help

Positive evidence can matter, especially when there is something negative to explain:

- Steady employment and tax filing history
- Caring for family members
- Volunteer and community work
- Rehabilitation after a past mistake, such as completed probation, treatment, or education
- Letters from employers, clergy, or community members

## Affidavit of good moral character

Some applicants submit sworn statements from people who know them, or their own declaration explaining an incident. These do not override a statutory bar, but they can help in discretionary cases.


## Frequently asked questions

### Do traffic tickets count?

Minor traffic tickets usually do not affect good moral character on their own, but tickets that are criminal offenses, such as reckless driving or driving without a license in some states, should be disclosed and reviewed.

### Can I reapply after a denial?

Yes. You can file a new N-400 after addressing the problem, or request a hearing on Form N-336 within 30 days of the denial.

### Does an old arrest outside the statutory period still matter?

It can. USCIS may consider conduct before the statutory period if it reflects on your present character, and some offenses are permanent bars. Disclose everything.

## What to do before you file

1. Get certified court dispositions for every arrest, ticket for a criminal offense, or charge, including dismissed cases.
2. Pull your IRS transcripts for the statutory period.
3. Confirm Selective Service registration if it applies to you.
4. Review your immigration history for any past misstatements.
