An O-1 visa lawyer builds the case that you have extraordinary ability in your field, arranges the required petitioner and consultation documents, and presents it all to USCIS in a form an officer can approve without having to guess. The cost of that work depends on how strong your record already is and how much of the petition has to be organized from scratch.

## What the O-1 visa is for

The O-1 is a nonimmigrant visa for people with extraordinary ability. O-1A covers sciences, education, business and athletics. O-1B covers the arts and, with a higher standard, motion picture and television. There is no annual cap and no lottery, which is why many founders, researchers, athletes and artists use it as an alternative to the H-1B.

An O-1 petition has to be filed by a U.S. employer, a U.S. agent, or a foreign employer through a U.S. agent. You cannot file for yourself, which is the part that surprises many applicants.

## What an O-1 attorney actually does

- **Maps your achievements to the criteria.** The O-1A asks for evidence in several categories, such as major awards, memberships, press coverage, judging the work of others, original contributions, scholarly authorship, a critical role at distinguished organizations, and high pay. Meeting enough of them is the starting point, not the end.
- **Finds the petitioner.** If you do not have a traditional employer, the lawyer helps structure an agent arrangement and the itinerary or contracts that show what you will be doing.
- **Gets the consultation letter.** O-1 petitions need a written advisory opinion from a relevant peer group or labor organization, or a statement that none exists.
- **Writes the legal argument.** Expert letters, a petition letter and an organized exhibit list that tie each piece of evidence to a regulatory criterion.
- **Handles RFEs and extensions.**

## What drives cost

Legal fees for an O-1 vary widely, and the same is true of what USCIS charges. The main variables:

1. **The strength of your record.** A person with strong media coverage and published work needs less assembly than someone building the case from partial evidence.
2. **The number of expert and recommendation letters** that have to be drafted, edited and collected.
3. **The petitioner structure.** An agent arrangement adds paperwork.
4. **Whether you add premium processing,** which is an optional USCIS fee.
5. **Dependents.** Spouses and children have their own applications under the O-3 category.

Ask how the lawyer prices the case: a flat fee, or a staged fee, with separate charges for letters or RFE responses. Ask what is not included before you sign.

## Questions to ask before hiring

- How many O-1 petitions has the attorney filed in my field?
- Who prepares the first draft, and how involved will I be?
- How will the agent or petitioner be structured if I have no employer?
- What does the fee cover if USCIS issues an RFE?

## Is an O-1 right for you

The O-1 allows you to hold a long-term immigration plan alongside it, unlike some other visas. It can also be a bridge to an EB-1A or national interest waiver green card. If you are early in your career or your evidence is thin, an honest attorney will tell you so, and suggest how to strengthen the record first. A consultation is the cheapest way to find that out.
