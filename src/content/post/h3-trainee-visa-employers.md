The H-3 visa lets a U.S. company or organization bring someone to the U.S. to receive training that isn't available in their home country. It is not a work visa in the usual sense. The trainee must be in the U.S. primarily to learn, and the training must prepare them for a career outside the U.S.

## When the H-3 visa fits

The H-3 is designed for structured training programs. Typical examples include:

- A multinational bank training staff from its foreign branches in U.S. compliance systems
- A manufacturer training foreign technicians on equipment they will operate abroad
- A hospital group training administrators on management systems before they return to run a facility overseas

The H-3 isn't for on-the-job work disguised as training, for graduate medical education, or for training in skills that are readily available in the trainee's home country.

## The requirements

The petitioning employer must show:

1. **The training isn't available in the trainee's home country.**
2. **The trainee won't be placed in a position that is in the normal operation of the business** and in which U.S. workers are regularly employed.
3. **The trainee won't engage in productive employment** unless it is incidental and necessary to the training.
4. **The training will benefit the trainee in pursuing a career outside the U.S.**

## The written training program

The H-3 petition needs a detailed, written training program. USCIS looks for:

- The type of training and supervision
- The structure of the program, including the number of hours spent in classroom instruction versus on-the-job training
- A description of the career abroad for which the training prepares the trainee
- Why the training can't be obtained in the home country
- Why the employer is providing the training and what it gains

Programs that are vague, too similar to a regular job, or lacking a fixed schedule tend to be denied. Programs with clear goals, assessments, and a defined endpoint have better results.

## Limits on productive work

Productive work is the biggest risk in H-3 cases. Some hands-on practice is normal and allowed when it is part of learning. But if the trainee spends most of their time doing the work a regular employee would do, USCIS may conclude the H-3 is a cover for employment.

As a rule of thumb, describe on-the-job components as supervised practice tied to specific learning outcomes, and keep the percentage of hands-on time reasonable relative to classroom and observation time.

## Length of stay

H-3 trainees can stay for up to two years. A special category for training in the education of children with physical, mental, or emotional disabilities allows up to 18 months and is capped at 50 participants per year.

After reaching the maximum stay, the trainee generally must remain outside the U.S. for six months before returning in H or L status.

## Filing the petition

The employer files Form I-129 with USCIS. Once approved, the trainee applies for an H-3 visa at a U.S. consulate. Premium processing is available. Spouses and children can accompany the trainee in H-4 status, but they can't work.

## H-3 vs other options

- **J-1 trainee or intern** programs, run through designated sponsors, can be an alternative for structured training, especially for younger participants.
- **B-1** allows short meetings and some training-related activities without compensation from a U.S. source, but not extended training programs.
- **L-1B** fits employees with specialized knowledge who will actually work in the U.S., not just train.

## Common mistakes

- Submitting a job description instead of a training plan
- Training in skills that are widely available abroad
- Using the H-3 to staff a U.S. operation
- Failing to explain the career the trainee will return to

## Building a training program that holds up

The strongest H-3 petitions read like a curriculum. Break the program into phases with dates, list the subjects or systems covered in each phase, name the instructors or supervisors by role, and describe how the trainee's progress will be assessed. Show the proportion of time spent in classroom instruction, observation, and supervised practice. Explain what the trainee will do afterward abroad, ideally with a letter from the foreign employer describing the role waiting for them.

It also helps to explain why the employer is willing to invest in this training. A company that needs foreign branch staff to apply U.S. methods consistently has a clear business reason. A company that simply needs extra hands does not, and USCIS will see the difference.

## H-3 and later immigration plans

The H-3 requires that the trainee intend to use the training abroad, and USCIS considers whether the employer is training someone to work in the U.S. afterward. A trainee who switches to H-1B or files for a green card shortly after an H-3 stay can face questions about whether the training plan was genuine. Plan any later U.S. role carefully and with a clear gap in purpose.
