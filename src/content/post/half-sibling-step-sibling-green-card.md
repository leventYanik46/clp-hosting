Yes. The Immigration and Nationality Act does not require two shared parents for a sibling petition. It requires that you and your brother or sister each once qualified as the "child" of the same parent. That single test covers half-siblings, step-siblings and adopted siblings, and it is why a U.S. citizen can file an F4 petition for a half-brother she has never lived with. The test also explains where these cases fail: the shared parent must be a parent under immigration law, with the proof to match. CLP's [brother and sister green card](/practice-area/key-services/sibling-green-card) practice handles each of these variations; here is how the rules work.

## The common-parent test

Section 203(a)(4) of the INA gives the fourth family preference to brothers and sisters of U.S. citizens who are at least 21. The statute does not define sibling, so the agencies and the Board of Immigration Appeals borrow the definition of "child" in section 101(b)(1): two people are siblings if each of them was, at some point, the child of a common parent under that section. It does not matter that both are now adults, married, or living on different continents. What matters is that the parental link to the shared parent was a qualifying one for each of you when you were under 21. Our step-by-step guide on [how to file an I-130 for a brother or sister](/how-to-file-i-130-for-sibling) covers the filing itself; this post is about the relationship.

## Half-siblings: one shared parent

A half-sibling green card case is routine when the shared parent is the mother. Two birth certificates naming the same woman prove the relationship, and USCIS needs nothing more about the parental link.

When the shared parent is the father, each of you must qualify as his child. A child born in wedlock to him qualifies automatically, so include his marriage certificate to each mother and the divorce or death records showing the marriages ended in sequence. A child born out of wedlock qualifies only if the father legitimated the child before 18, or if a bona fide father-child relationship existed before the child turned 21: financial support, shared residence, correspondence, the father's name on school or medical records. If either of you was born out of wedlock and that evidence is thin, the petition stalls. DNA testing between the two siblings can prove a shared father when documents cannot, and consulates sometimes suggest it on their own.

## Step-siblings: the before-18 marriage rule

Step-siblings share no blood at all. The link is a marriage: your parent married your step-sibling's parent, which made the step-sibling your parent's stepchild. Under section 101(b)(1)(B), a stepchild counts as a child only if the marriage took place before the stepchild turned 18. So a step-sibling green card petition works when the wedding happened before your step-sibling's 18th birthday (the petitioner's age at the wedding is irrelevant, because the petitioner is the natural child of the shared parent). The evidence is the parents' marriage certificate, proof that each parent's prior marriages ended, and both birth certificates.

If the parents later divorced, or the shared parent has died, the step relationship does not necessarily end. The BIA has recognized that a step-sibling relationship can continue where the family relationship in fact continued, and USCIS looks at the actual history. Gather proof of ongoing contact before filing. The same rule governs stepchild petitions, which we cover in our post on [sponsoring a stepchild for a green card](/stepchild-green-card-requirements).

## Adopted siblings

An adopted sibling qualifies if the adoption met the rules for an adopted child: adoption finalized before the child turned 16 (or before 18 for the natural sibling of a child adopted under 16 by the same parents), plus two years of legal custody and two years of joint residence with the adoptive parent. The decree, a custody order and residence records such as school enrollment or leases make the case.

Adoption also changes the biological side of the family. The statute strips the natural parents of immigration benefits through an adopted child, and petitions between an adopted child and biological siblings raise questions that deserve a careful look before anyone files.

## Evidence problems and how to fix them

- Names that differ across certificates: add marriage certificates, court orders or sworn explanations, and ask the registry for a corrected record if one is available.
- A birth certificate registered years late: pair it with secondary evidence made at the time (baptismal, school or medical records) and a non-availability statement if the original registry has no entry.
- No father listed on a birth certificate: legitimation records, a paternity acknowledgment, relationship evidence, or DNA testing.
- Multiple marriages by the shared parent: every divorce decree and death certificate, in order, so USCIS can see that each marriage was valid.
- Records from countries where the State Department's Reciprocity Schedule says certificates are unreliable: expect closer scrutiny and front-load the secondary evidence.

## What is the same for every sibling

Once the relationship is established, half, step and adopted siblings are treated identically. The petition is an F4 case with a priority date, your sibling's spouse and unmarried children under 21 are derivatives, and the family waits for the Visa Bulletin like everyone else in the category.
