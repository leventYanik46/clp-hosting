Most family-based green card applicants need a sponsor to file Form I-864, the Affidavit of Support. It's a legally binding promise that the sponsor will financially support the immigrant so they don't rely on certain public benefits. The form has strict income requirements tied to the federal poverty guidelines, and errors are a common cause of delays.

## Who must file Form I-864

- **The petitioner** in family-based cases, such as a U.S. citizen sponsoring a spouse or parent
- **Employers who are relatives,** or relatives with a significant ownership interest, in some employment-based cases
- **Joint sponsors,** when the petitioner's income isn't enough

Some immigrants are exempt, such as those who have earned or can be credited with 40 quarters of work in the U.S., and children who will become citizens automatically on arrival.

## The income requirement

The sponsor's income must be at least 125% of the federal poverty guidelines for their household size. The threshold is 100% for active-duty members of the U.S. armed forces sponsoring a spouse or child.

The Department of Health and Human Services updates the poverty guidelines each year, and USCIS publishes Form I-864P with the current figures. Always use the current I-864P chart. The figures are higher for Alaska and Hawaii.

## Counting household size

Household size includes:

- The sponsor
- The sponsor's spouse
- The sponsor's dependent children
- Other dependents listed on the sponsor's tax return
- The immigrants being sponsored on this affidavit
- Immigrants the sponsor previously sponsored whose obligations haven't ended

Many sponsors undercount household size by forgetting previously sponsored immigrants or tax dependents.

## How income is shown

Income is shown with:

- The most recent federal tax return or IRS transcript
- Current employment evidence, such as pay stubs or an employer letter
- W-2s and 1099s

USCIS generally uses the most recent tax year's income, but current income can count if it has changed.

## Using household members' income

Household members who live with the sponsor, or who are listed as dependents, can add their income by signing Form I-864A. The intending immigrant's own income can count in some cases, especially if it will continue from the same source after they get the green card.

## Using assets

If income falls short, assets can make up the difference. The value of assets must generally equal:

- **Five times** the shortfall for most cases
- **Three times** the shortfall when sponsoring a spouse or child (age 18 or older) of a U.S. citizen
- **One time** the shortfall when sponsoring an orphan to be adopted in the U.S.

Assets must be convertible to cash within one year. A primary residence usually doesn't count unless the sponsor can show equity beyond what the mortgage covers, and many officers are cautious about counting home equity.

## Joint sponsors

If the petitioner can't meet the requirement, a joint sponsor can file a separate I-864. The joint sponsor must be a U.S. citizen, green card holder, or U.S. national, at least 18, living in the U.S., and meet the 125% requirement on their own household size.

## Common mistakes

1. Using an outdated poverty guideline chart
2. Undercounting household size
3. Missing tax returns or transcripts
4. Not signing the form or using an unsigned I-864A
5. Joint sponsors who live outside the U.S.
6. Not including evidence of current employment

## When the sponsor lives abroad

The sponsor must be domiciled in the U.S. A U.S. citizen living abroad can still sponsor a relative if they can show they intend to reestablish domicile in the U.S. no later than the date the immigrant arrives, for example by showing a job offer, a lease, or steps taken to move. Without that evidence, the sponsor will need a joint sponsor who lives in the U.S.

## Updating the I-864 before the interview

If the sponsor's job or income changes after filing, bring updated evidence to the interview, such as a recent employment letter and pay stubs. Officers can ask for a new I-864 if the original no longer reflects the sponsor's situation.
