When a person dies because of someone else's negligence, the law recognizes two separate injuries. The family lost a spouse, parent, or child, along with the income and support that person provided. The deceased person, before dying, suffered pain, fear, medical expenses, and lost wages. A wrongful death claim addresses the first injury. A survival action addresses the second. The distinction between wrongful death vs survival action decides what damages are available, who receives them, whether creditors can reach them, and, in Virginia, whether the second claim exists at all.

## Two injuries, two claims

The wrongful death claim is the family's claim. It did not exist at common law and is created by statute in every state. It compensates the statutory beneficiaries for what they lost when the deceased died: financial support, services, companionship, and funeral expenses, with the exact list depending on the state. The personal representative files it, but the money belongs to the beneficiaries.

The survival action is the deceased's own claim. If the person had lived, they could have sued for their injuries. Survival statutes keep that claim alive after death and let the estate pursue it. It compensates for what the deceased experienced between the injury and death: conscious pain and suffering, medical bills, and lost earnings during that period. The proceeds go to the estate, pass under the will or intestacy, and are available to the estate's creditors.

## What a survival action covers

The survival claim is strongest when the deceased lived for a period after the injury and was conscious. A person who survived a crash for three weeks in intensive care has a substantial survival claim for pain, suffering, and the medical expenses of that period. A person who died instantly has little or none, because there was no conscious suffering to compensate. Evidence of pre-impact terror, the seconds in which the person saw the collision coming, is recognized in some jurisdictions as a component of the survival claim, and the time between injury and death is documented through medical records, witness accounts, and first responder reports.

## What a wrongful death claim covers

The wrongful death claim looks forward from the death rather than backward from the injury. Its components are the financial support the deceased would have provided over a lifetime, the value of services such as childcare and household work, funeral and burial costs, and, depending on the state, the family's grief and loss of companionship. Our post on [what damages a family can recover in a wrongful death case](/wrongful-death-damages-explained) breaks these down. The claim exists whether the death was instant or delayed.

## How Virginia, DC, and New York treat the two claims

The three jurisdictions where we practice handle the relationship between the claims differently, and the difference is significant.

- Virginia merges them. When an injury results in death, the claim must be brought as a wrongful death action. The deceased's own pain and suffering before death is not a separate recoverable item. Virginia compensates for this partly by allowing broad non-economic damages to the beneficiaries, including sorrow and mental anguish, but the survival component for the deceased's suffering is not available as it is elsewhere.
- The District of Columbia keeps them separate. The Survival Act allows the estate to recover for the deceased's pain and suffering, pre-death medical expenses, and lost earnings. The Wrongful Death Act allows the family to recover its financial losses. Both are usually pleaded in the same lawsuit.
- New York keeps them separate as well. The survival claim for conscious pain and suffering belongs to the estate. The wrongful death claim compensates the distributees for pecuniary injuries. As of this writing, New York does not allow recovery for the family's grief in a wrongful death claim, though the legislature has debated changing that repeatedly, so the current rule should be confirmed at the time of any claim.

## Why the distinction changes who gets the money

Because the two claims have different owners, a settlement must be allocated between them, and the allocation changes where the money goes. The survival portion enters the estate, where it is subject to the deceased's debts, estate administration costs, and the terms of the will. The wrongful death portion generally passes directly to the statutory beneficiaries and is typically protected from the deceased's creditors. A deceased person with significant medical debt or a will that leaves everything to one child creates very different outcomes depending on how a settlement is divided. Courts in Virginia and New York must approve the allocation, and defense insurers, who do not care how the money is split, will usually agree to whatever division the family's counsel proposes within reason. Getting that division right is one of the most consequential decisions in the case. Our post on [who is allowed to file a wrongful death claim](/who-can-file-wrongful-death-claim) explains the beneficiaries on the wrongful death side.

## Practical implications for the family

If your family member survived for a time after the injury, preserve everything that documents that period: hospital records, nursing notes, photographs, and the recollections of people who visited. If the death was immediate, the case will focus on the wrongful death claim and on the evidence of what the deceased contributed to the family. In either case, the general [personal injury](/practice-area/personal-injury-law) principles of proving negligence apply to the underlying conduct, and the two-year wrongful death deadline runs from the date of death in all three jurisdictions.
