You do not always need a lawyer to apply for U.S. citizenship, and many people file the N-400 on their own without trouble. But some situations carry real risk, because an error on a naturalization application can lead to a denial, and in serious cases it can expose a person to removal. Knowing which category you fall into is the first decision.

## Basic eligibility for naturalization

In general, you must:

- Be at least 18 years old
- Have been a lawful permanent resident for five years, or three years if you have been married to and living with a U.S. citizen spouse for that period and your spouse has been a citizen for the same time
- Have lived continuously in the United States for the required period, and been physically present for at least half of it
- Have lived in your state or USCIS district for the required time before filing
- Show good moral character
- Pass an English test and a civics test, unless you qualify for an exception
- Demonstrate attachment to the principles of the Constitution and take an oath of allegiance

Some applicants qualify under different rules, such as certain military service members.

## The naturalization process

1. **File Form N-400,** with the required documents and fee.
2. **Attend biometrics** for fingerprints and background checks.
3. **Interview and test.** An officer reviews your application, tests your English and civics, and asks about your history.
4. **Receive a decision.** USCIS may approve, deny, or request more evidence.
5. **Oath ceremony.** You become a citizen when you take the oath.

## How long does the naturalization process take

USCIS publishes citizenship application processing times by field office, and they change with workload. The timeline from filing to oath can take several months or longer. After approval, the oath ceremony may be scheduled quickly or take more time. Check the current estimates for your local office rather than relying on a past figure.

## When you may not need a lawyer

If your history is simple, such as a clean record, no long absences, consistent tax filing and no previous immigration problems, and you are comfortable with the forms and the interview, you may be able to file alone. There are also nonprofit organizations that help with straightforward applications at low or no cost.

## When a lawyer is worth hiring

Consider a naturalization attorney if you have any of these:

- **Arrests or convictions,** including old, dismissed, expunged or minor ones
- **Long trips abroad,** especially an absence of six months or more
- **A prior removal order or immigration court history**
- **A previous denial** of citizenship or another application
- **Tax problems,** unfiled returns, or unpaid support obligations
- **A claim to U.S. citizenship or voting** on a form or in an election in the past
- **Questions about how you got your green card,** such as past marriages or any fraud concerns
- **Failure to register for Selective Service,** if it applied to you
- **Medical conditions or disabilities** that may call for an exception to the tests
- **Name or identity discrepancies**

An attorney can spot an eligibility problem before you file, tell you when to wait, prepare you for the interview and respond to a denial if one comes.

## Questions to ask

- Am I eligible now, or should I wait?
- Will my history create an issue?
- What happens if USCIS denies my application?
- What does the fee include?
