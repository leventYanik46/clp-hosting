A lower quoted rent can cost more than a higher one, depending on the lease structure behind it. In a triple net lease the tenant pays its share of property taxes, building insurance, and common area maintenance on top of base rent, and those pass-throughs rise whenever the landlord's costs do. In a full-service gross lease, one rent covers everything and the landlord carries the cost risk. The triple net lease vs gross lease question is really a question about who absorbs increases over the term, and most leases land somewhere between the two.

## What a triple net lease shifts to the tenant

The three "nets" are real estate taxes, property insurance, and operating expenses, usually called common area maintenance or CAM. The tenant pays its pro rata share, calculated from the tenant's square footage divided by the building's rentable area. The landlord estimates the year's expenses in advance, bills them monthly alongside base rent, and reconciles against actual costs after year end, sending the tenant either a bill for the shortfall or a credit for the overpayment.

In a single-tenant building, the lease is often "absolute net," meaning the tenant also handles roof, structure, and capital repairs directly. In a multi-tenant building the landlord performs the work and passes the cost through. Either way, the tenant's total occupancy cost is base rent plus a number that only the landlord controls, which is why the definition of operating expenses is the most heavily negotiated section of a net lease. ## How a gross lease works

In a full-service gross lease the landlord pays taxes, insurance, maintenance, utilities, and janitorial services, and the rent is set high enough to cover them with a margin. The tenant gets predictability; the landlord gets a premium for taking the risk. Most gross leases in office buildings are not fully gross, though. They include a base year clause: the landlord absorbs expenses at the level of the first year of the term, and the tenant pays its share of any increase above that base year in later years.

Base year mechanics deserve attention. If the building is partially vacant in the base year, expenses are artificially low, and every later year looks like an increase. A gross-up clause fixes this by adjusting variable expenses to what they would have been at a stated occupancy level, typically near full, in both the base year and later years. A tenant whose lease starts mid-year should also ask that the base year be a full calendar year of normal operation.

## Modified gross and the middle ground

A modified gross lease splits the categories. The landlord might cover taxes and insurance while the tenant pays its own utilities and janitorial costs, or the tenant pays increases in taxes and insurance over a base year while the landlord handles maintenance outright. Industrial leases often lean net; office leases often lean gross with a base year; retail leases are usually net with additional charges for marketing or promotional funds. Rent quoted "plus electric" or "plus utilities" is a modified gross structure, and the lease should spell out exactly which items the tenant pays and how they are measured, whether by submeter, by survey, or by allocation.

## What to negotiate in the pass-through language

Whatever the structure, the tenant's protection lives in a handful of clauses that limit what can be charged and let the tenant check the math:

- a definition of operating expenses that excludes capital improvements (except cost-saving items amortized over their useful life), the landlord's leasing commissions and legal fees, costs reimbursed by insurance or other tenants, and penalties or fines caused by the landlord;
- a cap on increases in controllable expenses, with taxes, insurance, and utilities carved out as uncontrollable;
- a limit on the management fee, stated as a percentage of gross revenue or a fixed amount;
- an audit right with a reasonable window after the annual reconciliation, with the landlord paying the audit cost if the overcharge exceeds a stated threshold;
- a gross-up clause for variable expenses in any year the building is below the agreed occupancy level.

These are routine requests. A landlord that refuses all of them is telling you something about how the reconciliation will go. ## Which structure fits your business

A net lease suits a tenant that wants a lower base rent, can budget for variable charges, and has the staff to review reconciliations. A gross lease suits a tenant that values a fixed number and is willing to pay a premium for it. A single-tenant occupant of a freestanding building will almost always face a net lease and should treat the condition of the roof, HVAC, and parking lot as part of the rent.
