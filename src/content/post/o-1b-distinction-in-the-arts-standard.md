For an O-1B petition in the arts, extraordinary ability means distinction, and distinction is defined in 8 CFR 214.2(o)(3)(ii) as a high level of achievement evidenced by a degree of skill and recognition substantially above that ordinarily encountered, to the extent that the person is prominent, renowned, leading or well known in the field. That is a real standard, but it is a lower one than the O-1A, which asks scientists, executives and athletes to show they are among the small percentage at the very top of their field. Understanding what distinction in the arts requires, and what it does not, is the first decision in any artist's case.

## Distinction in the arts versus extraordinary ability in science

The regulations draw the line deliberately. Recognition in a creative field does not look like recognition in a laboratory: there are no citation counts, and the institutions that confer standing are festivals, critics, venues and publications. So the regulatory definition for science, education, business and athletics requires a person at the very top of the field, while the definition for the arts requires distinction. An O-1B artist does not need to be a household name. The artist needs to show that people in the field, and the outlets and institutions that matter in it, regard them as prominent. Our [O-1B visa for artists and entertainers](/practice-area/key-services/o-1b-visa) page sets out both O-1B standards; the [O-1A visa](/practice-area/key-services/o-1a-visa) page shows how much higher the science and business bar sits. One caution: the arts standard is lower only for the arts. Film and television professionals are judged under a separate and stricter "extraordinary achievement" test, which is explained in [O-1B for film and television work](/o-1b-film-television-standard).

## Who counts as working in the arts

The regulation defines the arts as any field of creative activity or endeavor, and names fine arts, visual arts, culinary arts and performing arts as examples without limiting the list. It also reaches beyond performers and principal creators to essential persons, listing directors, set designers, lighting designers, sound designers, choreographers, choreologists, conductors, orchestrators, coaches, arrangers, musical supervisors, costume designers, makeup artists, flight masters, stage technicians and animal trainers. Because the list is open-ended, chefs, fashion designers, illustrators, DJs, tattoo artists and game artists can fit when the work is creative and the recognition is documented. The question USCIS asks is whether the proposed U.S. work is in the area of your distinction. A singer coming to act, or a designer coming to manage a retail business, breaks that link and invites denial.

## How USCIS tests distinction: the award or the three criteria

The test has two steps. First, you show either a nomination for or receipt of a significant national or international award (the regulation names the Academy Award, the Emmy, the Grammy and the Director's Guild Award as examples) or at least three of six criteria:

- A lead or starring role in productions or events with a distinguished reputation.
- National or international recognition shown by published material by or about you in major newspapers, trade journals, magazines or other publications.
- A lead, starring or critical role for organizations and establishments with a distinguished reputation.
- A record of major commercial or critically acclaimed success, such as box office receipts, ratings or standing in the field.
- Significant recognition from organizations, critics, government agencies or other recognized experts, in testimonials that establish the writer's authority.
- A high salary or other substantial remuneration compared with others in the field.

Second, the officer reviews the record as a whole to decide whether it shows distinction. Three criteria met thinly can still fail that second look, and the general framework for this two-step review is described in [O-1 visa: proving extraordinary ability to USCIS](/o-1-visa-extraordinary-ability). For the arts, and only the arts, the regulation also allows comparable evidence when a criterion does not readily apply to your occupation; a muralist has no box office, so the petition can substitute commissions and public installations. [O-1B evidence checklist for musicians and actors](/o-1b-evidence-checklist-musicians-actors) lists the documents that satisfy each criterion.

## Where artists fall short

Most unsuccessful O-1B arts petitions share the same gaps. Recognition is local: strong reviews in one city, no national or trade press. The press is self-generated: a blog post, a press release or an interview the artist arranged, with no independent outlet. The productions are real but their reputation is never documented, so a lead role proves nothing to an officer who has never heard of the theater. Testimonials praise the artist but say nothing about the writer's own standing, so the letter cannot establish authority. Pay evidence shows a good income without any comparison to the field. Each of these is fixable with time and effort, but none can be fixed by adjectives in the petition letter.

## If you are not there yet

Distinction can be built. Artists who are a year or two away from the standard usually need independent press, documented lead roles at venues or productions with a reputation that can be proven, and one or two testimonials from critics or institutions. Meanwhile other classifications may fit: a group touring together may qualify for the P-1 under the lower "internationally recognized" test described on our [P-1 visa](/practice-area/key-services/p-1-visa) page, and a culturally unique program may fit the P-3. Neither is a shortcut to an O-1B, but both can bring you to the United States while the record grows.
