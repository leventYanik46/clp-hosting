Cancellation of removal is a form of relief only an immigration judge can grant, and it is available only to people already in removal proceedings. You cannot apply for it at USCIS, and you cannot apply for it before DHS files a Notice to Appear. If you qualify, the judge cancels the removal order and, for non-permanent residents, adjusts your status to lawful permanent resident. If you fall short on any single element, the judge has no power to grant it. That is why the first job in any case where cancellation is the plan is to test each requirement against your actual record before pleading at the master calendar hearing.

## Two versions of cancellation of removal

The Immigration and Nationality Act creates two separate forms of cancellation of removal, and they have little in common beyond the name.

The first, under INA 240A(a), is for lawful permanent residents who have become removable, usually because of a criminal conviction. The second, under INA 240A(b), is for people who have never had a green card and have lived in the United States for a long time without status. The application forms are different too: Form EOIR-42A for permanent residents and Form EOIR-42B for everyone else. Both are filed with the immigration court, with the filing fee paid to USCIS and biometrics taken before the hearing.

Our [removal defense practice](/practice-area/key-services/removal-defense) sees many people assume they qualify for the ten-year version because they have been here more than a decade. The time requirement is the easy part. The hardship requirement is where most cases are decided.

## Requirements for non-permanent residents

To win cancellation under 240A(b), you must prove four things:

- Ten years of continuous physical presence in the United States immediately before the application.
- Good moral character during those ten years.
- No conviction for an offense listed in INA 212(a)(2), 237(a)(2) or 237(a)(3), which together cover most crimes involving moral turpitude, controlled substance offenses, firearms offenses and document fraud.
- Exceptional and extremely unusual hardship to your spouse, parent or child who is a U.S. citizen or lawful permanent resident.

Your own hardship does not count. A qualifying relative must exist at the time of the decision, and a child who turns 21 before the judge rules stops being a qualifying child. The burden is on you for every element, and the judge weighs discretion on top of eligibility.

### How the ten years are counted

Continuous physical presence is measured backward from the date the clock stops, not from the date of your hearing. Two events stop the clock: service of a Notice to Appear that states the time and place of the hearing, or commission of one of the listed offenses. Under the Supreme Court's decisions in *Pereira v. Sessions* and *Niz-Chavez v. Garland*, an NTA that leaves out the hearing time and place does not stop the clock, and a later hearing notice does not fix it. Many people whose NTAs were issued before those decisions have more qualifying time than they realize.

Absences also matter. A single trip abroad of more than 90 days, or trips that add up to more than 180 days during the ten-year period, break continuous presence. Short trips do not, but you will need to document them.

### What exceptional and extremely unusual hardship means

This is a higher standard than the extreme hardship used for waivers. The Board of Immigration Appeals has described it as hardship substantially beyond what a family ordinarily suffers when a parent or spouse is removed. Lower income, a worse school system or sadness at separation are the ordinary consequences of removal, and they rarely carry the case alone. Judges look at the whole picture: a child's serious medical condition that cannot be treated in your country, a relative's disability that depends on your daily care, the number and ages of the qualifying relatives, conditions in the country of removal, and the family's financial dependence on you. The strongest applications pair medical and psychological evaluations with school records, financial records and country-conditions evidence.

## Requirements for permanent residents

Cancellation under 240A(a) has three elements: you were lawfully admitted for permanent residence for at least five years, you have resided continuously in the United States for seven years after being admitted in any status, and you have not been convicted of an aggravated felony. The stop-time rule applies to the seven years in the same way it applies to the ten-year version. There is no hardship requirement. Instead the judge balances positive factors (family ties, length of residence, employment, service, rehabilitation) against negative ones (the seriousness of the criminal record and any immigration violations). A permanent resident can receive this relief only once in a lifetime.

Because the aggravated felony bar and the stop-time rule both turn on the exact statute of conviction, the analysis overlaps heavily with [what we call crimmigration](/what-is-crimmigration): the elements of the state offense, not the facts of what happened, decide whether the conviction disqualifies you.

## Who is barred regardless of time

Some people cannot receive cancellation of removal no matter how long they have been here. The bars include persecutors of others, people who entered as crewmen or in certain J-1 categories, people who received cancellation or suspension of deportation before, and people who were ordered removed in absentia and failed to appear without exceptional circumstances within the past ten years. If any of these might apply, say so at the consultation. Discovering a bar after a year of preparing hardship evidence is a costly mistake.

## The annual cap and what happens after a grant

Congress limits the number of cancellation grants for non-permanent residents each fiscal year. When the cap is reached, judges who are ready to grant relief reserve their decisions and the case waits for a number in a later year. The wait does not hurt your eligibility, but it means you may remain in proceedings with work authorization for some time after the hearing itself.

When relief is granted, the court issues an order and you become a lawful permanent resident as of that date. If cancellation is denied, you can appeal to the Board within 30 days, and our [BIA appeals and motions practice](/practice-area/key-services/bia-appeals-and-motions) handles those appeals. The hardship finding in particular is now reviewable in federal court as a mixed question of law and fact, so a denial is not necessarily the end.
