Most adults who say they have an estate plan own one document: a will. A will matters, but it does nothing while you are alive, and it does not control the retirement accounts and life insurance that often make up the bulk of what you leave behind. A complete set of estate planning documents covers three different situations: your death, a period of incapacity, and the transfer of assets that pass outside a will entirely. This checklist explains what belongs in the set, what each piece does, and the gap you leave when one is missing.

## The estate planning documents checklist

The list below is the baseline we build for most individuals and couples through our [estate planning practice](/practice-area/estate-planning). Not everyone needs every item, but everyone should know why they are skipping one.

- Last will and testament
- Revocable living trust (optional, paired with a pour-over will)
- Durable power of attorney for finances
- Advance medical directive or health care proxy
- HIPAA authorization
- Beneficiary designations on retirement accounts, life insurance, and payable-on-death accounts
- Guardian nomination for minor children
- Letter of instruction and digital asset inventory

## Documents that take effect at death

### Last will and testament

A will names the person who will administer your estate (the executor, called a personal representative in Virginia and DC), says who receives property that passes through probate, and nominates a guardian for minor children. It must be signed with the formalities your state requires. Virginia, DC, and New York all require two witnesses for a typed will, and most states, including Virginia and New York, let the witnesses sign a self-proving affidavit before a notary so they do not have to appear in court later. A will that fails these formalities is treated as if it never existed, which is why we do not recommend handwritten or downloaded forms. Our note on [what makes a will valid](/what-makes-a-will-valid-in-texas-what-are-the-requirements) covers the execution rules in one state, and the logic is similar elsewhere.

### Revocable living trust and pour-over will

A revocable trust holds title to assets during your life and distributes them at death without a court proceeding. It is optional. It earns its cost when you own real estate in more than one state, value privacy, or want a successor trustee to step in during incapacity without a court order. If you use one, you still need a short pour-over will that sweeps anything left outside the trust into it. A trust that is signed but never funded, meaning the assets were never retitled, is the most common failure we see in plans drafted elsewhere.

## Documents that work while you are alive

### Durable power of attorney for finances

This document names an agent to handle money and property if you cannot: paying bills, managing accounts, dealing with insurers, signing tax returns, selling a house. "Durable" means it survives your incapacity. Without one, your family must petition a court for a conservatorship or guardianship of the estate, which is slower, public, and supervised. Our [conservatorship](/practice-area/key-services/conservatorship) page explains what that process involves, and it is the outcome a good power of attorney is designed to avoid. Banks and brokerages scrutinize these documents, so the form should be current and, in New York, must be both notarized and witnessed.

### Advance medical directive and health care proxy

Each of the firm's jurisdictions uses a slightly different form. Virginia combines a health care agent appointment and treatment instructions in an advance medical directive. New York uses a health care proxy to name the agent and a separate living will for instructions. DC uses a durable power of attorney for health care. Whatever the label, the document names who speaks for you when you cannot and records what you want about life-prolonging treatment. We explain the differences between the financial and medical documents in [power of attorney vs health care directive](/power-of-attorney-vs-health-care-directive).

### HIPAA authorization

A short release lets named people obtain your medical records and speak to providers. Health care agents usually have this authority once they are acting, but a standalone authorization avoids arguments with a hospital at the worst possible moment and lets you include people, such as adult children, who are not your agent.

## The assets your will does not control

Retirement accounts, life insurance, annuities, and payable-on-death or transfer-on-death accounts pass by the beneficiary form on file with the institution, not by your will. The same is true for jointly owned property with a right of survivorship. These forms are estate planning documents even though no lawyer drafts them, and they are the ones most often out of date. A will that leaves everything equally to three children does nothing if the largest account still names one child, or an ex-spouse. See [do beneficiary designations override your will](/beneficiary-designations-override-will) for the mistakes we fix most often.

## Supporting documents worth adding

A guardian nomination for minor children usually sits inside the will, but a separate standby or temporary nomination can help if you travel often or if the children might need immediate care before a will is admitted. The [legal guardianship](/practice-area/key-services/legal-guardianship) process still runs through the court, but the court gives real weight to a parent's written choice.

A letter of instruction is not a legal document. It lists accounts, passwords, advisers, insurance policies, the location of originals, and funeral wishes. Pair it with an inventory of digital assets and the authority for your fiduciaries to access them, which Virginia, DC, and New York each provide for by statute when the plan documents grant it.

## How the documents fit together

The will handles probate assets and names fiduciaries. The trust, if you have one, holds the assets you want to keep out of court. The powers of attorney cover the years before death. Beneficiary forms move the accounts the will cannot reach. When these pieces are drafted at the same time, by the same person, they agree with each other. When they are collected over twenty years from different sources, they contradict each other, and the contradiction usually surfaces after you can no longer fix it.
