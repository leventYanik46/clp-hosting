An L-1B petition needs documents that prove three things: that the U.S. and foreign companies are related through ownership and control, that the employee worked for the foreign company for one continuous year in the last three, and that the employee has specialized knowledge the U.S. role will use. Most L-1B Requests for Evidence come from gaps in one of those three areas, not from the forms themselves. Gathering the right L-1B petition documents before filing saves weeks, and it gives the support letter something concrete to cite. The checklist below follows the order our [L-1B specialized knowledge visa](/practice-area/key-services/l-1b-visa) team uses to build an exhibit list.

## L-1B corporate relationship documents

USCIS needs to see that the U.S. petitioner is a parent, branch, subsidiary or affiliate of the foreign employer, and that both entities are doing business. Ownership must be traced, not just asserted. A group with several layers of holding companies needs every link documented.

- Articles of incorporation or organization for both entities, and any amendments.
- Share registers, stock certificates, operating agreements or membership ledgers showing who owns what.
- An ownership chart that traces control from the foreign employer to the U.S. petitioner, with percentages.
- Recent tax returns or financial statements for both entities.
- Proof of active business, such as contracts, invoices, bank statements, a lease and payroll records.

If the U.S. entity has been doing business for less than a year, the petition is a new office case and needs more: a lease for physical premises, a business plan and evidence that the parent can fund the operation. Our post on [new office L-1A petitions](/new-office-l-1a-petition-requirements) covers that evidence, and much of it applies to new office L-1B filings too.

## Proof of the one year abroad

The employee must have worked for the foreign qualifying entity for at least one continuous year within the three years before filing. Part-time work generally does not count toward the year, and time the employee spent in the United States does not count toward it either, although it does not break the three-year window.

Gather an employment verification letter from the foreign company on letterhead with exact start dates and titles, payroll records or pay slips for the qualifying period, and the foreign entity's organizational chart showing where the employee sat. If the employee changed roles during the year, document each role. Mistakes in counting the year are a common and avoidable reason for denials.

## Specialized knowledge evidence

This is the heart of the petition, and the part where generic documents hurt more than they help. USCIS must see how the employee's knowledge differs from that of others in the company or the industry. Our article on [what counts as specialized knowledge](/l-1b-specialized-knowledge-explained) explains the legal standard; the evidence that supports it usually includes:

- A detailed support letter from the employer describing the knowledge, how it was gained, and how the U.S. role will apply it.
- Internal training records with course names, dates and hours.
- A list of projects, products, systems or client deployments the employee worked on, with dates.
- Technical documents, manuals, patents or internal guides the employee wrote or maintains.
- A comparison showing how many employees perform similar work and why this employee's knowledge is different.

Keep confidential material in proportion. USCIS does not need source code or trade secrets; a description of the proprietary system, what it does, and how long it takes to learn is usually enough. We draft this section around the company's real products rather than industry language.

## The U.S. position and third-party placement

Describe the U.S. job in specific terms: duties, the percentage of time on each, the team, the reporting line, the salary and the worksite. If the employee will work primarily at an unaffiliated client's site, add the client contract, the statement of work and a letter showing that the petitioner, not the client, controls and supervises the work. Without that, USCIS may see an arrangement to provide labor for hire, which the L-1B rules prohibit.

## Forms, fees and consular items

The petition itself is Form I-129 with the L Classification Supplement, filed with the current USCIS filing fee plus the fraud prevention and detection fee and any other fees that apply to the employer; check the USCIS fee schedule before filing. Larger employers with many H-1B and L-1 workers may owe an additional statutory fee. Form I-907 is added if the company requests premium processing.

After approval, an employee abroad applies for the visa at a U.S. consulate with the DS-160 confirmation, a valid passport, the I-797 approval notice and a copy of the petition. Canadian citizens can present the approved petition at a port of entry. Dependents applying for L-2 status need marriage and birth certificates, with certified translations where needed. Employees already in the United States in another status can request a change of status in the same filing. If the transfer could instead run under a [blanket L petition](/blanket-l-petition-explained), the document set shifts to Form I-129S and the consular interview.

## Final checks before filing

Read the support letter, the organizational charts and the employment letter side by side. Dates, titles and reporting lines must match across every document. Inconsistencies between what the U.S. company says and what the foreign company says are a red flag for an officer, even when the underlying facts are fine.
