A sibling petition routinely waits more than a decade, and petitioners do not always outlive the wait. When the U.S. citizen who filed Form I-130 for a brother or sister dies, the regulations treat the petition as automatically revoked. That is the starting point, not the end. Two tools can save the case: section 204(l) of the Immigration and Nationality Act, for beneficiaries living in the United States, and humanitarian reinstatement, for an approved petition whose beneficiary is abroad. Both require a substitute sponsor. CLP's [sibling green card](/practice-area/key-services/sibling-green-card) team has handled these requests; here is how each route works and what to do in the first months after a death.

## What automatic revocation means

Under the regulations at 8 CFR 205.1, an approved family petition is revoked automatically, as of the original approval date, when the petitioner dies before the beneficiary receives a visa or adjusts status. The National Visa Center will not schedule an interview, and a consulate will not issue the visa, on a revoked petition. If the petition was still pending at USCIS when the petitioner died, USCIS will deny it unless section 204(l) applies. Humanitarian reinstatement is available only for a petition that had already been approved. So the first question after a death is: was the I-130 approved, and where does the beneficiary live?

## Route one: section 204(l) for relatives living in the United States

Congress added section 204(l) in 2009. It lets USCIS approve a pending petition, or reinstate an approved one, as if the petitioner had not died, when the principal beneficiary or at least one derivative beneficiary was residing in the United States on the date of the death and continues to reside here. Residence means the person's actual principal home, not a visit. The provision covers the beneficiary and the derivatives together, so if your sibling is abroad but your sibling's spouse lives in New York, the spouse's residence can carry the whole family's petition.

Section 204(l) is not fully automatic. USCIS may still deny relief if it finds that approval would not be in the public interest, and the beneficiary still has to be admissible and otherwise eligible for the green card. In practice, the request is made in writing to the USCIS office handling the case, with proof of the death and of the qualifying relative's U.S. residence, and with a substitute sponsor's Form I-864. If the beneficiary is in the United States in a status that permits adjustment, the next step is Form I-485; our guide to [Form I-485, step by step](/what-is-form-i-485-how-to-complete-step-by-step) covers that filing.

## Route two: humanitarian reinstatement for a sibling abroad

If nobody in the beneficiary's family lived in the United States when the petitioner died, the remaining option for an approved petition is humanitarian reinstatement. It is discretionary. You ask USCIS, in a written request to the office that approved the petition, to reinstate the approval for humanitarian reasons. As of this writing there is no form and no filing fee for the request, though you should confirm that on the USCIS page before sending anything.

USCIS weighs factors such as the disruption to an established family unit, hardship to U.S. citizens or permanent residents in the family, the beneficiary's advanced age or poor health, how long the beneficiary has lived in the United States if at all, the beneficiary's ties or lack of ties to the home country, and any undue delay by the government in processing the case. The request should tell the story with evidence: medical records, affidavits, proof of financial dependence, and anything showing that the family had organized its life around the petition. A granted request reinstates the petition with its original priority date, so a sibling who waited twelve years does not go to the back of the line. A denial cannot be appealed, though a new request with better evidence is possible. For context on how long these cases wait, see our post on [why the F4 priority date wait is so long](/f4-sibling-priority-date-wait).

## The substitute sponsor

Both routes require a substitute sponsor, because the original petitioner's Form I-864 died with the petitioner. Section 213A of the INA limits who may step in: the beneficiary's spouse, parent, mother-in-law or father-in-law, sibling, child who is at least 18, son or daughter, son-in-law or daughter-in-law, sister-in-law or brother-in-law, grandparent, grandchild, or legal guardian. The substitute must be a U.S. citizen or permanent resident, at least 18, domiciled in the United States, and able to show income at or above 125 percent of the Federal Poverty Guidelines for a household that includes the immigrating family. The sponsor files Form I-864 with proof of the qualifying relationship to the beneficiary and the petitioner's death certificate. Without a substitute sponsor, neither 204(l) nor humanitarian reinstatement can succeed, so identifying one is often the first practical step.

## Practical steps in the first months after a death

- Obtain several certified copies of the death certificate.
- Check where every beneficiary and derivative was living on the date of death. If anyone had a principal residence in the United States, section 204(l) is the stronger route.
- Identify a substitute sponsor from the statutory list and confirm their income and status documents.
- Gather hardship evidence for a humanitarian reinstatement request if 204(l) is unavailable.
- Consider whether another U.S. citizen relative could file a new petition. A new I-130 gets a new priority date and loses the years already waited, so it is a backstop, not a first choice. Our guide on [how to file an I-130 for a brother or sister](/how-to-file-i-130-for-sibling) explains what a fresh petition involves.
- Keep derivative children's Child Status Protection Act dates in view; a long reinstatement process does not pause their ages.
