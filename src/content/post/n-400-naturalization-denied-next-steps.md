A naturalization denial arrives as a written decision that names the reason, and the reason decides your next move. Some denials, such as a failed civics test, are fixed by retesting. Others, such as a finding that you broke continuous residence, set a date before which a new application is pointless. A few, such as a problem in how you obtained the green card, call for a removal defense review before you do anything else. This post explains why USCIS denies Form N-400, the 30-day window to request a hearing, and how to choose between a hearing, a federal petition, and a fresh application.

## Why USCIS denies naturalization applications

Most denials fall into a handful of categories. The officer may find that you failed the English or civics test twice. The officer may decide that a trip abroad of more than six months broke continuous residence, or that your total days outside the country left you short of the physical presence requirement. Good moral character denials cover a wide range: a conviction during the statutory period, unpaid taxes with no payment plan, unpaid court-ordered child support, false testimony under oath at the interview, unlawful voting, or a false claim to U.S. citizenship on an employment form. Men who lived in the United States between 18 and 26 and knowingly skipped Selective Service registration can be denied while the failure sits inside the statutory period. Finally, USCIS can deny because you missed the interview, ignored a request for evidence, or were never lawfully admitted as a permanent resident in the first place.

Our [citizenship and naturalization practice](/practice-area/key-services/citizenship-and-naturalization) sees the last category most often in cases where a marriage-based green card or an old employer petition had a defect nobody caught. That kind of denial is the one that needs the most care, because the same facts that sank the N-400 may put the green card itself at risk.

## Read the decision before you decide anything

The denial notice states the legal ground, the evidence the officer relied on, and your right to request a hearing. Check two things first. One, is the notice actually a denial, or did the officer continue the case and ask for more documents? A continuance is not a denial and simply needs a timely response. Two, which statutory period did the officer apply? If you filed under the three-year rule as the spouse of a U.S. citizen and the officer analyzed five years, the error itself is a ground for a hearing.

## Form N-336: the 30-day hearing request

You may ask USCIS to review the denial by filing Form N-336 within 30 days of the decision date, with the current USCIS filing fee. A different officer conducts the hearing, looks at the whole file again, and may consider new evidence. If the denial rested on the English or civics test, the hearing includes a new test. If it rested on continuous residence, bring the proof the first officer never saw: a lease or mortgage, pay records, tax transcripts showing resident returns, school enrollment for your children, and evidence that you did not work abroad.

Miss the 30 days and the denial becomes final. You can still file a new N-400, but you lose the chance to have the original decision reversed, and the record of the first denial travels with you.

## Federal court review after the hearing

If the N-336 hearing goes against you, INA 310(c) lets you petition the federal district court for your district. The court reviews the application de novo, meaning the judge decides your eligibility fresh rather than deferring to USCIS, and may hold a hearing with testimony. The regulations set a filing deadline of 120 days after the N-336 decision. This route makes sense when the dispute is about law or about how evidence was weighed, for example a continuous residence finding built on a single long trip that you can document as temporary. Our [immigration appeals and federal court](/practice-area/key-services/immigration-appeals-delays-and-federal-court) team handles these petitions alongside the separate remedy for cases USCIS never decides at all, which is a suit under INA 336(b) once 120 days have passed after the interview.

## When a new application beats an appeal

Sometimes the calendar matters more than the argument. If the denial was for a broken continuous residence, you become eligible again four years and one day after you returned from the trip that caused the break, or two years and one day under the three-year rule. Fighting that finding only pays if you have strong evidence the trip was temporary. If the denial was for a conviction or a failure to pay taxes, eligibility returns when the conduct falls outside the statutory period, though some bars are permanent, including an aggravated felony conviction entered after November 29, 1990. If you failed the test twice, you may simply refile once you are ready and take it again.

A new filing means a new fee and a new interview, so check the arithmetic on [continuous residence and physical presence](/continuous-residence-vs-physical-presence) before you file. Reapplying with the same gap produces the same denial.

## When a denial puts your green card at risk

Read the decision carefully if it says you were not lawfully admitted for permanent residence, that you misrepresented a fact in the green card process, or that a conviction makes you removable. A denial on those grounds can be followed by a Notice to Appear. Before refiling, obtain your full file through a FOIA request, have the conviction records reviewed under the rules explained in our post on [crimmigration](/what-is-crimmigration), and talk to our [removal defense](/practice-area/key-services/removal-defense) team about whether a second application is safe.
