The most common EB-1C denial reason is a finding that the U.S. job, the foreign job, or both are not primarily managerial or executive. That usually happens when the U.S. company is small and the record does not show who does the day-to-day work. The other frequent grounds are gaps in the ownership chain, doubts that the U.S. company has been doing business for a full year, and weak proof of ability to pay. Many of these cases involve employees whose L-1A was approved without trouble, which surprises clients. The I-140 is a fresh review, and USCIS does not have to follow its earlier L-1A decision.

## The U.S. role reads as operational

USCIS asks whether the manager spends most of the time managing or doing the work. In a U.S. office with three or four employees, the officer will look for evidence that someone else handles sales, customer service, bookkeeping and logistics. If the job description lists those tasks among the beneficiary's duties, or the organizational chart shows no one below the beneficiary who could do them, a Request for Evidence or a denial is likely.

The law gives some room. When staffing levels are used as a factor, USCIS must consider the reasonable needs of the organization in light of its overall purpose and stage of development. But that protection works only with evidence: contracts with outside accountants, logistics firms or sales agents, a breakdown of the beneficiary's time, and an explanation of how the business model works with a lean staff. ## The foreign role is not documented

The qualifying year abroad gets less attention from petitioners and more from USCIS than many expect. A one-paragraph description of the foreign job, without an organizational chart or proof of the people the beneficiary supervised, invites an RFE. So does a foreign year that falls outside the three-year window or that included long leave or part-time work. Rebuild the foreign record with the same detail as the U.S. one: a chart of the foreign department, subordinates with titles and education, and the decisions the beneficiary made.

## Corporate and financial gaps

These grounds are technical, and they are often easier to fix than the managerial issue.

- The ownership chain skips an intermediate company or relies on an unsigned share ledger.
- A restructuring or share sale changed the relationship before or during the case.
- The U.S. company was formed more than a year ago but had little real activity in its first months.
- The tax returns show a net income below the offered wage, and the company was not already paying the beneficiary that amount.

The response is complete documents, filed in the order USCIS asked for them. Our [EB-1C I-140 evidence checklist](/eb-1c-i-140-evidence-checklist) lists what each element requires.

## Answering an RFE or NOID

A Request for Evidence gives a fixed deadline stated in the notice. A Notice of Intent to Deny means the officer is inclined to deny and is giving the employer one chance to change that outcome. Treat both as an opportunity to rebuild the weak part of the record, not to repeat the original arguments.

Start by reading exactly what the officer found lacking, then answer each point in the order raised. Use documents first and explanations second. If the officer relied on a fact that is wrong, such as a misread chart or a miscounted employee, correct it directly with evidence. If the officer applied the wrong legal standard, say so, and cite the regulation.

## Options after an EB-1C denial

### Refile the I-140

Often the most practical route. There is no bar on refiling, and a new petition can fix the evidence problems the denial identified. The new petition gets a new priority date unless the prior one can be retained, and a denied I-140 generally does not preserve its date.

### Appeal or motion

The employer can file Form I-290B to appeal to the Administrative Appeals Office or to ask USCIS to reopen or reconsider. The filing deadline is generally 30 days from the decision. An appeal suits a case where USCIS misread the law. A motion to reopen suits a case where new evidence answers the officer's concern. Federal court review under the Administrative Procedure Act is sometimes available after the agency's decision is final, and our [immigration litigation](/practice-area/key-services/immigration-litigation) team handles those cases.

### Change routes

If the facts make the EB-1C a poor fit, a PERM-based EB-2 or EB-3 petition or an EB-2 national interest waiver may work. Our guide on [how to sponsor an employee for a green card](/how-to-sponsor-employee-green-card) explains the PERM route.

## What a denial means for current status

An I-140 denial does not end L-1A or H-1B status. If an I-485 was filed concurrently, it will be denied too, and the employee should rely on the underlying nonimmigrant status rather than the work permit or advance parole tied to the I-485. Check the status expiration date before any travel.
