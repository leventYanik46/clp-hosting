A conservatorship ends in one of four ways: the protected person regains capacity and the court restores their rights, the person dies, the estate is exhausted or small enough that the court closes it, or, for a minor, the child reaches adulthood. Separately, a conservator can be removed or replaced while the conservatorship continues, and the order itself can be expanded or narrowed as the person's condition changes. Each route runs through the court, and each finishes the same way: with a final accounting that the court approves before the conservator is discharged and the bond released. Here is how to end a conservatorship, how to change the conservator, and what the final account requires. The same rules apply in Virginia and Washington, DC, and to the equivalent guardian of the property in New York.

## Restoration of capacity: when the person can end a conservatorship

The person under conservatorship, or anyone acting on their behalf, can petition the court to end the order on the ground that the incapacity no longer exists. This happens more often than families expect: recovery from a stroke or brain injury, stabilization of a psychiatric condition, or a young adult who has matured into managing money. The court will want current medical evidence, typically a new evaluation, and will hold a hearing. It can restore all rights, or it can modify the order into a limited one that keeps the conservator in place for particular assets while returning day-to-day control to the person.

The person has the right to counsel in this proceeding, and the conservator has a duty to cooperate rather than defend their own position. A conservator who resists a well-founded restoration petition invites questions about whose interest they are serving. ## Death of the protected person

When the person dies, the conservator's authority to manage assets ends, but the duty to account does not. The conservator preserves the assets, files a final account covering the period through the date of death, and transfers what remains to the personal representative of the estate once one is appointed. If the conservator is also named executor in the will, the two roles are kept separate on paper: the conservatorship closes with its own account, and the estate opens with its own inventory. Probate administration from that point is described on our [wills, trusts, and probate page](/practice-area/key-services/wills-trusts-probate-administration).

## Removing or replacing a conservator

A conservator can be removed for cause, and interested parties, including the person, relatives, and the court itself, can initiate that. The usual grounds are failure to file the inventory or accountings, commingling or self-dealing, an unapproved sale or gift, neglect of the person's needs, a conflict of interest that emerged after appointment, or the conservator's own incapacity or relocation. The process starts with a petition or a show-cause order, followed by a hearing at which the conservator must answer. If removed, the conservator still files a final account, and the court can surcharge them for any loss and pursue the bond. The accounting failures that most often trigger removal are described in [what a conservator's annual accounting must show](/conservator-annual-accounting-requirements).

A conservator can also leave voluntarily. Resignation requires court approval, and the court will not accept it until a successor is ready to qualify, so a conservator who wants out should propose a replacement in the same petition. Moving away, illness, or a job that no longer allows the time are all reasons courts accept. The successor's appointment follows the same priority and suitability analysis as the original one, outlined in [how to become conservator of a parent's finances](/how-to-become-conservator-for-parent).

## Modifying the order

Conditions change, and the order should change with them. A conservator can ask the court for additional powers when a new need arises, such as authority to sell the house to fund care. The person or a relative can ask the court to narrow the order when the person's abilities improve. Either side can ask the court to add or remove a [guardianship over personal decisions](/practice-area/key-services/legal-guardianship) if the person's medical or living situation has shifted. Each request is a petition with notice to the interested parties, and the court applies the same least-restrictive principle it applied at the start.

## The final accounting and discharge

Every ending passes through a final account. It runs from the close of the last approved annual account to the termination date, in the same format, with the same vouchers and statements, and it ends with a schedule showing where every remaining asset went: to the restored person, to the estate's personal representative, to the successor conservator, or to the adult who was a minor. The reviewer examines it as closely as any annual account, and more closely if the conservatorship is ending because of removal.

Once the final account is approved, the conservator asks the court for an order of discharge, which releases them from further liability for the administration and releases the surety on the bond. Do not skip this step. Without a discharge, the bond stays in force, the premium keeps coming due, and the conservator remains exposed to claims for years. In Virginia the commissioner of accounts' approval of the final account is the trigger for release; in DC and New York the court enters the order. Keep every record for several years after discharge regardless; beneficiaries and heirs sometimes raise questions long after the file is closed.
