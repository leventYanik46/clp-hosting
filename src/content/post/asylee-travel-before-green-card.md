Yes, an asylee can travel abroad before getting a green card, but only with a refugee travel document, and never to the country you fled without accepting a real risk to your status. Three rules explain nearly every travel problem we see at our [green card through asylum practice](/practice-area/key-services/green-card-through-asylum): which document you carry, where you go, and how the trip affects the one-year presence clock and a pending Form I-485. This post takes them in that order.

## The refugee travel document, Form I-131

A refugee travel document is the asylee's passport substitute. You apply for it on Form I-131 while you are in the United States, attend biometrics, and receive a booklet that most countries accept for entry under the same visa rules they apply to your nationality or stricter ones; check with the destination's consulate before you book. The document is valid for one year from issuance and cannot be extended, so a long trip needs a document issued close to the departure date. Apply well before travel; processing varies, and the USCIS processing-times page is the only reliable estimate. Each derivative asylee, including children, needs their own document.

If you leave without one, USCIS can issue a refugee travel document to an asylee who is already abroad, but only within a year of departure, only at its discretion, and only after an explanation of why you did not apply first. Do not plan on it. Carry your asylum approval letter and I-94 record with the travel document, because an inspector at the port of entry may ask for both.

## Why using your home passport is a problem

Many asylees still hold a valid national passport, and some are tempted to renew it because it is easier to use. The problem is legal, not practical. To adjust status you must continue to meet the definition of a refugee, and USCIS and the courts treat obtaining or renewing a passport from the government you fled, and traveling on it, as evidence that you have placed yourself back under that government's protection. It is not an automatic bar; an asylee who renewed a passport only as proof of identity, and can show that, has an explanation. But it becomes a question at the I-485 stage, at the naturalization interview years later, and in any proceeding to terminate asylum, and every answer has to be consistent. Talk to counsel before renewing, and never use the passport to cross a border.

## Returning to the country you fled

A trip to your home country is the single most damaging thing an asylee can do before adjustment, and often after it. The reasoning is simple: you told the government you could not return safely, and then you returned. USCIS can issue a notice of intent to terminate asylum, call you back for an interview, and, if it terminates, place you in removal proceedings where you would need to prove the claim again under a harder standard. Entry records and passport stamps make the trip visible; so do social media posts.

There are rare cases where a return is defensible: a parent dying, with a short stay, precautions documented, and no contact with the persecutor. Even then the trip should be discussed with counsel before departure, and the reasons and precautions written down at the time rather than reconstructed years later. If the choice is between going and keeping your status, the status usually wins.

## How travel affects the one-year clock

Eligibility for the green card requires one year of physical presence after the grant. Days outside the United States do not count. A three-week trip in the first year moves your eligibility date three weeks past the anniversary of the grant; a six-month stay abroad moves it six months and invites questions about whether you have resettled elsewhere. Keep a log of every trip with dates and destinations, because the I-485 asks for it and USCIS compares it with the government's entry records. Our post on [when an asylee can apply for a green card](/when-can-asylee-apply-for-green-card) explains the counting; the [asylee I-485 document list](/asylee-i-485-documents-checklist) shows how the travel history is presented.

## Travel while the I-485 is pending

Travel on a valid refugee travel document does not abandon a pending asylee I-485; USCIS treats that document as the proper travel authorization for an asylee adjustment applicant, and advance parole is not the right tool, though some applicants file both out of caution. Four things can still go wrong. A biometrics appointment or interview scheduled while you are away is missed, and a missed appointment can be treated as abandonment of the application. The travel document expires while you are abroad, leaving you to seek a replacement from overseas. Your mail goes to an address no one is checking, and a request for evidence lapses. Or the trip itself is to a place that raises the questions above. Before any trip during a pending case, confirm no appointment is scheduled, file a change of address if needed, and plan to return before the document expires.

After the green card is approved, you will travel on it with a passport or refugee travel document, and the rules of [travel and reentry for permanent residents](/practice-area/key-services/travel-and-reentry-permits) apply. The trip home remains a sensitive topic even then, because a green card obtained through asylum can be questioned at naturalization if the trip suggests the original claim was not genuine. The safest sequence for most asylees is to adjust first, travel second, and leave the home country for after citizenship, if at all.
