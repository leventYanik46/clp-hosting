EB-5 source of funds evidence answers two questions, and investors often prepare for only one. The first is whether the money was earned lawfully: salary, profits, a property sale, a gift, an inheritance. The second is how that money traveled, account by account and wire by wire, from where it was earned to the new commercial enterprise. A clean lawful source with a broken path still draws a Request for Evidence. So does a perfect path that starts with an unexplained deposit. This post covers what USCIS expects for each common source and where files usually break.

## EB-5 lawful source and path of funds are separate questions

USCIS must be satisfied that the investor's capital was obtained through lawful means. In practice, the officer reads the record as a story: here is how the investor earned the money, here is where it was held, here is how it moved, and here is where it was invested. Every chapter of that story needs a document.

The lawful source half usually rests on tax returns, employment records, and transaction documents. The path half rests on bank statements and transfer records that connect each account to the next. Our [EB-5 petition](/practice-area/key-services/eb-5-green-card) team builds both halves together, because a document gathered for one often reveals a gap in the other.

## Evidence by type of funds

### Salary and accumulated savings

Expect to provide several years of personal tax returns from every country where you filed, along with employment letters, pay records, and bank statements showing savings building over time. The officer will compare your reported income with the amount you are investing. If a decade of modest salary does not plausibly add up to the investment, you will need another source to explain the difference.

### Business income and dividends

If the funds came from a company you own, provide the company's registration, ownership records, financial statements, tax filings, and records of the distributions or dividends paid to you. Officers want to see the business itself is legitimate and that the money left it in a documented way.

### Sale of real estate or shares

Provide the sale contract, closing statement, and proof that the proceeds reached your account. Officers often ask how you acquired the asset in the first place, so keep the original purchase documents and evidence of how that purchase was funded. A property bought decades ago with family money can need its own short history.

### Gifts and inheritances

A gift is acceptable, but USCIS looks through it to the donor. You will need a gift letter, proof of the relationship, and the donor's own lawful source evidence, such as tax returns and bank records showing the donor had the money. For an inheritance, provide the will or estate documents, probate or succession records, and proof of the transfer to you.

### Borrowed money

Loans raise their own questions about how the debt is secured and who is liable for it. The EB-5 regulations treat indebtedness secured by the investor's own assets as capital, while debt secured by the assets of the enterprise does not count. Before you borrow for an EB-5 investment, get advice on how USCIS currently treats the specific loan structure, and document the collateral and its value.

## Tracing money across banks, borders, and currencies

The path of funds is where international investors most often struggle. Money that moved from a foreign account to a relative's account, then through an exchange service, then to a U.S. account, can be entirely lawful. But each step needs a record: account statements on both ends, transfer confirmations, and an explanation of why the money moved that way.

Investors from countries with currency controls sometimes move funds in stages or through family members. That can work, but each person in the chain may need to show their own transfer records, and the amounts must reconcile. Cash deposits are the hardest to explain; avoid them where you can.

Every foreign-language document needs a complete English translation with the translator's certification of competence and accuracy. Incomplete or summary translations invite an RFE.

## Gaps that lead to RFEs and denials

The patterns we see most often:

- a large deposit with no matching source document
- tax returns that report less income than the investment requires, with no other explanation
- a gift with no evidence of the donor's own source
- a missing bank statement for one month in the middle of the path
- property sale proceeds that cannot be tied to the account they supposedly reached

Most of these are fixable before filing and expensive to fix after. If your records have gaps you cannot close, it may be better to restructure which funds you invest than to file and argue.

## Put it all in a summary

A good source-of-funds exhibit opens with a short written summary and a chart of the money's path, keyed to the exhibits that prove each step. That lets the officer check the story instead of reconstructing it.
