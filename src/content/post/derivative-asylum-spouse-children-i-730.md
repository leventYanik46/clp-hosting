A grant of asylum protects more than the person who applied. Your spouse and your unmarried children under 21 can receive derivative asylum, either by being included on your Form I-589 or, if they are abroad or were left off the application, through Form I-730, the Refugee/Asylee Relative Petition, filed within two years of your grant. The rules on who qualifies turn on dates: when the marriage happened, when the child was born or conceived, and when the I-589 was filed. Getting those dates wrong is the most common reason a family stays separated after a grant, which is why our [asylum and humanitarian protection group](/practice-area/key-services/asylum-and-humanitarian-protection) maps every family member at the first meeting rather than after the approval.

## Derivative asylum: who qualifies

Two relationships qualify. A spouse qualifies if the marriage was legally valid where it took place and existed on the date asylum was granted. A marriage entered after the grant does not make the new spouse a derivative; that spouse waits for a family-based petition once you hold a green card. A child qualifies if the child is unmarried, under 21, and the parent-child relationship existed when asylum was granted. Biological children, stepchildren where the marriage occurred before the child turned 18, and adopted children who meet the adoption rules all count. A child born after the grant can still qualify if the child was conceived before it.

The age cutoff has a built-in protection. Under the Child Status Protection Act, a child's age is frozen on the date the principal's I-589 was filed. A child who was 19 when you filed and turns 22 during a long wait still qualifies, as long as the child remains unmarried. Marriage, by contrast, ends eligibility for a derivative grant, so a child who is thinking of marrying before the family's status is resolved should talk to counsel first.

Derivatives cannot pass asylum on. A spouse who receives derivative asylum cannot file an I-730 for her own child from a prior relationship; that child must be your stepchild to qualify through you, or must wait for a family petition later.

## Including family on the I-589

Family members who are in the United States should be listed on your I-589 as dependents seeking asylum. They attend the interview, give biometrics, and receive asylee status the day you do. You can add a spouse or child to a pending application if the relationship arises after filing and before the decision, for example a marriage or a birth while the case waits. Tell your attorney immediately when that happens; a child born during the wait who is never added needs a separate I-730 later, and a spouse married during the wait but not added before the grant may be eligible only through the I-730 if the marriage preceded the grant.

Listing a family member on the I-589 as a dependent is different from listing them as a relative who is not applying. The form asks both questions, and the distinction decides whether the person gets status on approval.

## Form I-730 for family abroad or left off the application

Form I-730 is filed by you, the principal asylee, one petition per relative, within two years of the date asylum was granted. USCIS can extend the two years for humanitarian reasons, but the extension is discretionary and should never be the plan. There is no filing fee for the I-730. You may file it even after you adjust to permanent residence through asylum, but not after you naturalize; a citizen petitions through the family-based system instead.

The petition needs proof of your asylee status and proof of the relationship: marriage certificate, birth certificates, adoption decrees, and certified translations. When a document is unavailable, as it often is for people who fled, secondary evidence and an explanation are accepted, and DNA testing may be suggested for a parent-child relationship when records are thin.

For a relative abroad, an approved I-730 is sent to the consulate or USCIS office with jurisdiction, where the relative is interviewed, screened, and issued a travel document to enter the United States as a derivative asylee. The relative is subject to the same bars that apply to asylum itself, such as the persecutor bar and serious criminal grounds, but not to the public charge or entry document grounds. For a relative already in the United States, the approval itself confers derivative asylee status. The filing itself, document by document, is covered in our guide to [bringing your family with Form I-730](/i-730-asylee-relative-petition-family).

## What derivatives receive and what comes next

A derivative asylee holds asylee status in their own right. They can work, apply for a Social Security number, and travel on a refugee travel document. One year after their grant or admission, each derivative files a separate Form I-485 for a green card; the timing and documents are covered in our post on [when an asylee can apply for a green card](/when-can-asylee-apply-for-green-card) and on our [green card through asylum](/practice-area/key-services/green-card-through-asylum) page. A derivative who was admitted later than you will become eligible later than you, so families often adjust in stages.

Derivative status does not depend on your fear. If the relationship with you later ends through divorce, or a derivative wants to petition for their own spouse or child, the derivative can ask USCIS for a grant of asylum as a principal, nunc pro tunc, meaning dated back to the original grant. That request requires the derivative to establish their own claim, so it should be prepared with the same care as a first application.

## Common traps

- Marrying after the grant and expecting the spouse to qualify as a derivative. Only a marriage that predates the grant counts.
- Letting the two-year I-730 window pass while waiting for the green card. The windows run independently.
- Leaving a child off the I-589 because the child is in the home country and assuming the I-730 will be easy later; it is usually straightforward, but it requires documents that are easier to gather while the case is open.
- Inconsistent family details between the I-589, the I-730, and the relative's own interview. Every date and name must match.
- Travel by the relative to a third country during I-730 processing without checking whether the consulate can still reach them.

Family members who do not fit the derivative definition, such as parents, married children, and siblings, become reachable through [family-based immigration](/practice-area/key-services/family-based-immigration) once you hold a green card or citizenship, and the one-year rollback on an asylee green card shortens that wait.
