An F-1 student who takes an off-campus job without authorization is out of status the day the job starts, yet that job adds not one day of unlawful presence. A visitor who stays three weeks past the I-94 date accrues unlawful presence from the first morning. These are two separate ledgers with separate consequences, and immigration decisions go wrong when people treat them as one. This post explains the difference between unlawful presence and being out of status, when each clock starts, the 180-day and one-year bars, and the exceptions that keep some people safe.

## Out of status: breaking the terms of your admission

Every nonimmigrant admission comes with conditions: a class of admission, an end date or duration of status, and limits on work and study. You fall out of status when you break them. Staying past the I-94 date, working without authorization, dropping below a full course of study, enrolling in classes as a B-2 visitor, or an H-1B worker remaining after employment ends and the grace period passes are all status violations. The consequence is eligibility, not a bar: you generally cannot extend or change status, you cannot adjust status unless an exception such as immediate relative adjustment applies, and you are removable. A status violation can also be cured in some situations, for example through F-1 reinstatement or a nunc pro tunc request, which is one of the services on our [citizenship, status and travel](/practice-area/key-services/citizenship-status-and-travel) page.

## Unlawful presence: a count that bites when you leave

Unlawful presence is a defined term in INA 212(a)(9)(B). It counts days spent in the United States after the expiration of an authorized period of stay or without being admitted or paroled. Its penalty is a bar on returning: accrue more than 180 continuous days and then depart, and you are inadmissible for three years; accrue one year or more and depart, and the bar is ten years. The bars are triggered only by leaving, which is why someone who has accrued unlawful presence and has a path to a green card from inside the country is often told not to travel. A separate permanent bar under INA 212(a)(9)(C) applies to anyone who accrues more than one year of unlawful presence in total, leaves, and then enters or attempts to enter without inspection.

The two ledgers diverge on the start date. Unlawful presence begins the day after a date-certain I-94 expires. A status violation that happens while the I-94 is still valid, such as unauthorized work, does not start it.

## When unlawful presence does not accrue

Several groups are protected from the count even while out of status:

- Students and exchange visitors admitted for duration of status, who as of this writing accrue unlawful presence only from the day USCIS denies a benefit after finding a violation or an immigration judge orders removal, not from the day of the violation itself.
- Anyone with a timely filed, non-frivolous extension or change of status pending; the days while USCIS decides do not count, even after the I-94 date passes.
- Applicants with a pending asylum application, provided they do not work without authorization.
- Children under 18, whose days do not count until their eighteenth birthday.
- Certain VAWA self-petitioners, TPS beneficiaries and others covered by statute or policy.

The protection is narrow. A pending extension protects you from unlawful presence while pending, but it does not make a status violation disappear, and USCIS will deny the application if the violation shows up in the record. Our post on [I-94 expiration versus visa expiration](/i-94-expiration-vs-visa-expiration) covers how to find the date that starts the count.

## After a denial: where the two ledgers meet

The ledgers converge when an application is denied. If your I-94 expired while a timely Form I-539 was pending and USCIS denies it, you are out of status on the denial date and unlawful presence begins that same day. The days before the denial are not counted. From that point you have fewer than 180 days to leave before a departure triggers the three-year bar. Our post on [what happens if your change of status is denied](/change-of-status-denied-what-happens) walks through the choices, and our [change and extension of status](/practice-area/key-services/change-and-extension-of-status) page explains how to avoid the denial in the first place.

## Waivers and the advance parole exception

Two tools soften the bars. A waiver on Form I-601 is available to applicants who can show extreme hardship to a U.S. citizen or permanent resident spouse or parent, and the provisional version on Form I-601A lets some applicants get the waiver approved before leaving for a consular interview. The second tool is advance parole: under the Board of Immigration Appeals decision in Matter of Arrabally and Yerrabelly, an adjustment applicant who leaves on advance parole has not made the kind of departure that triggers the three-year and ten-year bars. Our post on [advance parole for pending green card applicants](/advance-parole-pending-green-card) explains the limits of that rule. Nothing in it helps with other inadmissibility grounds, and anyone with a criminal record or a prior removal order should have the whole history reviewed by our [removal defense](/practice-area/key-services/removal-defense) team before travel.

## Keeping both ledgers clean

Calendar the I-94 date. File extensions and changes before it, and file them completely, because a frivolous or incomplete filing does not stop the count. Do not work or study outside the terms of your status while an application is pending. If a denial arrives after the I-94 date, count 180 days from the denial and make the leave-or-stay decision with the bars in view. People in the ten-year-bar range who have a U.S. citizen spouse often have a workable path through a waiver, but only if nobody leaves the country before the plan is in place.
