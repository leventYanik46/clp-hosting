A federal judge can order a consulate to decide your visa application; no judge can order the consulate to approve it. That single distinction governs every mandamus case over 221(g) administrative processing, and it explains both why these suits work and why they sometimes end in a refusal rather than a visa in hand. If your interview ended with a 221(g) slip, your CEAC status has read "Refused" or "Administrative Processing" for months, and emails to the post produce the same form reply, this is what a lawsuit can and cannot do. Capitol Law Partners files these cases through its [immigration mandamus practice](/practice-area/key-services/immigration-mandamus), most often against the Secretary of State and the consular post.

## What 221(g) administrative processing actually is

Section 221(g) of the Immigration and Nationality Act tells a consular officer to refuse a visa when the application does not establish eligibility or when further information or processing is needed. In practice the officer uses it two ways. Sometimes the post needs a document from you, and the case resumes when you supply it. More often the application is held for security or advisory opinion review, inter-agency checks or a fraud unit referral, with no action required from you and no timeline given. The State Department's website calls this administrative processing. The consulate's records call it a refusal under 221(g). That dual label is the crux of the litigation.

## Why consular cases are harder than USCIS cases

Courts apply a doctrine called consular nonreviewability: they will not second-guess a consular officer's decision to grant or refuse a visa, and the Supreme Court has repeatedly declined to open that door. A mandamus plaintiff therefore cannot argue that the refusal was wrong. The argument available is narrower: the officer has a duty under the regulations to either issue or refuse the visa, a 221(g) hold is not a real decision, and the State Department has unreasonably delayed performing that duty.

The government's standard reply is that a 221(g) refusal is a decision, full stop, so there is nothing left to compel. Courts are divided on this. Some judges accept the government's position and dismiss; others look at the substance and hold that a case parked for review with the stated expectation of further action has not been decided. The split runs district by district and judge by judge, which is why the venue analysis in [which federal court hears your immigration case](/which-federal-court-hears-immigration-case) matters more in consular suits than anywhere else.

## Who sues and where

The visa applicant is abroad and the consulate is abroad, so the case is usually brought by the U.S. petitioner, whether a spouse, parent, employer or sponsoring company, together with the applicant. Defendants typically include the Secretary of State, the consular post's principal officer and sometimes the Secretary of Homeland Security where a USCIS step is involved. Because the State Department sits in Washington, the District of Columbia is the default venue, though a petitioner may also file where they live. The broader menu of federal remedies is described on our [immigration appeals, delays and federal court](/practice-area/key-services/immigration-appeals-delays-and-federal-court) page.

## What the court weighs

Judges measure the delay against the same unreasonable-delay factors used in USCIS cases: whether the agency has a rule of reason for its timing, whether Congress set a timetable, the human cost of waiting, the effect on other applicants if this case jumps ahead, and whether the agency has acted in bad faith. Consular cases carry an extra argument on both sides. The government says security checks cannot be rushed; the plaintiff says an indefinite hold with no updates is the absence of a rule of reason. Documented harm, such as a spouse separated from a U.S. citizen or a company unable to fill a role, strengthens the case. [How courts decide whether a delay is unreasonable](/how-courts-decide-uscis-delay-unreasonable) explains how each factor is argued.

## Realistic outcomes

Most consular mandamus cases that survive a motion to dismiss end with the post acting: the visa is issued, or a final refusal is entered under a specific ground such as 214(b) or an inadmissibility provision. The second outcome is not a failure of the lawsuit; it is what the lawsuit asked for. It also opens doors a hold does not. A final refusal on inadmissibility grounds can be addressed with a waiver, and a 214(b) refusal can be answered with a new application and better evidence of ties, as described in [what to do after a visa refusal](/after-your-visa-was-refused-f-1-visa-process). An indefinite hold offers neither path.

The risk is symmetrical. If the hold exists because the file has a problem, forcing a decision brings the refusal forward. We review the application and the interview before filing and tell clients when a quiet inquiry, a congressional request or a response to the post's document list is the better move.

## Before you file

- Confirm that every document the post requested has been delivered, with proof of delivery.
- Use the State Department's inquiry channels and a congressional office; the responses become exhibits showing you asked.
- Gather evidence of harm: separation, lost employment, medical or educational deadlines.
- Check the petitioner's own status and the petition's validity, since a lapsed petition ends the case.
- Decide the venue with counsel, because the court's treatment of the 221(g) question will shape the outcome.
