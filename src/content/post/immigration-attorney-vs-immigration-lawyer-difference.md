In the United States, people use "immigration attorney" and "immigration lawyer" interchangeably, and you can treat them as the same thing when you are looking for help. The distinction that matters is not the title. It is whether the person assisting you is actually authorized to give legal advice and represent you in immigration matters.

## The difference in the words

Technically, a lawyer is someone who has studied law, and an attorney, short for attorney-at-law, is a lawyer who is licensed to practice and can act for clients. In ordinary speech and in the legal profession, the words are used for the same people. A person who has passed a state bar exam, been admitted to the bar and is in good standing is both. Some people with law degrees who are not licensed are lawyers in the first sense but cannot represent clients.

So when you hire, ask the practical question: is this person licensed, and in good standing?

## Who may represent you in immigration matters

Only certain people can give legal advice and represent you before USCIS, immigration courts and consulates:

- **Licensed attorneys.** An attorney licensed in any U.S. state can practice federal immigration law in any state, because immigration law is federal.
- **Accredited representatives.** People who work for recognized nonprofit organizations and are accredited by the Department of Justice can represent clients in limited settings.

Others, such as notarios, visa consultants, immigration "assistants" and some form-preparation services, are not allowed to give legal advice. In many Latin American countries a notario is a legal professional, but in the United States a notary public has no authority to advise on immigration. Relying on one can damage a case.

## What immigration lawyers do

- **Assess your options.** They review your history and goals and explain which visas, green cards or defenses may apply.
- **Prepare filings.** They draft petitions, applications and supporting letters, collect evidence and make sure forms are accurate and consistent.
- **Represent you before agencies.** They file with USCIS, communicate with officers, respond to Requests for Evidence and attend interviews.
- **Handle consulate matters,** including visa interview preparation and refusals.
- **Defend you in immigration court,** including bond hearings, relief applications and appeals.
- **Advise on risk.** They tell you what can go wrong, such as past overstays, arrests or misrepresentation, before you file.
- **Plan long term.** They help sequence steps, such as moving from a work visa to a green card or to citizenship.
- **Support employers,** with sponsorship, compliance and workforce planning.

What a good immigration lawyer does not do is guarantee an outcome, which no one can honestly promise.

## How to check that someone is licensed

- **Search your state bar's lawyer directory** for the person's name and look at their status.
- **Check the Department of Justice list** of attorneys and accredited representatives, where applicable.
- **Ask for the bar number** and verify it yourself.
- **Be careful with promises** of guaranteed results, pressure to sign quickly or requests for cash only.
- **Ask for a written fee agreement.**

## Choosing the right lawyer

Experience in your type of case matters more than title. Ask how many cases like yours they have handled recently, who will be working on the file day to day, and how communication works. A first consultation is a chance to evaluate the person as well as the case.
