Following to join lets a spouse or child who did not travel with you immigrate later on your original case: no new petition, no new priority date, and no new wait in the Visa Bulletin beyond the one you already finished. It exists only in the family preference and employment-based categories, where the law treats a spouse and unmarried children under 21 as derivative beneficiaries. Immediate relative cases have no derivatives, so a U.S. citizen's new spouse cannot bring children this way. The test is deceptively simple and the mistakes are expensive. CLP's [family reunification](/practice-area/key-services/family-reunification) practice handles following-to-join cases from Form I-824 through the consular interview.

## Who can follow to join

Section 203(d) of the Immigration and Nationality Act gives derivative status to the spouse and unmarried children under 21 of a principal immigrant in any family preference category (F1, F2A, F2B, F3, F4), any employment-based category, and the diversity visa program. If the derivative immigrates with you or within six months of your admission, they are "accompanying." If they come later, they are "following to join," and the statute puts no outer time limit on it as long as you remain a permanent resident and the derivative still qualifies.

Who does not qualify: the spouse or child of an immediate relative. A citizen's spouse who leaves a child behind needs a separate I-130 for that child, filed by the citizen stepparent if the marriage predates the child's 18th birthday, or later by the spouse as a permanent resident in F2A. Our [child green card](/practice-area/key-services/child-green-card) page and our post on [how a U.S. citizen gets a green card for a child](/green-card-for-child-of-us-citizen) explain those routes.

## The relationship date test

The relationship must have existed on the date you became a permanent resident. If you married before your visa was issued or your Form I-485 was approved, your spouse can follow to join. If you married after, your spouse is not a derivative; you file an F2A petition and a new priority date starts. The same logic applies to children: a child born before you immigrated qualifies, and a child born after your admission is not a derivative and needs an F2A petition. There is a separate rule for a child born abroad to a returning permanent resident during a temporary trip; ask about it before assuming the child is stranded. A child must also be unmarried and under 21, adjusted for the Child Status Protection Act, when the visa is issued.

## How the case is opened: Form I-824 or the National Visa Center

The procedure depends on how you got your own green card. If you adjusted status in the United States, there is no consular file for your family to attach to. You file Form I-824, Application for Action on an Approved Application or Petition, asking USCIS to notify the National Visa Center and the consulate that your derivatives are following to join. USCIS sends the notification, the NVC opens a case for each family member, and the consular steps begin. If you received your immigrant visa abroad, the consular case already exists; you or your attorney contact the NVC with your case number and ask it to add the derivatives, and the I-824 is usually unnecessary.

If the derivative is already in the United States in a lawful status, they may be able to file Form I-485 directly with USCIS as a following-to-join derivative, with proof of your residence and of the relationship. For the embassy route, our post on [consular processing step by step](/consular-processing-immigrant-visa-steps) covers each stage.

## Documents and the affidavit of support

The derivative's file needs proof of your permanent residence (your green card, or the visa and admission stamp), the marriage certificate or the child's birth certificate, proof that the relationship predates your immigration, each derivative's DS-260, civil documents, police certificates for anyone 16 or older, a medical exam with a panel physician, and photographs.

The affidavit of support follows the original case. In a family preference case, the original petitioner (your relative who filed the I-130) must sign Form I-864 for each following-to-join derivative. If the derivative immigrates more than six months after you did, the petitioner files a new I-864 with current income rather than a copy of the old one, and a joint sponsor is possible if the petitioner's income is no longer enough. In an employment-based case, no affidavit of support is needed unless a relative of yours owns a significant share of the sponsoring company.

## CSPA, naturalization and other things that close the door

- A derivative child's age is protected by the Child Status Protection Act using your visa availability date and the time your petition was pending, and the child must take a formal step, such as the DS-260 or an I-824 filed on their behalf, within one year of that date. Do not let a following-to-join child wait years. Our post on [how the CSPA age calculation works](/cspa-age-out-calculation-explained) covers the formula.
- A child who marries before the visa is issued loses derivative status.
- Divorce ends a spouse's eligibility.
- Your own naturalization ends following to join entirely. Once you are a citizen, you file I-130 petitions instead. For a spouse and children under 21 that is good news, because they become immediate relatives with no wait. For an unmarried son or daughter over 21 it can be bad news, because they drop from derivative status into the F1 category. Time the N-400 with that in mind; our [citizenship and naturalization](/practice-area/key-services/citizenship-and-naturalization) page explains the options.
- Losing your own permanent residence, through abandonment or removal, ends it as well.
