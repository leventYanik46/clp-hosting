Personal injury damages are the money a court can award to put you, as nearly as money can, back where you were before someone else's negligence hurt you. The categories are consistent across Virginia, Washington, DC, and New York, but the rules about caps, consortium claims, and fault vary enough that the same injury can be worth very different amounts across the Potomac. This post explains what you can recover, what you must prove to get it, and why the number on a settlement check is smaller than the number on the agreement.

## Economic damages: the losses with receipts

Economic damages are the measurable financial costs of the injury. Medical expenses come first: the emergency room, imaging, surgery, physical therapy, prescriptions, and assistive devices. If treatment will continue, future medical costs are recoverable too, usually supported by a treating physician's plan or a life care planner. Lost wages cover the income you missed while you could not work. Loss of earning capacity covers the harder question of what you can no longer earn, which matters most for a tradesperson who cannot lift or a professional who cannot concentrate after a head injury. Property damage, mileage to appointments, and paid household help round out the category.

Each item needs documentation. Our [personal injury practice](/practice-area/personal-injury-law) asks clients to keep a running file of every bill, explanation of benefits, pay stub, and receipt from the day of the injury forward, because an adjuster will not pay for what cannot be shown.

## Non-economic damages: pain, suffering, and lost enjoyment

Non-economic damages compensate for harm that has no invoice: physical pain, mental anguish, disfigurement, inconvenience, and the loss of the ability to do things you valued, from running to playing with grandchildren. These are typically the largest component of a serious injury claim and the hardest to quantify. Juries weigh the severity of the injury, how long recovery took, whether the condition is permanent, and how credible the injured person is about daily effects. Medical records, a consistent treatment history, and testimony from people who know you all contribute.

One state-level difference matters here. Virginia does not recognize a spouse's claim for loss of consortium, the loss of companionship and support caused by an injury to the other spouse. The District of Columbia and New York do allow it. In those jurisdictions the uninjured spouse may have a separate claim.

## Punitive damages and statutory caps

Punitive damages punish rather than compensate, and they are available only for conduct well beyond ordinary carelessness: a drunk driver, a company that knew about a defect and shipped the product anyway, a landlord who ignored repeated warnings. All three jurisdictions reserve them for willful, wanton, or reckless conduct. Virginia caps punitive damages at a statutory amount, and it also caps total recovery in medical malpractice cases, with the cap adjusting over time. New York and DC do not impose a general cap on compensatory damages in injury cases. If a cap applies, it limits the ceiling regardless of how a jury values the harm.

## How fault rules and liens change the final number

Two things stand between a damages figure and the money you actually receive. The first is fault. In Virginia and DC, contributory negligence can eliminate recovery entirely if you were partly at fault; New York reduces the award by your percentage of fault instead. Proving the other side's negligence, which we cover in our post on [how to prove negligence in a personal injury claim](/how-to-prove-negligence-injury-claim), is therefore tied directly to the damages question.

The second is liens and reimbursement rights. Health insurers, Medicare, Medicaid, and employer-sponsored ERISA plans often have a right to be repaid from your settlement for the treatment they covered. Medical providers sometimes file liens of their own. These must be identified, verified, and negotiated before the case resolves, and an attorney who ignores them can leave a client owing money after the case ends.

- Expect the gross settlement to be reduced by attorney fees and case costs under the contingency agreement.
- Expect outstanding medical liens and insurer reimbursement claims to be paid from the proceeds.
- Expect the net figure, not the gross, to be what reaches your account.

A realistic conversation about these deductions should happen at the start of the case, not at the end.

## When the injury results in death

If the injured person dies, the damages framework changes entirely. The claim becomes a wrongful death action with its own categories, beneficiaries, and limits, which we explain in our post on [wrongful death damages](/wrongful-death-damages-explained).
