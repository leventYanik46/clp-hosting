A strong trademark is one that the USPTO will register without a fight and that a court will enforce against a wide range of imitators. Strength is not about how much you like the name or how well it describes what you sell. It is about distinctiveness: how far the name sits from the ordinary words a competitor would need to describe the same product. The further it sits, the stronger it is. Our [trademark law services for brand owners](/practice-area/key-services/trademark-law) start with this question because every later step, from clearance to enforcement, gets easier with a distinctive mark and harder with a descriptive one.

## The five levels of trademark strength

Courts and the USPTO sort marks into five categories along a spectrum. The categories matter because they decide whether a mark can be registered at all and how broad its protection will be.

- Fanciful marks are invented words with no meaning outside the brand. They are the strongest category and are registrable the day you file.
- Arbitrary marks are real words used for goods that have nothing to do with their meaning: a fruit name on computers, a planet name on candy. Also inherently distinctive and registrable.
- Suggestive marks hint at a quality of the goods but require imagination to connect the two. They are inherently distinctive and registrable, and they are where most well-designed brand names land.
- Descriptive marks immediately convey a quality, feature, function or purpose of the goods. They are not registrable on the Principal Register until they acquire distinctiveness through use, and they are weak even then.
- Generic terms name the product itself. They can never be trademarks, no matter how long or exclusively they are used.

The line between suggestive and descriptive is the one that matters in practice, and it is the one the USPTO draws most often.

## Why descriptive names cost more over time

A descriptive name feels efficient. It tells customers what you do and saves advertising. The costs come later and keep coming.

At the USPTO, a descriptive mark draws a Section 2(e)(1) refusal. Our post on [responding to a merely descriptive trademark refusal](/merely-descriptive-trademark-refusal-options) covers the options, and none of them is free: you argue the mark is suggestive, you prove acquired distinctiveness with years of use and sales evidence, or you settle for the Supplemental Register, which carries no presumption of validity.

In the market, a descriptive mark gets narrow protection. Because competitors are entitled to describe their own goods with ordinary words, a court will stop only close copies, and third parties using the same descriptive term for similar goods crowd the field. Your enforcement budget goes further with a strong mark and produces less with a weak one.

## A strong trademark also depends on the field

Distinctiveness is judged in relation to the goods and the market, not in the abstract. A word that is arbitrary for software may be descriptive for food. A suggestive mark in a field where dozens of businesses use the same root word is commercially weak even though it is conceptually fine, because consumers have learned to tell those businesses apart by small differences. Examining attorneys and courts both look at third-party use when they decide how much protection a mark deserves.

The opposite is also true. A mark that is conceptually ordinary can become commercially strong through heavy use and advertising, which is why some famous brands began as descriptive terms. That path works for companies with the budget to build recognition over years. It is a poor plan for a startup choosing a name this quarter.

## How to test a candidate name before you commit

Run each finalist through a short set of questions before anyone designs a logo. Does the name state what the product is? If so, it is generic and off the table. Does it describe an ingredient, function, quality or intended user? Would a competitor reasonably need those words to describe a similar product? Is it primarily a surname, or the name of the place where the goods are made? Does it take a mental step to get from the name to the product? If yes, you are likely in suggestive territory.

Then search. A distinctive name that someone else already uses for related goods is not available, and a [trademark clearance search](/practice-area/key-services/trademark-search-and-clearance) is the only way to know. Our guide to [how to search for a trademark](/how-to-search-for-a-trademark) explains what a self-search can find before you ask for a written opinion.

## The tradeoff founders actually face

Descriptive names market themselves and die at the trademark office. Fanciful names register easily but need advertising to mean anything. Suggestive marks sit in the middle and are usually the right answer for a business that wants both a recognizable name and a registrable one.

Two structures help when you want a descriptive message without a weak mark. Pair a distinctive house mark with a descriptive product name, so the house mark carries the legal protection and the product name does the explaining. Or put the descriptive message in a tagline and keep the brand name itself distinctive.
