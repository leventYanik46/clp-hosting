An H-2B denial can come from three places. DOL can deny the temporary labor certification, USCIS can deny the I-129 petition, and a consular officer can refuse an individual worker's visa. Delays come from the same places, usually as a Notice of Deficiency from DOL or a request for evidence from USCIS. Most problems trace back to a short list: a temporary need that is not proven, dates that do not match the employer's history, a job order with an error, or a petition filed after the cap filled. Our [H-2B visa practice](/practice-area/key-services/h-2b-visa) handles both prevention and response. Here is where H-2B cases go wrong and what you can do about it.

## At DOL: the Notice of Deficiency

DOL's National Processing Center reviews every ETA-9142B and job order before accepting it. If something is missing or inconsistent, it issues a Notice of Deficiency listing the problems and a short deadline to fix them. Failing to respond, or responding incompletely, leads to denial.

The most common triggers:

- A statement of temporary need that does not explain why the need is seasonal, peakload, intermittent, or one-time, or is not backed by payroll records
- More workers requested than the business history supports, or a number that jumps without explanation
- Job requirements, such as experience, that exceed what employers in the area normally require
- A wage offer that does not match the prevailing wage determination
- Missing recruiter agreements or disclosures
- Job order terms that leave out required assurances, or contradict the application

Temporary need causes the most trouble. Our article on [H-2B temporary need](/h-2b-temporary-need-types) explains what DOL expects to see for each type.

## After acceptance: recruitment problems

Once DOL accepts the application, the case can still fail at recruitment. Rejecting a qualified U.S. applicant without a lawful, job-related reason, failing to contact former U.S. workers, or filing an incomplete recruitment report can lead DOL to deny certification or certify fewer positions. A partial certification because U.S. workers were hired is not a penalty. A partial certification because the recruitment report did not account for applicants is a problem you created.

## At USCIS: requests for evidence and denials

USCIS takes a second look at temporary need, even after DOL has certified it. It may issue a request for evidence asking for more payroll history, contracts, or an explanation of dates. It also checks that the I-129 matches the certification: same employer, same dates, same number of workers, same worksite.

The cap creates a separate problem. A petition received after USCIS has reached the cap for that half is rejected, not decided, and the employer generally cannot use those numbers until the next half or the next fiscal year, unless supplemental visas become available. Our explainer on [how the H-2B cap works](/h-2b-visa-cap-explained) covers that timing.

## At the consulate: worker visa refusals

Even with an approved petition, each worker must qualify for the visa. Consular officers apply the presumption of immigrant intent under INA 214(b), so a worker who cannot show ties at home may be refused. Prior overstays, past unauthorized work, misrepresentation, and certain criminal history raise grounds of inadmissibility. A worker who paid a recruitment fee is a warning sign for the officer and a compliance problem for the employer. When the officer needs more information, the worker receives a 221(g) notice instead of a decision.

## Options after a denial

What you can do depends on who denied the case.

### A DOL denial

The employer can request administrative review or a de novo hearing before DOL's Board of Alien Labor Certification Appeals. The deadline is short, counted in business days, and stated in the decision, so read it the day it arrives. Refiling is sometimes faster, but the new application must still fall inside the 75-to-90-day window before a real date of need.

### A USCIS denial

The employer can file a motion to reopen or reconsider, or appeal to the Administrative Appeals Office, on Form I-290B. Given the seasonal timing, refiling with a stronger record is often the better use of time if cap numbers remain.

### A visa refusal

The worker can reapply with better evidence of ties for a 214(b) refusal. Inadmissibility findings may require a waiver. Our article on [what to do after a visa denial](/my-visa-application-was-denied-what-should-i-do) covers the general options.

## Habits that prevent most denials

Employers who file H-2B year after year without trouble share a few habits. They keep three or more years of monthly payroll by occupation. They use the same dates each season unless the business changes, and explain the change when it does. They request the prevailing wage early, review the job order template every year, and keep signed recruiter agreements on file.
