If you're a U.S. citizen age 21 or older, you can sponsor your parents for a green card. Parents of adult citizens are immediate relatives, so there's no annual cap and no waiting line in the Visa Bulletin. That makes sponsoring a parent for a green card one of the faster family-based paths.

## Who can sponsor a parent

- **U.S. citizens age 21 or older** can sponsor their parents.
- **Green card holders** can't sponsor parents. They must naturalize first.
- **U.S. citizens under 21** must wait until they turn 21.

## Who counts as a parent

- **Biological mothers:** Your birth certificate showing her as your mother.
- **Biological fathers:** If your parents were married when you were born, your birth certificate. If not, evidence of legitimation before age 18 or a bona fide relationship.
- **Stepparents:** If the marriage creating the relationship happened before you turned 18.
- **Adoptive parents:** If the adoption happened before you turned 16 and you lived with them for two years.

## The process

1. **File Form I-130** for each parent. Each parent needs a separate petition.
2. **Prepare Form I-864,** the Affidavit of Support, showing you can support your parent at 125% of the poverty guidelines.
3. **Choose adjustment of status or consular processing:**
   - **Adjustment of status:** If your parent is in the U.S. after a lawful entry, they may be able to file Form I-485.
   - **Consular processing:** If your parent is abroad, the case goes to the National Visa Center, then to a consular interview.

## The May 2026 policy change

USCIS guidance issued in May 2026 tells officers that applicants in temporary status, including visitors, should generally apply through a consulate abroad and that adjustment of status should be granted only in limited circumstances. Parents who entered on a visitor visa and planned to adjust status should get advice before filing, since consular processing may now be expected.

## How long it takes

U.S. citizen petition for parents timelines depend on:

- **I-130 processing:** several months to over a year
- **National Visa Center processing:** a few months
- **Consular interview scheduling:** varies by consulate

Overall, sponsoring a parent typically takes one to two years, though some cases are faster.

## Financial requirements

You must meet the income requirement on Form I-864. If your income isn't enough, you can use assets or a joint sponsor.

## Health insurance

Parents may not be eligible for Medicare right away. Consider private health insurance for your parents, especially for older parents.

## Common issues

- **Unlawful entry or overstays:** These can make your parent ineligible to adjust status in the U.S. Consular processing may require a waiver.
- **Prior immigration violations:** Prior overstays or removals can trigger bars.
- **Missing documents:** Birth certificates and marriage certificates must meet requirements.

## Parents abroad who want to keep their home there

Some parents don't want to live in the U.S. full time. A green card requires the holder to make the U.S. their permanent home. Long stays abroad can lead to questions about abandonment at the border. Parents who plan to split time between countries should understand the rules before immigrating. A reentry permit can help for planned absences of up to two years.

## Practical example

A U.S. citizen daughter files an I-130 for her widowed mother, who lives abroad. After the I-130 is approved, the National Visa Center collects civil documents, the I-864, and fees. Her mother attends an interview at the U.S. embassy, receives an immigrant visa, and enters as a permanent resident. Her green card arrives by mail. If the daughter's income falls short, a joint sponsor can file a separate I-864.

## Parents with past immigration problems

If your parent previously overstayed a visa, worked without authorization, or was removed, the case needs a closer review before filing. For immediate relatives, an overstay alone usually doesn't block adjustment of status if the parent entered lawfully, but unlawful presence after a departure can create a three-year or ten-year bar for consular processing. A waiver for that bar requires extreme hardship to a qualifying relative, and for a parent, the qualifying relative must be the parent's U.S. citizen or green card holder spouse or parent, not the sponsoring child. That rule surprises many families and can make some parent cases very difficult.
