An exclusive license with no field-of-use limit, no minimum royalty, and no termination right is a sale with extra steps. Licensors sign agreements like that more often than you would expect, usually because the licensee's counsel drafted the first version and the licensor focused on the royalty rate alone. An IP licensing agreement is a bundle of about a dozen decisions, and the rate is rarely the most important one. This post lists the terms that matter and the position each side typically takes, drawn from the deals our [intellectual property practice](/practice-area/ip-law) negotiates for licensors and licensees.

## The grant clause in an IP licensing agreement

Everything flows from the grant. It should identify the licensed IP precisely (registration numbers for trademarks and patents, a schedule of works for copyrights, a defined term for know-how), the rights granted (to make, use, sell, reproduce, distribute, display, modify), and the limits on those rights.

Three limits do most of the work. Field of use restricts the license to a market or application, so a licensor can license the same technology to a medical device company and a consumer electronics company without conflict. Territory restricts geography. Channel restricts how the product reaches customers, such as online only or through a specific retailer. A licensor who leaves these blank has licensed every field, everywhere, through every channel.

## Exclusive, sole, or non-exclusive

Exclusive means the licensor will license no one else and will not practice the rights itself. Sole means the licensor will license no one else but keeps the right to compete. Non-exclusive means the licensor may license anyone. The words are short and the consequences are large: an exclusive licensee usually gets the right to sue infringers, while a non-exclusive licensee generally does not.

Exclusivity should always be paired with performance obligations. Minimum royalties, minimum sales, launch deadlines, or marketing spend commitments give the licensor a way out if the licensee sits on the rights. Without them, an exclusive license can become a dead asset the licensor cannot reclaim.

## Royalties, reporting, and audit

Payment can be a lump sum, a running royalty on net sales, a per-unit fee, milestones, or a mix. For running royalties, the definition of net sales decides the actual rate: what deductions are allowed (returns, taxes, shipping, discounts), whether sales to affiliates count at arm's-length prices, and how bundled products are allocated.

The reporting and audit clause enforces all of that. The licensee should deliver periodic statements with enough detail to verify the calculation, keep records for a set number of years, and allow an audit by an independent accountant. A common compromise shifts the audit cost to the licensee if the audit finds underpayment above a stated percentage.

## Quality control for trademark licenses

Trademark licenses carry a rule the other IP types do not. A trademark owner who licenses the mark without controlling the quality of the licensed goods or services risks a finding of naked licensing, which can result in abandonment of the mark. The agreement therefore needs real quality standards, a right to inspect and approve samples, and the ability to terminate for non-compliance, and the licensor must actually exercise those rights. The [trademark law overview](/practice-area/key-services/trademark-law) page covers how use by a controlled licensee inures to the benefit of the owner.

## Improvements and grant-backs

Who owns improvements the licensee makes to the licensed technology? The default answer is the licensee, because they created them. Licensors often ask for a grant-back: an assignment or license of improvements back to the licensor. A non-exclusive grant-back is common and generally acceptable. An exclusive grant-back or assignment of all improvements can raise antitrust questions and will be resisted by any sophisticated licensee. Define "improvement" narrowly so the clause does not sweep in the licensee's unrelated products.

## Sublicensing and assignment

A license that is silent on sublicensing usually does not permit it. If the licensee needs to use contract manufacturers, distributors, or affiliates, the agreement should say so and set the conditions: licensor consent, flow-down of the same restrictions, and licensee responsibility for sublicensee breaches. Assignment on a change of control is a separate point. Licensors often want consent rights if the licensee is acquired by a competitor; licensees want the license to travel with the business so it does not block a sale. The [IP due diligence checklist](/ip-due-diligence-checklist-startups) explains how buyers treat licenses that cannot be assigned.

## Confidentiality, enforcement, and termination

Licenses that include know-how or software need a confidentiality clause with teeth, since trade secret protection depends on it. The limits of an NDA in that setting are covered in our post on [whether an NDA alone protects trade secrets](/nda-trade-secret-protection-limits). Enforcement should state who may sue infringers, who controls the litigation, who pays, and how recoveries are split. Termination should cover breach with a cure period, insolvency, challenge to the licensed IP's validity, and failure to meet performance minimums, and it should spell out what happens to inventory, sublicenses, and confidential materials when the agreement ends.
