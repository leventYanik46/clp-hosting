Every personal injury claim, whether it comes from a rear-end collision on I-66 or a fall in a Manhattan lobby, rests on the same four elements: duty, breach, causation, and damages. To prove negligence you have to establish all four, and the adjuster on the other side is looking for the one you cannot. This post walks through each element, the evidence that typically carries it, and the places where claims in Virginia, Washington, DC, and New York tend to fall apart.

## The four elements you must prove

Negligence is a legal conclusion, not a feeling that someone was careless. A court or an adjuster reaches that conclusion only when each element is supported by evidence.

Duty means the person who hurt you owed you a legal obligation to act with reasonable care. Drivers owe it to everyone on the road. Property owners owe it to invited visitors. Doctors owe it to patients. In most injury cases duty is not seriously disputed, but it can be, for example when a trespasser is injured or when a contractor claims it had no control over the area where you fell.

Breach means the person failed to meet that standard. The question is what a reasonably careful person would have done in the same situation. Running a red light, ignoring a reported patch of ice on a walkway, or leaving a loading dock unmarked are all breaches. The standard shifts with the context: a commercial truck driver is measured against federal safety rules, and a surgeon is measured against what a competent surgeon in the same field would do.

Causation means the breach caused the injury. Lawyers split this into two questions. Actual cause asks whether the injury would have happened without the breach. Proximate cause asks whether the injury was a foreseeable result of it. Causation is where many claims stall, especially when you had a pre-existing condition or waited to see a doctor.

Damages means you suffered a real loss: medical bills, lost income, pain, or a lasting limitation. Without damages there is no claim, even if the other person was clearly at fault. Our post on [what damages you can recover in a personal injury case](/damages-in-personal-injury-case) covers the categories in detail.

## Evidence that proves each element

Our [personal injury practice](/practice-area/personal-injury-law) starts every case by mapping the evidence against these four elements, because that is exactly what the adjuster and, later, the jury will do.

- Duty and breach: the police crash report, incident reports, surveillance footage, photographs of the scene and the hazard, witness statements, maintenance logs, and any code or regulation the defendant ignored.
- Causation: medical records that document the complaint on the first visit, imaging, the treating physician's notes linking the condition to the event, and, when needed, a retained expert.
- Damages: itemized bills, pay stubs and employer letters, a treatment timeline, and your own notes on how the injury changed daily life.

Timing matters as much as content. A photograph of a broken stair tread taken the same day is worth far more than one taken after a repair. A medical record created within days of the crash is worth far more than one created six weeks later. If you are reading this soon after an injury, preserving evidence now is the single most useful thing you can do.

## Where causation arguments fall apart

Three patterns show up repeatedly. First, gaps in treatment. If you waited a month to see a doctor, the insurer will argue something else caused the pain. Second, pre-existing conditions. A prior back injury does not defeat your claim; the law lets you recover for the aggravation of an existing condition. But your medical records must show that the new event made it worse, and your doctor must be willing to say so. Third, inconsistent accounts. If the crash report says you had no complaints and the emergency room record says neck pain, that contradiction will be used against you. Tell every provider the full story from the first visit.

## Fault rules in Virginia, DC, and New York

Proving the other side was negligent is only half the fight in two of the firm's jurisdictions. Virginia and the District of Columbia follow contributory negligence: if your own carelessness contributed to the injury in any degree, the defense can bar your recovery entirely. New York follows pure comparative negligence, which reduces your award by your share of fault but does not eliminate it. We explain the practical consequences in our post on [how contributory negligence works in Virginia and DC](/contributory-negligence-virginia-dc-explained). The short version: in Virginia and DC, expect the defense to put as much effort into proving your negligence as into disproving its own.

## Negligence per se and special defendants

Sometimes a statute sets the standard of care. When a driver violates a traffic law designed to protect people like you, and the violation causes the kind of harm the law was meant to prevent, courts in many situations treat the violation itself as proof of breach. This is called negligence per se. It does not win the case by itself, because causation and damages still have to be proved, but it simplifies the breach argument. Crash cases often turn on it, which is why the citation on the police report matters so much. Our [car, truck, and motorcycle accident team](/practice-area/key-services/car-truck-and-motorcycle-accidents) reviews the report for exactly this kind of detail.

Cases against government entities, hospitals, and commercial carriers each have their own wrinkles, such as shorter notice deadlines or expert certification requirements, which is why the type of defendant shapes the strategy from day one.

## What this means for your next step

Write down what happened while it is fresh. Get the names of witnesses. Photograph everything, including your injuries as they change over time. See a doctor and describe every symptom. Keep every bill and every record. Then get advice before you give a recorded statement to anyone's insurer, because that statement is where the defense will look for the element you cannot prove.
