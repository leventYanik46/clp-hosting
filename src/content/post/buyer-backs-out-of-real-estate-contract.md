It depends on when. A buyer who terminates within an unexpired contingency gets the deposit back and owes nothing more. A buyer who refuses to close after the contingencies have run is in default, and the contract's remedies clause decides what happens next: forfeit the deposit as liquidated damages, pay the seller's actual losses, or in rare cases be ordered to complete the purchase. When a buyer backs out of a real estate contract, the deposit clause becomes the most important sentence in the document, and it is negotiated far less often than it should be.

## Walking away under a contingency

If the contract gives the buyer a financing, inspection, appraisal, or title contingency and the deadline has not passed, termination is a right, not a breach. The buyer delivers written notice in the manner the contract requires, and the escrow agent returns the deposit. In practice the return is not always automatic: most escrow agents require a release signed by both parties or a court order before disbursing, though some regional forms allow the agent to release the funds after a notice period passes without objection. A seller who refuses to sign a release can delay the refund but has no right to the money, and the buyer's remedy is a demand letter followed, if needed, by a claim. Our summary of [how contract contingencies work](/real-estate-contract-contingencies) explains the deadlines and notice requirements that make this exit clean.

## Default and the deposit as liquidated damages

Once the contingencies have been satisfied or waived, a buyer who will not close is in breach, and the first question is what the contract says the seller may recover. Many residential forms, and most New York contracts of sale, make the deposit the seller's sole remedy: the seller keeps it as liquidated damages and may not sue for more. Courts enforce such clauses when the amount is a reasonable estimate of the seller's likely loss rather than a penalty, and deposits in customary ranges are routinely upheld. New York practice uses larger deposits than the District or Virginia, and the law there has long allowed a seller to retain a defaulting buyer's deposit of customary size without proving actual damages.

Other forms give the seller an election: keep the deposit, or return it and sue for actual damages. A buyer's counsel should push for the deposit as the sole and exclusive remedy; a seller's counsel should at least preserve the election if the deposit is small relative to the price. Our [real estate contracts](/practice-area/key-services/real-estate-contracts-and-negotiations) group drafts the remedies clause with the size of the deposit in mind, because the two terms only make sense together.

## Actual damages and specific performance

Where the contract allows a damages claim, the seller's measure is generally the difference between the contract price and the property's market value at the time of breach, often shown by a lower resale price, plus carrying costs such as taxes, insurance, and mortgage interest during the delay, less any amount saved. The seller must make reasonable efforts to resell. Specific performance, an order compelling the buyer to buy, is available in principle because land is unique, but courts grant it against buyers rarely, since what the seller wants is money and a damages award supplies it. Buyers seeking specific performance against a defaulting seller fare better, and a buyer in that position typically records a notice of the pending lawsuit to prevent a sale to someone else.

## When the buyer has a defense

A buyer accused of default may have an answer. If the seller failed to deliver insurable title, did not complete agreed repairs, missed a deadline that was a condition of the buyer's obligation, or misrepresented the property in a way that induced the contract, the buyer may be entitled to terminate and recover the deposit rather than forfeit it. A contingency the seller claims was waived may not have been, if the contract required written waiver and none was given. Disputes over whether a condition was satisfied, such as whether the buyer's loan was "denied" or merely delayed, are the most common source of litigation over deposits. The facts and the documents decide them, which is why every notice should be in writing and every deadline tracked.

## Protecting yourself on either side

Buyers should set contingency deadlines they can actually meet, tie the financing contingency to the loan they are truly seeking, avoid waiving the appraisal contingency without the cash to cover a gap, and treat the contract calendar as binding from the day of ratification. Sellers should size the deposit to the risk, decide whether to accept it as the sole remedy, consider a time-is-of-the-essence clause for the closing date, and keep a backup offer alive where the form allows. Both sides should put every communication about termination in writing and avoid informal assurances that could be read as an extension or a waiver.
