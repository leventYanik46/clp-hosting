If an asylum officer or an immigration judge granted you asylum, you do not receive a green card automatically. You have to apply for it, and you can do that once you have been physically present in the United States for one year after the grant. An asylum-based green card is the step that turns protection into permanent residence.

## Asylum status and a green card are different things

Asylum, which you request on Form I-589, protects you from being sent back to a country where you fear persecution. It lets you live and work in the United States, but it is not permanent. A green card gives you lawful permanent resident status, which does not depend on conditions in your home country and is the path to U.S. citizenship.

People who are still waiting for a decision on their asylum case cannot apply for an asylum-based green card. You must win asylum first.

## Who is eligible

To file for an asylum-based green card, you generally need to show that you:

- Were granted asylum and still qualify as a refugee, or are the spouse or child of someone who does
- Have been physically present in the United States for at least one year since the grant
- Are not firmly resettled in another country
- Are admissible, or qualify for a waiver of the ground that makes you inadmissible

Criminal history, past immigration fraud, and security-related concerns are the grounds that cause trouble. Some can be waived for humanitarian reasons, and some cannot, so a record of any kind deserves an attorney's review before you file.

## What the application includes

The application is Form I-485, Application to Register Permanent Residence or Adjust Status. Unlike most green card routes, you do not need a job offer or a family sponsor. The usual package includes:

- A copy of your asylum approval notice or the immigration judge's order
- Government-issued identification and passport-style photographs
- A medical exam on Form I-693 completed by a USCIS-designated doctor
- Any court or police records if you have ever been arrested or charged

Check the current USCIS fee schedule and any fee exemptions before filing, since fee rules change. After filing, USCIS schedules a biometrics appointment and may call you in for an interview. Processing times vary widely, so use the USCIS processing times page for the office handling your case.

## Your green card date is rolled back one year

When USCIS approves an asylee's green card, it generally sets the date you became a permanent resident one year earlier than the approval date. This matters because the five-year clock for naturalization starts from that earlier date. An asylum-based green card can therefore bring citizenship closer than the calendar suggests.

## Bringing family members

Your spouse and unmarried children under 21 who were included in your asylum application can usually apply for permanent residence along with you. Family members who were not included may qualify through Form I-730, the refugee/asylee relative petition, which a principal asylee generally must file within two years of the grant. Once a relative arrives with derivative status, that person can later apply for a green card too.

## Travel and your status

Do not travel to the country where you claimed persecution. Voluntarily returning there can lead USCIS to question whether you really feared that country, and it can put your status at risk. If you must travel anywhere else, apply first for a refugee travel document on Form I-131. Leaving the United States without one can cause serious problems when you try to return.

## Problems that slow these cases

- Inconsistencies between the green card forms and what you said in the asylum case
- Missing or outdated medical exams
- Criminal records that were never disclosed or discussed
- Name or date of birth errors across documents
- An address change that was not reported to USCIS
- Evidence that country conditions have changed, or any sign that the asylum grant was based on false information

USCIS can terminate asylum in some circumstances, such as a fundamental change in conditions or fraud. A green card gives you much stronger protection against that outcome, which is a good reason to file once you are eligible rather than letting the case sit.

## After approval

Once you receive your green card, keep it current and carry it. After the required period as a permanent resident, counting from your rolled-back date, you can apply for citizenship on Form N-400. The earlier you file, the sooner you can plan for that next step.
