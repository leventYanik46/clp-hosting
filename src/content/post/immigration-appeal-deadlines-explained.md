Most immigration appeal deadlines are 30 days, they run from the date on the decision rather than the day you read it, and almost none of them can be extended. That combination is why a person who waits two weeks to open an envelope can lose an appeal that would have won. This guide lists the immigration appeal deadlines that follow each kind of adverse decision, explains which clock applies to which forum, and flags the few situations where a late filing is still possible. CLP's [immigration appeals and federal court practice](/practice-area/key-services/immigration-appeals-delays-and-federal-court) starts every intake by counting these days.

## Appeals from an immigration judge: 30 days to the Board

If an immigration judge orders you removed or denies relief, the Notice of Appeal on Form EOIR-26 must be received by the Board of Immigration Appeals within 30 calendar days of the decision. Received means in the Board's hands, not postmarked. The regulations give the Board no authority to extend the period. If the decision was given orally at the hearing, the clock starts that day. A timely appeal has one large benefit beyond review: it automatically stays removal while the Board decides, which other filings do not. See [whether an immigration appeal stops deportation](/does-immigration-appeal-stop-deportation).

Appeals of custody and bond decisions follow the same 30-day rule but run on a separate track from the removal case itself.

## Appeals from USCIS: Form I-290B and the 33-day mail rule

When USCIS denies a petition or application, the denial notice says whether you may appeal to the Administrative Appeals Office or only file a motion. Either way, Form I-290B is due within 30 days of the decision, or 33 days if the decision was mailed to you. Not every USCIS denial goes to the AAO. A denied Form I-130 family petition, for example, is appealed to the Board on Form EOIR-29, again within 30 days. The same I-290B deadline governs a motion to reopen or a motion to reconsider filed with USCIS. [Whether to file an I-290B or go to federal court](/i-290b-appeal-vs-federal-lawsuit) is a separate question, but both decisions have to be made inside that first month.

## Motions in immigration court or at the Board

Motions follow two clocks. A motion to reconsider, which argues that the judge or the Board made a legal or factual error on the existing record, is due within 30 days of the final decision. A motion to reopen, which presents new facts and evidence, is due within 90 days. Generally only one of each is allowed.

The 90-day limit has real exceptions:

- A motion to reopen based on changed country conditions in an asylum case may be filed at any time if the evidence was unavailable at the original hearing.
- A motion to rescind an in absentia removal order may be filed within 180 days if exceptional circumstances kept you from the hearing, and at any time if you never received proper notice or were in government custody.
- A joint motion, filed with the government's agreement, is not time-limited.
- Ineffective assistance by prior counsel can support equitable tolling in many circuits, provided you meet the procedural requirements for raising it.
- The Board and the immigration judge retain limited authority to reopen on their own motion, though they use it sparingly.

## Petitions for review: 30 days to the court of appeals

If the Board dismisses your appeal, the next step is a petition for review in the federal court of appeals for the circuit where the immigration judge completed the case. The petition must be filed within 30 days of the Board's decision. Courts treat this deadline as strict and do not extend it. Filing a motion to reopen with the Board does not pause the 30 days for the original decision; each Board order carries its own petition-for-review clock, and a person who wants both must file both.

## Federal lawsuits and FOIA appeals

### Lawsuits over delays and denials

Mandamus, habeas and FOIA suits have no comparable short deadline. An APA challenge to a USCIS denial falls under the general six-year statute of limitations for civil actions against the United States. Do not read that as leisure. Delay in a mandamus case undercuts your argument that the wait is unreasonable, and a stale denial is harder to litigate once the applicant's status has lapsed. Our [immigration litigation](/practice-area/key-services/immigration-litigation) page explains when a court challenge beats an administrative appeal.

### FOIA administrative appeals: 90 days

If an agency withholds records or says it has none, you may file an administrative appeal within the period stated in its letter, which by statute is at least 90 days. Missing it usually means filing a new request and starting the wait again.

## How to protect an immigration appeal deadline

Photograph the decision and its envelope the day it arrives. Count the days on a calendar, treating the decision date as day zero. For the Board, send the filing so that it arrives with days to spare and keep proof of delivery. If the deadline is within a week, call a lawyer the same day; a short filing that preserves the appeal can be supplemented with a brief later, while a perfect brief filed on day 31 cannot be heard at all.
