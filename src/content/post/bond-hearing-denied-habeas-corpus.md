When an immigration judge denies a bond hearing, the first question is what kind of denial it was, because the answer decides your next move. A judge who held a hearing and set no bond, or set bond you cannot pay, made a discretionary decision that you appeal to the Board of Immigration Appeals. A judge who refused to hold the hearing at all, ruling that the court has no jurisdiction over your custody, made a legal decision that the Board may be bound to uphold, and in that situation a habeas corpus petition in federal district court is often the only realistic route to release. Confusing the two wastes the weeks a detained person cannot afford, which is why our [habeas corpus team](/practice-area/key-services/habeas-corpus) starts by reading the judge's order before anything else.

## Bond hearing denied on the merits: appeal to the Board

In a bond hearing under INA 236(a), you carry the burden under *Matter of Guerra* of showing you are not a danger to the community and not a flight risk. The judge weighs criminal history, immigration history, family and community ties, employment, the strength of your relief and your record of appearing at hearings. If the judge denies bond or sets a high amount, you may appeal to the Board on Form EOIR-26 within 30 days. The bond appeal is separate from the removal case and does not stop the merits hearings from proceeding.

Two features of bond appeals matter. First, there is no automatic stay in your favor; you stay detained while the Board decides, and DHS can also appeal a grant and obtain an automatic stay of your release in certain cases. Second, INA 236(e) bars federal courts from reviewing the judge's discretionary bond decision, so habeas is generally unavailable to argue that the judge weighed the equities wrongly. Habeas may still reach constitutional errors in how the hearing was conducted, such as a refusal to consider ability to pay or alternatives to detention, which some courts have required. Our [detention and bond practice](/practice-area/key-services/immigration-detention-and-bond) handles bond hearings and bond appeals.

You can also request a new bond hearing before the immigration judge if circumstances have materially changed, for example an approved I-130 or the dismissal of a criminal charge. Every route after a merits denial is compared in our post on [what to do when an immigration judge denies bond](/immigration-bond-denied-next-steps).

## Denial for lack of jurisdiction: three scenarios

### Mandatory detention under INA 236(c)

If DHS charges you with a crime-based ground listed in 236(c), the judge has no authority to set bond. The only agency check is a *Matter of Joseph* hearing on whether you actually belong in the category, for example because the conviction is not a crime involving moral turpitude or is not an aggravated felony under the categorical approach. Our explanation of [crimmigration](/what-is-crimmigration) covers that analysis. The Supreme Court held in *Nielsen v. Preap* that 236(c) applies even if ICE did not arrest you immediately after your criminal custody ended. If the judge finds 236(c) applies and you disagree, a habeas petition can argue that the statute does not cover you; if 236(c) does apply and detention has become prolonged, the argument shifts to due process, which we cover in [when prolonged immigration detention violates due process](/prolonged-immigration-detention-due-process).

### Arriving aliens and applicants for admission under INA 235(b)

People who present themselves at a port of entry, and others treated as applicants for admission, are detained under 235(b) and are not eligible for immigration judge bond. Release is by ICE parole, requested in writing from the field office with evidence of identity, ties and lack of danger. Where parole is refused without individualized consideration or detention has become prolonged, habeas is the remedy.

### People who entered without inspection

For decades, people who entered without inspection and were later arrested inside the country could request bond under 236(a). As of this writing, the government's position, adopted by the Board in *Matter of Yajure Hurtado*, is that these individuals are applicants for admission under 235(b)(2)(A) and ineligible for immigration judge bond, and judges have been denying hearings on that basis. Many federal district courts have rejected that reading and ordered bond hearings; others have deferred to the Board. The law differs from district to district and is changing quickly, and our [federal habeas team](/practice-area/key-services/habeas-corpus) checks the current decisions in the district where the client is held before filing.

## Why habeas, and why not just appeal

When the denial is jurisdictional, appealing to the Board is often futile because the Board's own precedent dictates the result, and you remain detained throughout the appeal. Exhaustion of the agency route is not a statutory requirement for a 2241 habeas petition in most circuits, and courts regularly excuse it where the Board has already decided the legal question against you. A habeas petition asks a federal judge to decide whether ICE is applying the right statute and whether the Constitution permits detaining you without any hearing. If the court agrees with you, it typically orders a bond hearing before an immigration judge within a set number of days, often with the burden placed on the government to justify continued detention, and sometimes orders release outright.

Our overview of [2241 habeas petitions](/what-is-2241-habeas-petition-immigration) explains the filing mechanics, venue and the custodian rule.

## What to do in the first week after the denial

- Get the judge's bond order or the written reason for declining jurisdiction, plus the Form I-286 custody determination from ICE.
- Calendar 30 days from the bond decision for a Board appeal, even if habeas is the plan, so the option stays open.
- Collect certified dispositions for every arrest and conviction; they decide whether 236(c) applies.
- Gather proof of ties and a release plan: address, family, employment letter, medical needs, community letters.
- Find out the detention facility and A-number, and watch for transfers, since a petition must be filed where the person is held.

A bond denial is a setback, not the end. The detention case and the removal case run on separate tracks, and progress on relief in the removal case, such as an approved petition or a strong asylum claim, strengthens every request for release.
