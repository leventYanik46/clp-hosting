The physician national interest waiver lets a doctor get an employment-based green card without the PERM labor certification by committing to practice full time in a medically underserved area or a Veterans Affairs facility for five years. It is a statutory program with its own rules, separate from the standard EB-2 NIW test most professionals use.

## How the physician NIW differs from the standard NIW

A standard NIW requires showing that your work has substantial merit and national importance, that you are well positioned to advance it, and that waiving the job offer benefits the U.S. A physician NIW replaces that analysis with a service commitment. If you meet the service conditions, the national interest is established by law.

The trade-off is time. A standard NIW can lead to a green card as soon as the priority date allows. A physician NIW green card cannot be finalized until the five years of qualifying service are complete.

## Who qualifies

You must:

- Be a physician who will work full time in clinical practice
- Practice in a Health Professional Shortage Area, a Medically Underserved Area, or a VA facility
- Have a statement from a federal agency or a state department of public health confirming that your work is in the public interest
- Complete five years of qualifying service, generally within six years of the petition

Primary care specialties and specialists can both qualify, depending on the shortage designation for the location and specialty.

## The petition

The employer, or the physician if self-employed, files Form I-140 in the EB-2 category with:

- The employment contract or a letter committing to full-time clinical work in the qualifying area
- Evidence that the location is designated as underserved
- The public interest attestation from the federal or state agency
- The physician's credentials, including licensure and board eligibility

## Timeline and the five-year clock

Time worked in H-1B status or as a J-1 waiver physician before the I-140 is filed generally counts toward the five years. Time spent in J-1 training does not. After the I-140 is approved, you can file Form I-485 when your priority date is current, and you will receive work authorization while the case is pending. Toward the end of the five years, you submit evidence that you completed the service, and USCIS can then approve the green card.

## Overlap with the J-1 waiver

Many international medical graduates on J-1 visas first get a Conrad 30 or other waiver, which requires three years of service in an underserved area. Those years can count toward the five-year physician NIW requirement when properly documented, so many physicians pursue both in sequence.

## Common pitfalls

- Changing employers without confirming the new position also qualifies
- Part-time or administrative roles that do not meet the full-time clinical requirement
- Shortage designations that change for the location during the service period
- Gaps in documentation, such as missing payroll or schedule records for some years


## Frequently asked questions

### Can I change specialties during the five years?

Possibly, if the new position also meets the shortage area and full-time clinical requirements. A new I-140 may be needed. Talk to counsel before accepting a new role.

### Does my family get green cards too?

Yes. A spouse and unmarried children under 21 can file for green cards as derivatives. They can generally get work and travel permits while the case is pending.

### What happens if I stop working in the underserved area?

If you do not complete the five years, the green card cannot be approved on this basis. You would need another path, such as an employer-sponsored case or a standard NIW.

### Can I file the I-485 before the five years are done?

Yes, once your priority date is current. The I-485 stays pending until you submit proof of completed service, and you can work and travel on the pending case in the meantime.

## Is the physician NIW the right path?

For some physicians, an EB-1A, a standard NIW, or employer-sponsored PERM may be faster. The best route depends on your specialty, location, country of birth, and how long you want to stay with your current position.
