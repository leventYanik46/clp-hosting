A merits hearing is the most important day in an immigration court case. It's when the immigration judge hears testimony and evidence and decides whether you qualify for relief from removal, such as asylum, cancellation of removal, or adjustment of status. Preparation makes a major difference.

## Master calendar vs merits hearing

**Master calendar hearings** are short, procedural hearings. The judge confirms your information, takes pleadings, and sets deadlines. Many respondents appear at the same session.

**Merits hearings,** also called individual hearings, are scheduled for your case alone. They can last several hours. The judge hears your testimony, cross-examination by the government attorney, and testimony from witnesses.

## Before the merits hearing

**Evidence deadlines.** The immigration judge sets a deadline for filing evidence, often 15 days before the hearing for non-detained cases. Late evidence may be excluded.

**What to file:**
- Your application for relief, such as Form I-589 for asylum or Form EOIR-42B for cancellation of removal
- Supporting documents: identity documents, country condition reports, medical records, police reports, and letters
- Witness lists
- Translations of foreign documents with a certificate of translation

**Prepare your testimony.** You'll be asked about your history and the basis for relief. Your testimony must be consistent with your application.

## At the hearing

1. **Opening:** The judge reviews the record.
2. **Your testimony:** Your attorney asks questions.
3. **Cross-examination:** The government attorney asks questions.
4. **Witnesses:** Others may testify.
5. **Closing statements:** Each side summarizes.
6. **Decision:** The judge may decide that day or later in writing.

## The immigration judge's role

The judge evaluates credibility, the evidence, and the law. Credibility is central, especially in asylum cases. The judge considers consistency, detail, and plausibility.

## Interpreters

The court provides interpreters. Speak in your strongest language, not English, if you're not fluent.

## Hearings by video

Some hearings are held by video (WebEx). Confirm the format in advance and make sure your technology works if appearing remotely.

## After the decision

- **Granted:** You receive relief.
- **Denied:** You can appeal to the Board of Immigration Appeals within 30 days.
- **Reserved:** The judge will issue a written decision.

## Tips

- Arrive early
- Dress respectfully
- Speak clearly and wait for the full question
- If you don't understand, ask
- Tell the truth

## Credibility is built before the hearing

Judges compare your testimony with everything in the record: your application, any earlier interviews, border statements, and documents you filed. Inconsistencies on key facts, such as dates, places, or who was involved in an event, can damage your credibility. Review your application carefully with your attorney before the hearing and explain any differences honestly. Small differences in memory are normal. Unexplained contradictions on central facts are what judges focus on.

## Witnesses and expert testimony

Witnesses who can confirm parts of your story, such as family members who saw events or community members who know your work, can strengthen your case. In asylum and cancellation cases, expert testimony about country conditions or medical and psychological conditions can also help. The court needs advance notice of witnesses, so include them in your pre-hearing filings.

## What judges look for in common forms of relief

**Asylum:** a well-founded fear of persecution based on race, religion, nationality, political opinion, or membership in a particular social group, along with the one-year filing deadline or an exception to it.

**Cancellation of removal for non-permanent residents:** 10 years of continuous physical presence, good moral character, and exceptional and extremely unusual hardship to a U.S. citizen or permanent resident spouse, parent, or child.

**Adjustment of status in court:** an approved or approvable petition and eligibility to adjust.

Your evidence should map to each element. A judge who can see how every document supports a specific requirement is in a better position to grant relief.
