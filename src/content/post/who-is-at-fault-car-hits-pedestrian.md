A driver who hits a pedestrian is not automatically at fault, and a pedestrian who was outside the crosswalk is not automatically barred from recovering. Who is at fault when a car hits a pedestrian comes down to three questions: who had the right of way at that exact spot, what each person could see and do in the seconds before impact, and which state's fault rule applies. That last question matters more in this region than almost anywhere else, because Washington, DC, Virginia, and New York answer it three different ways. Capitol Law Partners handles these claims through its [pedestrian and bicycle accident practice](/practice-area/key-services/pedestrian-and-bicycle-accidents), and the fault analysis is the first thing we do on every file.

## Right of way is the starting point, not the end

Every state in this region gives pedestrians the right of way in a marked crosswalk and in the unmarked crosswalk that legally exists at most intersections, as long as the pedestrian is crossing with the signal. Drivers must yield there and must use due care toward pedestrians everywhere else. Pedestrians, in turn, must obey walk signals and may not step suddenly into the path of a vehicle that is too close to stop.

A right-of-way violation is strong evidence of negligence, but it does not close the question. A driver who had the green still has to keep a proper lookout, control speed for conditions, and avoid a person who is visibly in the road. Most urban pedestrian crashes involve a turning vehicle at an intersection where the pedestrian had the walk signal, and the driver's defense is usually that the pedestrian was hard to see. Lighting, speed, phone use, and the driver's line of sight through the A-pillar all become evidence.

## How fault is decided when a car hits a pedestrian in DC, Virginia, and New York

### Virginia: contributory negligence

Virginia follows pure contributory negligence. If the pedestrian's own carelessness contributed to the crash in any degree, the pedestrian recovers nothing. Insurers know this, which is why the adjuster's first questions after a Virginia pedestrian crash are about where you crossed, what you were wearing, and whether you were looking at your phone. The counterweight is the last clear chance doctrine: a driver who saw, or should have seen, a pedestrian in danger and had a real opportunity to avoid the collision can still be held fully liable even if the pedestrian was negligent first.

### Washington, DC: a special rule for pedestrians and cyclists

The District also follows contributory negligence for most injury claims, but it changed the rule for people on foot and on bikes with the Motor Vehicle Collision Recovery Act of 2016. Under that act, a pedestrian's or cyclist's negligence bars recovery only if it was greater than the combined negligence of the drivers involved. A pedestrian who was partly careless, but less careless than the driver, can still recover in DC. The act preserved the last clear chance doctrine as well. This is one of the most pedestrian-friendly fault rules in the country, and it only applies to collisions with motor vehicles, not to a trip on a broken sidewalk.

### New York: pure comparative negligence

New York assigns a percentage of fault to each person and reduces the pedestrian's award by the pedestrian's share. A pedestrian found 30 percent at fault recovers 70 percent of the damages. New York also pays medical bills and lost wages through the vehicle's no-fault coverage regardless of fault, which we explain in [which insurance pays for a pedestrian or cyclist injury](/which-insurance-pays-pedestrian-cyclist-injury).

## The evidence that actually decides fault

The police crash report (the FR300P in Virginia, the PD-10 in DC, the MV-104AN in New York City) records the officer's view, but an officer who arrived after the fact rarely saw the collision. Fault is usually decided by evidence that disappears quickly:

- Traffic, transit, storefront, and doorbell camera footage, much of which is overwritten within days.
- The vehicle's event data recorder, which stores speed, braking, and throttle for the seconds before impact.
- The 911 recording and the driver's first statement at the scene.
- Signal timing records from the transportation agency, which show whether the walk signal was on.
- Witness names collected at the scene, since people who stop to help rarely wait for police.

The driver's insurer takes the driver's statement within hours. The pedestrian is often in a hospital bed. That imbalance is why the first week matters, and our checklist for [what to do after a bicycle accident with a car](/bicycle-accident-with-car-next-steps) applies just as well on foot.

## The arguments insurers make, and where they break

Dark clothing at night, crossing mid-block, headphones, and alcohol are the four claims that show up in almost every pedestrian denial letter. Each one is answered the same way: with the driver's conduct. A driver traveling at the speed limit on a lit street with working headlights can see a person in dark clothing well before the stopping distance runs out. Mid-block crossing is relevant only if a reasonable driver could not have avoided the pedestrian, and the event data recorder often shows no braking at all. Headphones matter only if a sound would have changed the result.

In Virginia, these arguments carry real risk because any proven fault ends the claim. In DC and New York, they reduce or shift the number rather than erase it. Either way, the response has to be built on evidence, not on a competing story.
