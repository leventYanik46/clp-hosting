The most common reason for an L-1B RFE or denial is that the petition describes the employee's knowledge in terms that would fit anyone in the industry. USCIS reads "expert in cloud infrastructure" or "deep understanding of our clients' needs" as general skill, not specialized knowledge. The other frequent problems are a miscounted year abroad, an undocumented ownership chain, and a placement at a client site that looks like labor for hire. Each has a fix, and most can be avoided before filing.

## Specialized knowledge described too generally

USCIS must see how the employee's knowledge is different from that of others in the company or the industry. A Request for Evidence on this point usually asks for a more detailed description of the knowledge, how it was gained, how many other employees have it, and why the U.S. operation cannot get it by hiring locally or training someone.

The response should answer those questions with documents. Name the proprietary systems, product lines or internal methods. Attach training records with hours and dates, project histories, internal documentation the employee wrote, and a headcount comparison showing how few employees perform the same function at the same level. If the knowledge is advanced knowledge of internal processes, show how it compares to coworkers in the same role. Our explanation of [what counts as specialized knowledge](/l-1b-specialized-knowledge-explained) covers the standard USCIS applies; our [L-1B specialized knowledge visa](/practice-area/key-services/l-1b-visa) team builds RFE responses around it.

## The U.S. role does not use the knowledge

An employee may have specialized knowledge abroad but be headed to a U.S. job that does not need it. If the U.S. duties read as general engineering, sales or support work, USCIS may find that the position itself is not a specialized knowledge role. The petition should connect each major U.S. duty to the specific knowledge the employee brings, and explain the business reason for the transfer: a product launch, a client implementation, the training of a U.S. team.

## Problems with the year abroad or the corporate link

These are technical issues, and they are the most frustrating denials because the facts are often fine.

- The year abroad was not continuous, was part-time, or fell outside the three years before filing.
- The employment letter, payroll records and resume give different dates or titles.
- The ownership chain between the foreign employer and the U.S. petitioner has a missing link, such as an intermediate holding company with no share register in the file.
- One of the entities is not doing business regularly, systematically and continuously.

The response is a complete paper trail. Our [L-1B petition documents checklist](/l-1b-petition-documents-checklist) lists what each element requires.

## Third-party worksite placements

An L-1B employee who will work primarily at an unaffiliated client's site must be controlled and supervised by the petitioner, not the client, and the placement must be connected to the petitioner's own product or service. USCIS often issues RFEs asking for the client contract, the statement of work, an itinerary and a letter from the client. If the contract shows that the client directs the work day to day, or the work is generic staffing, the petition is likely to fail. Fix this at the contract stage, not in the RFE response.

## Consular refusals after approval

An approved I-797 does not guarantee a visa. Consular officers can refuse under section 221(g) to request more documents or to send the petition back to USCIS for review, and they look closely at whether the employee can explain the specialized knowledge in plain words at the interview. Prepare the employee to describe the proprietary product, the training and the U.S. assignment in two or three sentences. Under a blanket L petition, the officer decides eligibility directly, so weak blanket cases are better filed as individual petitions.

## Options after an L-1B denial

A denial is not always the end. The main options:

### Refile with a stronger record

Often the fastest route. A new petition can fix gaps in the evidence and request premium processing. If the employee is in the United States and the status has expired, a refiled petition may need to go through consular processing rather than a change or extension of status.

### Appeal or motion

The employer can file Form I-290B to appeal to the Administrative Appeals Office or to ask USCIS to reopen or reconsider. The deadline is generally 30 days from the decision, so act quickly. An appeal makes sense when USCIS misapplied the legal standard; a motion to reopen when there is new evidence. If an administrative route fails, federal court review under the Administrative Procedure Act is sometimes available, and our [immigration litigation](/practice-area/key-services/immigration-litigation) team handles those cases.

### Switch categories

If the employee will manage people or a function, the [L-1A route for managers](/practice-area/key-services/l-1a-visa) may fit better. Some specialists also qualify for H-1B or O-1 status.
