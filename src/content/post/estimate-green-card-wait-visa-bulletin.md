No one can tell you the exact date your green card will become available. But you can make a reasonable estimate from public data, using the same logic behind most green card calculators. The method takes about ten minutes, and it gives you something better than a single prediction: a range, and a sense of how much it can move.

## What you need

- **Your priority date.** Found on your I-130 or I-140 receipt or approval notice. For PERM cases, it is usually the date the labor certification was filed.
- **Your category.** Such as F2A, F4, EB-2, or EB-3.
- **Your chargeability country.** Usually your country of birth, not citizenship.
- **The current and past visa bulletins.** Published monthly by the State Department.

## Step 1: Find the right chart

Each visa bulletin has two charts:

- **Final Action Dates**: when a green card can actually be approved or an immigrant visa issued.
- **Dates for Filing**: when you can submit paperwork early.

USCIS announces each month which chart applies to adjustment of status filings. For estimating when your green card can be approved, use **Final Action Dates**.

## Step 2: Measure the gap

Compare your priority date with the current Final Action Date for your category and country. If the cutoff is January 1, 2018 and your priority date is January 1, 2021, you are three years behind the line.

## Step 3: Measure the speed

Look at the Final Action Date for your category and country 12 months ago, 24 months ago, and 36 months ago. Calculate how far it moved each year.

Example: if the cutoff moved from January 2015 to January 2018 over three years, it advanced about one year per calendar year.

## Step 4: Divide

Divide your gap by the average speed. Three years behind, advancing about one year per year, suggests roughly three years of waiting. If the cutoff advanced only six months a year, the same gap suggests about six years.

Use the slowest and fastest years you measured to build a range rather than a single date.

## Why estimates miss

The method assumes the past repeats. Several things break that assumption:

- **Retrogression.** Dates can move backward when demand exceeds supply.
- **Spillover.** Unused family visas can increase employment numbers, and vice versa, in unpredictable amounts.
- **Policy changes.** Pauses, processing slowdowns, and rule changes affect how many visas are used.
- **Hidden demand.** People with older priority dates may appear when dates advance.

Treat any visa bulletin prediction, including your own, as a planning tool.

## What a green card calculator cannot tell you

Online calculators use the same inputs. They cannot account for your personal timing issues:

- **Children aging out.** The Child Status Protection Act formula depends on how long the petition was pending and when a visa became available.
- **Category changes.** A permanent resident petitioner who naturalizes can convert a spouse's F2A case to immediate relative. An F2B child who marries may move to a different category or lose eligibility.
- **Cross-chargeability.** A spouse's country of birth may give a shorter line.

## Check monthly

The visa bulletin is usually released in the middle of each month for the following month. Mark your calendar, record the Final Action Date for your category each month, and update your estimate once or twice a year.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why does my category show "C"?
"C" means current: any priority date qualifies, so no wait for a visa number exists that month.

### Why did my date go backward?
That is retrogression, used when demand exceeds the visas available. It can reverse later.

### Is the Dates for Filing chart useful?
Yes, for timing paperwork. When USCIS allows it, you can file adjustment of status earlier based on that chart.

### Do online green card calculators use official data?
Most use visa bulletin history, but their assumptions vary. Check their inputs.

### Can I speed up my wait?
Sometimes through a category upgrade, cross-chargeability, or a petitioner naturalizing. The visa bulletin itself cannot be expedited.

### When is the visa bulletin released?
Usually in the middle of each month for the following month, on the State Department website.

### Does my spouse's country of birth matter?
It can. Cross-chargeability lets an applicant use a spouse's country when they immigrate together, which may give a shorter line.

### Are immediate relatives in the visa bulletin?
No. Spouses, unmarried children under 21, and parents of adult U.S. citizens are not subject to annual limits, so they do not wait for a priority date.

### Does my priority date change if my petition is refiled?
Usually a new petition gets a new date, though some employment-based cases can keep an earlier date from an approved I-140.

### Is the State Department's annual projection reliable?
It reflects official expectations at the time, but actual movement often differs. Use it as one input, not a guarantee.
