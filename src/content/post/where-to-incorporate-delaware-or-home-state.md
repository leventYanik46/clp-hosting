Here is the short rule: incorporate in Delaware if you expect to raise venture capital or sell to a large acquirer, and form in your home state if you do not. A company that incorporates in Delaware but operates in Virginia pays Delaware's franchise tax and registered agent, then registers in Virginia as a foreign entity and pays there too. That second layer is worth it when investors require Delaware law. It is wasted money for a consulting firm in Herndon or a restaurant in Washington. The rest of this post explains what Delaware actually buys, what it costs, and how the three jurisdictions where we practice compare.

## Why investors prefer a Delaware incorporation

Delaware's corporation statute is flexible, its Court of Chancery decides business disputes without juries and with deep experience, and decades of case law make outcomes predictable. Venture funds, their lawyers, and public-company acquirers all know the Delaware playbook. The standard financing documents used across the startup market assume a Delaware corporation, and a fund investing in a Virginia corporation may ask you to reincorporate before closing, at your expense and on their timeline.

Delaware also offers some operational advantages. Formation is fast, the state does not require the names of directors or officers on the certificate of incorporation, and the annual franchise tax can be modest for a small company if the right calculation method is used. For a company that will have a board, preferred stock, option grants, and multiple financing rounds, Delaware is the path of least resistance. ## What Delaware costs a company that does not need it

A Delaware entity with no Delaware operations still owes the state an annual franchise tax or LLC tax, must keep a Delaware registered agent, and must file an annual report if it is a corporation. None of that replaces the obligations in the state where the company actually operates. You will register there as a foreign entity, appoint a registered agent there, file that state's periodic reports, and pay that state's income and employment taxes. Two states, two calendars, two sets of fees.

There is a less obvious cost. Delaware corporate disputes between shareholders or against directors will generally be heard in Delaware, which is convenient for institutional investors and inconvenient for a two-founder company in New York. And a Delaware incorporation does not reduce your home-state taxes. Where income is earned, not where the certificate is filed, decides that.

## Forming in DC, Virginia, or New York

Each of the firm's home jurisdictions has its own formation office and its own quirks. Rules change, so treat the following as the current picture and confirm before filing.

Virginia entities are formed through the State Corporation Commission. Filing is inexpensive, the annual registration fee is low, and corporations file a short annual report. Virginia has no publication requirement and is a practical choice for companies based in Northern Virginia with no near-term plans for institutional investment.

District of Columbia entities are formed through the Department of Licensing and Consumer Protection. DC requires a biennial report, and most businesses operating in the District also need a Basic Business License in the appropriate category, which is a separate process from formation and one that trips up many new owners. Factor the license into your launch timeline.

New York entities are formed through the Department of State. New York LLCs must publish a notice of formation in two newspapers for six consecutive weeks within 120 days of formation and then file a certificate of publication. In New York City, the newspaper costs can exceed the cost of formation itself. Corporations have no publication requirement, which is one reason some New York founders choose a corporation. Both file a biennial statement. Our post on the [documents required to set up a company in America](/documents-required-to-set-up-a-company-in-america) lists the filings in more detail.

## Foreign qualification: the step people skip

Wherever you form, you must register as a foreign entity in every state where you are "doing business," a term each state defines by its own statute and case law. Having an office, employees, or regular in-person sales in a state almost always qualifies. Selling into a state from elsewhere usually does not. A Delaware corporation with its only office in Herndon is doing business in Virginia and must register with the SCC before it starts.

Skipping foreign qualification has real consequences. In most states, an unregistered foreign entity cannot maintain a lawsuit in that state's courts until it registers and pays back fees and penalties. Contracts remain valid, but the company loses the ability to enforce them in court at the moment it most needs to. Registration also triggers the periodic reports and taxes that keep the company in good standing, which lenders, landlords, and investors check. ## How to decide

Ask five questions. Will you raise institutional money or sell to a strategic buyer within a few years? Where will your office and employees be? Do you have, or expect, investors whose counsel will insist on Delaware? Is there a state-specific cost, such as New York's publication requirement, that shifts the math? And how much administrative calendar can you honestly maintain?

If the answers point to Delaware, form there and foreign-qualify at home on the same day. If they point to your home state, form there and keep the option open: a Virginia or New York corporation can convert or reincorporate into Delaware later, usually as a tax-free reorganization, when a financing makes it necessary. That path is well worn, and forming at home first is not a mistake.
