A general counsel retainer is an ongoing arrangement in which a law firm acts as your company's outside legal department, usually for a fixed monthly fee that covers a defined scope of routine work. Work outside that scope, such as litigation or a major transaction, is priced separately. The structure gives you predictable legal costs and a lawyer you can call without watching the clock, and it gives the firm a reason to learn your business well. Whether it works depends on how clearly the scope and terms are written at the start.

Most clients of our [general counsel and advisory practice](/practice-area/general-counsel-services) work with us this way, so the questions below are the ones we walk through before every engagement.

## Common fee structures

The word retainer is used loosely, so it helps to separate the models.

### Flat monthly fee
A set amount each month for a defined list of services and, often, an expected volume of work. This is the most common structure for ongoing general counsel work. It rewards early questions, because calling counsel before signing a contract costs nothing extra.

### Hourly with a monthly cap or budget
You pay for time spent, up to an agreed limit, with a conversation required before going over it. This suits companies whose needs swing sharply from month to month.

### Prepaid blocks of hours
The company buys a block of time at an agreed rate and draws it down. Check whether unused hours expire or roll over.

### Hybrid arrangements
A flat fee for the routine core, plus hourly or project pricing for anything outside it. Many companies land here.

You may also see an advance fee deposit: money paid up front and held in the firm's client trust account until it is earned. Ethics rules require lawyers to keep unearned client funds separate from the firm's own money, so ask how any advance will be handled.

## What the scope should say

Scope disputes cause most retainer friction. A good scope description lists what is included (for example, review of standard commercial contracts, employment questions, board consents, a compliance calendar, and a set number of strategy meetings), what is excluded (litigation, financings and acquisitions, patent prosecution, regulatory investigations), and how out-of-scope work is quoted and approved. It should also set response time expectations and say who on the firm's team handles what.

Look at your legal work over the last year before you negotiate. If you sign about ten customer contracts a month, say so. Pricing built on a realistic volume is more stable than pricing built on guesses. ## Engagement letter terms to read closely

The engagement letter is the contract that governs the relationship. Under the ethics rules followed in most states, a lawyer must communicate the scope of the representation and the basis of the fee, preferably in writing. Read for:

- Who the client is. Under the rule governing organizational clients (Rule 1.13 in most states), the company is the client, not its owners or officers individually. Founders who want personal advice on their own equity or estate may need a separate engagement.
- Conflicts of interest. The firm should run a conflicts check and disclose any relationship with your counterparties.
- Billing terms: when invoices issue, which costs are passed through, and how billing questions are resolved.
- Rate changes and an annual review of the flat fee.
- Termination: either side can usually end the relationship, but the letter should say what happens to pending matters and your files.
- Confidentiality and data handling, especially if you will share sensitive customer or employee information.

## How to get value from a general counsel retainer

The companies that get the most from a retainer use it early and often. Send counsel draft contracts before the redline round, not after signature. Schedule a regular check-in, monthly or quarterly, to review upcoming hires, deals, and filings. Give counsel the governing documents and key contracts so no call starts from scratch. And measure value by problems avoided as well as hours used: a dispute that never happens never shows up on an invoice.

If a retainer starts to feel like a poor fit, raise it. Scope can be adjusted, and a frank conversation about volume and expectations is part of a healthy relationship.
