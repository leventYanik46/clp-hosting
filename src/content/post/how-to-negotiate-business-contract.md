The way to negotiate a business contract well is to decide, before you send a single redline, which three or four terms you would walk away over and which twenty you would give up for the right price. Most negotiations are lost in the preparation, not at the table. The party that has ranked its priorities trades low-value points for high-value ones and closes. The party that has not fights every clause with equal energy, exhausts the other side, and either signs a worse deal out of fatigue or loses the counterparty altogether. What follows is the process we use in our [contract negotiation](/practice-area/key-services/contract-drafting-review-negotiation) work, from first read to signature.

## Prepare before the first redline

Read the entire draft once without marking it. Then map it: who has to do what, by when, for how much, and what happens if they do not. Compare that map against how the deal was described on the sales call or in the term sheet. The gaps are your first list of issues.

Next, sort every issue into three groups. Must-haves are the terms you cannot sign without, such as an IP assignment for a product you are paying to build, or a liability cap you can insure against. Wants are terms that improve your position but that you would concede for something in return. Tradeables are terms you do not much care about but the other side might, and they are your currency. Write down your walk-away position for each must-have before emotions enter the room.

Finally, learn what the other side needs. A vendor at quarter end needs a signature date. A customer with a procurement policy needs a specific insurance certificate. A startup needs cash flow more than it needs a high cap. Knowing their constraints tells you which of your tradeables are worth the most to them. Our checklist of [which clauses every business contract needs](/essential-business-contract-clauses) is a useful frame for the mapping step.

## Who holds the pen

The party that writes the first draft sets the structure and the defaults, and every change from that baseline has to be argued for. If you can offer your paper, do. If the other side insists on theirs, which large customers and most SaaS vendors will, accept it and redline rather than attempting to swap in your form. Rewriting their template from scratch signals that the deal will be difficult and usually gets routed to their legal department's slowest queue.

Keep strict version control. One master document, tracked changes on, each turn labeled with the date and the party. Confusion about which version contains which concession is a common way for an agreed point to come undone late in the process.

## Redline with reasons, not just strikeouts

A deleted sentence with no explanation invites a flat rejection. A deleted sentence with a comment that says "we cannot accept uncapped liability because our insurance coverage is limited; we propose a cap of twelve months of fees with carve-outs for indemnity and confidentiality" invites a conversation. Every substantive change should come with a reason and, where possible, proposed replacement language. That shifts the work of drafting the compromise onto you, which is an advantage: your language becomes the starting point.

Keep the comments short and businesslike. Keep the tone cooperative; the person on the other side is usually a counterpart you will work with for years after signing. And resist marking up points that do not matter to you. A redline that touches every paragraph tells the other side you have no priorities, and they will respond by rejecting everything and waiting to see what you actually fight for.

## Trade terms instead of conceding them

Serial concessions are how good positions erode. The other side pushes back on your cap; you raise it. They push on the payment terms; you extend them. Each concession was small, and together they changed the deal. The alternative is to package: "we can accept your liability cap if the indemnity for third-party IP claims sits outside it," or "we can agree to net 60 if the termination-for-convenience notice period goes from 30 days to 90." Each trade preserves something you ranked higher than what you gave.

Liability terms are where trades happen most often, because each side's exposure is a matter of structure rather than a single number. Our post on [how limitation of liability and indemnity clauses work](/limitation-of-liability-indemnification-clauses) explains the pieces that can be exchanged: the cap amount, the carve-outs, the consequential damages waiver, and the indemnity scope.

## The terms worth fighting for

Across most commercial agreements, the fights that matter concentrate in a handful of places: scope and acceptance, payment timing and disputed invoices, termination rights and what triggers them, liability allocation, ownership of IP and data, and any exclusivity, non-solicit, or auto-renewal provision. Governing law and venue matter if a dispute is foreseeable. Almost everything else, from notice mechanics to counterparts, can usually be accepted as drafted by a reasonable counterparty.

Companies that negotiate the same type of contract repeatedly should write down their positions and fallbacks once, so each negotiation starts from an approved baseline instead of from scratch. ## When to stop and when to walk

Negotiations have diminishing returns. After two or three turns of the document, the remaining open points are usually ones where the parties genuinely disagree, and another round of redlines will not resolve them. At that point, get the principals on a call with a short list of open items, and settle them in conversation. Lawyers can then paper the result in one turn.

Walk away when a must-have is refused and the other side has shown it will not trade for it. Walking away is also the strongest signal in any negotiation, and it only works if you have decided in advance that you mean it. Whatever the outcome, do not start performing before the contract is signed.
