Business owners expanding into the U.S. usually narrow their choices to two visas: the L-1 for intracompany transfers and the E-2 for treaty investors. The L-1 visa vs E-2 visa comparison comes down to where your business is now, your nationality, and whether you want a green card.

## Quick comparison

| | L-1A | E-2 |
|---|---|---|
| Nationality requirement | None | Must be a national of a treaty country |
| Foreign business required | Yes, operating and related to the U.S. company | No |
| Prior employment abroad | 1 continuous year in last 3 | Not required |
| Investment required | No minimum, but U.S. office must be funded | Substantial investment at risk |
| Initial stay | 1 year for new office, up to 3 years otherwise | Up to 2 years per admission, visa up to 5 years |
| Maximum stay | 7 years | No limit, renewable |
| Green card path | EB-1C for managers and executives | No direct path |
| Spouse work | Authorized incident to status | Authorized incident to status |

## The L-1 path

The L-1A works when you already own or manage a business abroad and want to open a U.S. branch, subsidiary, or affiliate. You must have worked for the foreign company for at least one continuous year in the three years before the petition, in a managerial, executive, or specialized knowledge role.

**New office L-1.** If the U.S. company is new, the first L-1 approval is limited to one year. Within that year, the U.S. office must grow enough to support a managerial or executive role, usually with hired staff. The extension requires evidence the business is operating and that you are managing rather than doing the daily work yourself.

**Green card advantage.** L-1A managers and executives can apply for an EB-1C green card, which doesn't require PERM. This is a major advantage over the E-2.

## The E-2 path

The E-2 works for nationals of treaty countries who invest a substantial amount in a U.S. business they will develop and direct. You don't need a foreign business or prior employment. You can buy an existing U.S. business or start a new one from scratch.

**Investment.** There's no fixed minimum. The investment must be substantial relative to the business's total cost and must be committed and at risk.

**Duration.** E-2 status can be renewed indefinitely as long as the business continues to qualify. But the E-2 has no direct green card path, and you must state an intent to leave the U.S. when your E-2 status ends. Officers generally accept that statement, but it means a green card needs a separate strategy.

## Which is better for you?

**Choose the L-1 if:**
- You have an established foreign business with at least a year of your employment
- You want a green card through EB-1C
- Your nationality isn't on the treaty list

**Choose the E-2 if:**
- You're a treaty national
- You don't have a foreign business, or yours is too small to support an L-1
- You want flexibility to stay long-term without a green card
- You want to buy an existing U.S. business

## Using both

Some owners start on one and switch to the other. A treaty national might start on an E-2 for speed, then move to an L-1A once the U.S. company is established, to pursue EB-1C. Others start on an L-1 and switch to E-2 once they reach the L-1 maximum.

## Common pitfalls

**L-1:** Failing to grow the new office within the first year. Taking a title without real managerial duties.

**E-2:** Investing too little relative to the business's cost. Starting a business that won't hire anyone beyond the owner and family.

## Planning your expansion

The right choice depends on your current business, your nationality, and your long-term goals. A thoughtful strategy at the start can save years and preserve your green card options.

## Ownership and control details that matter

For an L-1, the U.S. and foreign companies must have a qualifying relationship: parent and subsidiary, branches of the same company, or affiliates owned by the same people in roughly the same proportions. The foreign company must keep doing business during the entire time the employee is in the U.S. on L-1. If the foreign operation shuts down, the L-1 basis disappears.

For an E-2, what matters is that the U.S. business is at least 50% owned by treaty nationals and that the investor controls it, either through majority ownership or operational control. An investor with a 50% stake in an equal partnership can qualify if the ownership is with another treaty national and the investor has real control.

## Family members

Both visas allow spouses to work incident to status, and children can study but not work. The practical difference is long-term: on L-1, the family can be included in an EB-1C green card case. On E-2, children face the age-21 cutoff without a built-in path to permanent residence.
