Before launching a new product, answer five legal questions. Can you use the name? Can you prove what the marketing says? Does a regulator need to approve the product or be told about it? Do your customer terms and privacy policy cover the new offering? And who pays if it hurts someone or fails? Each question is cheaper to answer before launch than after, when packaging is printed, ads are running, and a cease-and-desist letter or an agency inquiry forces changes on someone else's schedule.

## Clear the name before a new product launch

Teams often fall in love with a product name months before anyone checks it. A trademark clearance search looks for existing registered and unregistered marks that are similar in sound, appearance, or meaning and used for related goods or services. A conflict can mean rebranding after launch, or a lawsuit. Once the name is cleared, you can file a U.S. trademark application with the USPTO on an intent-to-use basis before the product reaches the market, which secures an early priority date. Check domain names and social media handles at the same time.

If the product includes technology or content created by contractors, confirm the company owns it through written assignments. Software should also be reviewed for open-source components whose licenses could require attribution or disclosure of your source code.

## Back up every marketing claim

Under the FTC Act, advertising must be truthful and not misleading, and objective claims need a reasonable basis before they run. Claims like clinically proven, twice as fast, eco-friendly, or Made in USA all need support. The FTC's Made in USA standard, for example, requires that all or virtually all of the product be made domestically. Comparative claims about competitors can also draw false advertising suits under the Lanham Act.

If you use influencers or customer testimonials, the FTC's Endorsement Guides require clear disclosure of material connections, such as free products or payment, and testimonials that are not typical must be properly qualified. Build a substantiation file for each major claim before launch so you can answer a challenge quickly.

## Check for regulatory approvals and rules

Some products need permission, registration, or testing before sale. Examples:

- Food, dietary supplements, drugs, cosmetics, and medical devices fall under FDA rules, which range from labeling requirements to premarket review.
- Consumer products, especially children's products, are subject to Consumer Product Safety Commission standards and testing.
- Devices that emit radio frequency energy generally need FCC equipment authorization.
- Financial products, alcohol, firearms, and many professional services require state or federal licenses.
- Online services directed to children under 13 must comply with COPPA.

State law adds another layer, including consumer protection statutes, automatic renewal rules for subscriptions (New York has one), and state privacy laws such as the Virginia Consumer Data Protection Act, which applies to companies that handle personal data on a large number of Virginia residents.

## Update your customer terms and privacy policy

A new product often changes what you promise and what data you collect. Review terms of service or sales terms for warranties, disclaimers, limitation of liability, subscription and cancellation terms, and dispute resolution. Update the privacy policy to describe any new data collection, especially location, biometric, health, or children's data. The policy has to match what the product actually does, because a privacy policy that describes less than the product collects is a deceptive practice risk.

If the product is sold through distributors, retailers, or app stores, check those agreements too. Their terms can override yours or impose requirements of their own.

## Allocate the risk of something going wrong

Ask your insurance broker whether your general liability policy covers the new product, and whether you need product liability, professional liability, or cyber coverage. Review contracts with manufacturers and suppliers for quality standards, indemnification, recall cost allocation, and insurance; our post on [vendor contract clauses that control legal risk](/vendor-contract-clauses-legal-risk) covers the terms that matter most. Plan how you would handle a defect or recall before launch, including who makes the call and how customers are notified.
