To apply for an E-1 visa, most people go through a U.S. consulate in their home country: an online DS-160, the DS-156E treaty trader form, a documentary package showing the company's trade, and an interview. If you are already in the United States in valid status, you can instead ask USCIS to change your status on Form I-129. Either way, the work that decides the case happens before anything is filed, when you prove the trade and the ownership on paper. Below is the sequence our [E-1 treaty visa filing](/practice-area/key-services/e-1-visa) team follows and where delays usually come from.

## Step 1: Confirm the company and the trade qualify

Before you open a single form, confirm three facts. Your country must be on the State Department's E-1 treaty list. The company must be at least 50 percent owned by nationals of that country who are not U.S. citizens or green card holders. And the company must already carry on substantial trade, more than half of it international trade with the treaty country.

The trade test is where most of the preparation goes. Pull a year or more of invoices, shipping records, and payment confirmations, and have your accountant total international trade by country. If the numbers are thin or the treaty-country share is close to half, decide now whether to wait. Our post on [what counts as substantial trade for an E-1](/substantial-trade-e-1-visa) explains how officers read those figures.

## Step 2: Decide where to apply for the E-1 visa

Applying at a consulate gives you a visa you can travel on. A change of status through USCIS gives you E-1 status without a visa, so the first trip abroad means a consular application anyway. Change of status is not available to people who entered on the Visa Waiver Program, and you must be in valid status when USCIS receives the petition.

The tradeoffs mirror the ones we cover for investors in our [E-2 change of status and consular processing comparison](/e-2-change-of-status-vs-consular-processing): an interview and an immediate decision at the consulate, or a paper review with a chance to answer a Request for Evidence at USCIS. If you are in the U.S. on another status, our [change and extension of status](/practice-area/key-services/change-and-extension-of-status) page covers the mechanics.

## Step 3: Register the company if your consulate requires it

Some consulates handle E visas through a dedicated unit that first reviews the company itself. In that model, the treaty company submits its ownership and trade evidence, and once the post accepts the company, individual employees apply under it with lighter paperwork. Other posts review the company and the applicant together in one case.

Check your post's E visa instructions before you assemble anything. They often set page limits, tab orders, and submission methods, and a package that ignores them can be returned unread. We build every file to the specific post's format.

## Step 4: Complete the DS-160, the DS-156E, and the package

The DS-160 is the general nonimmigrant visa form, completed online for the applicant and for each dependent. The DS-156E is the treaty trader and investor application. It asks about the company's ownership, its trade, its finances, and the applicant's role, and it is usually signed by an officer of the company.

The supporting package typically includes:

- passports and proof of nationality for the applicant and the company's owners
- corporate documents showing ownership, such as articles, share registers, and an organizational chart
- a summary of international trade by country and period, with sample invoices, bills of lading, customs entries, and wire records
- financial statements and tax returns for the U.S. company
- a letter describing the applicant's executive, supervisory, or essential role and the reasons for it

Pay the application fee, then schedule the interview. Check the consulate's website for the current fee and the order of steps, because posts differ.

## Step 5: Prepare for the interview

E-1 interviews are usually short. The officer may ask what the company trades, who its main U.S. and treaty-country counterparties are, how much of its trade goes to the treaty country, and what you will do in the United States. Answer from knowledge, not notes. If you are an employee rather than the owner, be ready to explain why your role needs you specifically.

The officer may approve at the window, refuse under section 214(b), or issue a 221(g) letter asking for more documents. A 221(g) letter is not a final refusal; send exactly what it asks for, in the format it asks for.

## Step 6: Enter, check your I-94, and plan the renewal

The visa's validity period depends on the reciprocity schedule for your country. Each time you enter on it, you are admitted for two years. Check your electronic I-94 after every entry to confirm the class and the date, and confirm that your spouse's record shows the E-1S annotation, which serves as evidence of work authorization.

To stay longer, you can file Form I-129 with USCIS for an extension in two-year increments, or travel and re-enter on a valid visa for a new two-year admission. When the visa expires, you renew it at a consulate, where the trade will be reviewed again.

## What drives the timeline

We do not give fixed timelines, because they depend on factors outside your control. At a consulate, the main drivers are the appointment backlog at your post, whether the post requires company registration first, and whether the case enters administrative processing. At USCIS, they are the service center's workload and whether you pay for premium processing. Check the consulate's website and USCIS processing times before you set a start date for the U.S. assignment.
