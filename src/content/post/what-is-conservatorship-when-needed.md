A conservatorship is a court order that gives one person, the conservator, legal authority over another adult's money and property because that adult can no longer manage it. It covers income, bank accounts, real estate, bills, benefits, and lawsuits. It does not cover where the person lives or what medical care they receive; those are a guardian's decisions. Virginia and Washington, DC use the word conservator. New York does not: the same role is called a guardian of the property under Article 81 of the Mental Hygiene Law. The label changes, the job does not. Here is what a conservator actually controls, who needs one, and what the court expects in return.

## What a conservator controls

Once appointed, the conservator steps into the person's financial shoes. In practice that means:

- Collecting income: pensions, rent, dividends, and, after a separate application to the Social Security Administration, benefit payments as representative payee.
- Managing accounts and investments under a prudent-investor standard, which generally means preserving capital rather than chasing returns.
- Paying for the person's care, housing, taxes, and debts, and keeping records that show every dollar.
- Dealing with real estate, though in Virginia and DC a sale usually requires separate court approval.
- Handling claims and lawsuits, including signing settlements on the person's behalf.
- Filing an inventory and annual accountings with the court.

What the conservator cannot do matters as much. They cannot make a will for the person, change beneficiary designations, or make gifts, except with specific court authority. They cannot mix the person's money with their own. And they cannot override the person's personal choices unless they also hold a guardianship. The [conservatorship practice page](/practice-area/key-services/conservatorship) lists the filings that come with the role.

## Conservatorship versus guardianship

The simplest way to keep the two straight: the conservator handles the estate, the guardian handles the person. Virginia and DC appoint them separately, often to the same individual but not always, and a petition can ask for one, the other, or both. New York's Article 81 produces a single guardian who may be given property powers, personal powers, or both, tailored to the person's functional limitations.

Families often assume they need both when one will do. A parent with advancing dementia who lives with an adult child and accepts care may only need a conservator to deal with the bank and the pension. A young adult with a disability who lives in a supported setting but has no assets may only need a [guardian for personal and medical decisions](/practice-area/key-services/legal-guardianship). Asking for both powers when the evidence supports one invites the judge to narrow the order, and it doubles the reporting burden.

## Who needs a conservatorship

The petitions we see fall into a few patterns. An older adult with dementia, a stroke, or a serious psychiatric condition who never signed a durable power of attorney, or whose power of attorney a bank refuses to honor. A person being financially exploited, where the court needs to freeze accounts and recover transfers, and the agent under the existing power of attorney is the one doing the exploiting. An adult child with an intellectual disability who is about to receive an inheritance or a settlement that a representative payee cannot manage. And a minor who inherits or receives insurance proceeds, since no institution will pay a lump sum to a child.

In each case the court applies the same test: the person must be unable to manage property or financial affairs, that inability must be proved by clear and convincing evidence, and no less restrictive arrangement can protect them. If a durable power of attorney or a funded trust would solve the problem, a judge will expect to hear why it is not being used; our post on [whether a trust or power of attorney can avoid a conservatorship](/avoid-conservatorship-trust-power-of-attorney) explains those alternatives.

## What the court supervises

This is the most heavily supervised arrangement in estate law, and the supervision is the reason courts prefer alternatives. Expect the following, in roughly this order. The conservator qualifies before the clerk and usually posts a surety bond sized to the estate, paid from the estate. Within the first months the conservator files an inventory listing every asset and its value. In Virginia that goes to the commissioner of accounts; in DC and New York, to the court. Then come annual accountings showing every receipt and disbursement, backed by statements and receipts, reviewed by the commissioner, an auditor, or a court examiner. Certain acts, including selling real estate, making gifts, and paying the conservator, need advance approval. The details are in [what a conservator's annual accounting must show](/conservator-annual-accounting-requirements).

Fail to file, and the court issues a show-cause order. Keep failing, and the conservator is removed and the bond is called.

## Limited orders

Every jurisdiction allows the court to limit the conservator's powers to the areas where the person actually needs help. A limited order might give the conservator authority over the brokerage account and the house while the person keeps a checking account and an allowance. That design protects the person's dignity and is also easier to obtain, because the evaluation only has to support the limited powers requested. When the facts fit, ask for it.
