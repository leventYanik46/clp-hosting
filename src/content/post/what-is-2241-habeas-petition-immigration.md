A 2241 habeas petition is a lawsuit filed in federal district court under 28 U.S.C. 2241 that asks a judge to decide whether the government is lawfully holding you in immigration custody. It does not decide your immigration case. It decides your detention, and it is the only route to a judge who sits outside the immigration system: an Article III judge who can order ICE to release you, to give you a bond hearing it has refused, or to leave you in the district while the court rules. For someone held by ICE without a hearing or far longer than the law permits, it is often the only remedy that works on a timeline measured in weeks rather than years.

## What the petition challenges, and what it does not

Habeas corpus asks a single question: is this custody lawful? In immigration cases that question usually takes one of these forms.

- ICE is holding you under the wrong statute, for example treating you as a mandatory detainee under INA 236(c) when your conviction does not fit the category.
- The immigration judge refused to hold a bond hearing on the ground that the court has no jurisdiction over your custody.
- You have been detained for many months without any hearing at which the government had to justify continued custody.
- Your removal order is final but ICE cannot actually remove you, and detention has passed the six-month mark described in *Zadvydas v. Davis*.
- ICE transferred you across the country in a way that cut off access to your lawyer and your evidence.

What habeas does not do is review your removal order. Federal law channels review of removal orders to the courts of appeals through a [petition for review](/petition-for-review-after-bia-decision), and INA 242(e) sharply limits habeas review of expedited removal orders. A habeas court also cannot second-guess the bond amount an immigration judge set as a matter of discretion; INA 236(e) bars that. Our [habeas corpus for immigration detention](/practice-area/key-services/habeas-corpus) page details the statutes ICE relies on and the arguments that fit each.

## Who the petition names and where it is filed

A habeas petition is filed against the person who holds you, and under *Rumsfeld v. Padilla* that is generally the immediate custodian, the warden or facility director, in the federal district where you are physically detained. Petitions also customarily name the ICE field office director, the Secretary of Homeland Security and the Attorney General. Filing in the wrong district or against the wrong custodian does not usually end the case for good, but it costs weeks while the government moves to dismiss or transfer, and weeks are what a detained person does not have.

Venue matters for a second reason. Federal district courts have reached different conclusions on several detention questions, including whether people who entered without inspection can receive bond from an immigration judge and whether the government must carry the burden at a prolonged-detention hearing. The law that applies to your petition is the law of the district and circuit where you are held.

## How a 2241 habeas petition proceeds

The petition is filed with a memorandum of law and the detention records: the Notice to Appear, Form I-286 custody determination, any bond order and the immigration judge's ruling. If transfer or removal is imminent, a motion for a temporary restraining order is filed the same day asking the court to bar both while it decides. Courts commonly order the government to respond, in a filing called the return, within a short period. You file a reply, sometimes called a traverse. Most petitions are decided on the papers; a minority involve a hearing. There is no jury.

The remedies are practical. A court can order immediate release, release on conditions, a bond hearing before an immigration judge within a set number of days, and in many districts a hearing at which the government, not you, bears the burden of proving danger or flight risk. It can also enjoin transfer out of the district and set a deadline for an ICE custody decision. Our guide to [filing a habeas petition for someone in ICE custody](/how-to-file-habeas-petition-ice-custody) covers what families should gather before the petition is drafted.

## Habeas versus a bond hearing versus mandamus

These three tools are often confused, and they are not interchangeable.

A bond redetermination is a hearing before an immigration judge under INA 236(a). You carry the burden of showing you are neither a danger nor a flight risk, and the judge cannot rule that the detention statute is being applied unconstitutionally. It is the first step when it is available, and a denial can be appealed to the Board of Immigration Appeals. Our [detention and bond](/practice-area/key-services/immigration-detention-and-bond) practice handles those hearings.

A writ of mandamus asks a federal court to compel an agency to perform a duty it owes you, almost always to decide an application that has been unreasonably delayed. It has nothing to do with custody. Our article on [what a writ of mandamus is](/what-is-a-writ-of-mandamus) explains when it fits.

A habeas petition asks a federal judge whether your detention is lawful at all. It is the right tool when the immigration judge will not or cannot hear your bond request, when Board precedent makes the bond request futile, or when the length of detention itself has become the problem. Exhaustion of the agency route is prudential rather than mandatory in most circuits, and courts regularly excuse it where the immigration judge has already said no jurisdiction.

## What a win and a loss look like

A granted petition does not end your immigration case. It gets you out, or gets you a fair hearing, while the removal case continues before the immigration judge or on appeal. A denied petition can be appealed to the court of appeals, and because habeas challenges ongoing custody, a new petition can be filed if the facts change, for example when detention continues for another six months without a hearing. ICE sometimes releases a detainee after the petition is filed to moot the case; that is a win for the client even if the court never rules.
