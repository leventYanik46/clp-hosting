No statute says a USCIS delay becomes unreasonable at twelve months, or twenty-four, or forty. When you sue over a stalled case, the judge decides whether the USCIS delay is unreasonable by weighing six factors drawn from a 1984 D.C. Circuit decision, Telecommunications Research and Action Center v. FCC, known everywhere as the TRAC factors. Understanding them tells you two things before you file: whether your wait is the kind courts act on, and what evidence to gather. Capitol Law Partners runs this analysis on every file before our [mandamus and delay litigation team](/practice-area/key-services/immigration-mandamus) drafts a complaint, because a suit that fails the factors wastes money and can harden the agency's position.

## The six TRAC factors courts use for an unreasonable USCIS delay

The factors, in the order courts usually discuss them:

- Whether the time the agency takes follows a rule of reason.
- Whether Congress has indicated how fast the agency should move.
- Whether human health and welfare are at stake, which makes delay less tolerable.
- The effect of ordering this case decided on the agency's other priorities.
- The nature and extent of the interests harmed by the delay.
- Whether the agency acted improperly, though no impropriety is required to find a delay unreasonable.

Courts say the first factor is the most important and that the factors are weighed, not counted. Three favorable factors do not guarantee a win.

## Factors one and two: the agency's timing and what Congress said

### Factor one: is there a rule of reason?

USCIS argues that it decides cases first in, first out within each form type and office, and that this is a rule of reason even when the queue is long. Plaintiffs answer with the agency's own published processing times: if the posted time for your form at your office has passed, the agency has by its own measure departed from its rule. A case within posted times is very hard to win. A case well beyond them, with no explanation, is the core of a good complaint. Where the delay is attributed to a background check or security review, courts have been more deferential, but they still expect the agency to show that the check is actually moving.

### Factor two: what Congress said

Congress declared in 8 U.S.C. 1571 that immigration benefit applications should be processed within 180 days. Courts treat that as a statement of policy rather than a binding deadline, but it is routinely cited to show that delays of years exceed what Congress contemplated. For naturalization, Congress went further and created a direct remedy when a decision is not made within 120 days after the interview, described in [the 120-day rule for a stuck N-400](/n-400-120-day-rule-after-interview).

## Factors three and five: the harm to you

These factors are where your evidence matters most. A declaration describing a lapsed work permit and a lost job, a child aging out of eligibility, a medical condition that travel restrictions aggravate, a spouse kept apart, or a business unable to operate without the beneficiary turns an abstract wait into a concrete injury. Generic frustration does not. Document the harm with employer letters, medical records and financial statements, and connect each harm to the pending decision.

## Factor four: the line-jumping argument

This is the government's strongest point and the one that defeats many suits. USCIS argues that ordering your case decided simply moves you ahead of equally deserving applicants who filed earlier, so the court would be reordering a queue rather than curing a delay. Courts in some districts accept this argument readily, especially in asylum and certain employment-based backlogs where the queue is long and the agency can show it is working through it. The reply is to show that your case is not stuck in a queue at all but has fallen out of it: cases filed after yours have been decided, your inquiries produced form responses, or the file has sat at a step that does not depend on other cases.

A related point concerns visa availability. An I-485 that cannot be approved because a visa number is not yet available under the Visa Bulletin is not unreasonably delayed in a legal sense; the agency has no duty to decide what it cannot grant. [Priority date versus filing date](/priority-date-vs-filing-date) explains how to check where you stand.

## Factor six: bad faith

You do not need to prove that USCIS is acting in bad faith, and most complaints do not allege it. But where a case has bounced between offices, where the agency has given inconsistent explanations, or where a decision was promised in writing and never came, those facts belong in the complaint.

## What the court orders when you win

A judge who finds the delay unreasonable generally orders the agency to decide within a set number of days. The court does not tell USCIS how to decide. Many cases never reach that point, because the agency adjudicates after the complaint is served and the case is dismissed as moot. Either way, the outcome is a decision, which is why the strength of the underlying application has to be reviewed before suit; a forced decision on a weak file is a fast denial.
