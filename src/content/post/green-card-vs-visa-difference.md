A visa, a green card, and citizenship are three different things, and confusing them leads to real mistakes, from overstays to missed deadlines. In plain terms: a visa is a travel document that lets you ask to enter the U.S. for a purpose. A green card shows you are a permanent resident who can live and work here indefinitely. Citizenship is full membership, with a U.S. passport and the right to vote.

## What a visa is

A U.S. visa is a stamp placed in your passport by a U.S. embassy or consulate. It lets you travel to a U.S. port of entry and request admission for a specific purpose, such as tourism, study, or work.

Key points:

- **The visa is not your status.** Your status and authorized stay are set by CBP at entry and recorded on your I-94.
- **A visa can expire while you are lawfully in the U.S.** That is normal. It only matters when you travel again.
- **Nonimmigrant visas** are for temporary purposes. **Immigrant visas** are issued to people who will become permanent residents on arrival.

## Passport vs. visa

A passport is issued by your country of citizenship and proves your identity and nationality. A visa is issued by the country you want to visit and goes inside your passport. U.S. citizens travel on U.S. passports and do not need U.S. visas.

## What a green card is

A green card, officially a Permanent Resident Card, shows you are a lawful permanent resident. Permanent residents can:

- Live in the U.S. permanently
- Work for any employer, or be self-employed
- Travel abroad and return, within limits
- Sponsor a spouse and unmarried children for green cards
- Apply for citizenship after meeting residence requirements

Green cards are usually valid for ten years and must be renewed, but permanent resident status itself does not expire. Conditional green cards, valid for two years, require removal of conditions.

## What U.S. citizenship adds

The difference between a U.S. citizen and a U.S. permanent resident comes down to rights and security:

- **Voting** in federal elections, and running for most offices
- **A U.S. passport**, with consular protection abroad
- **No risk of removal** based on most later conduct, unlike permanent residents, who can be deported for certain crimes
- **Sponsoring more relatives**, including parents, married children, and siblings
- **Passing citizenship to children** born abroad, subject to rules
- **Eligibility for federal jobs** that require citizenship

## How people move from one to the next

A common path: a nonimmigrant visa, such as F-1 or H-1B, then a green card through an employer or family member, then naturalization through Form N-400 after three or five years.

Others arrive on an immigrant visa and are permanent residents from day one. Some gain citizenship automatically through a citizen parent and only need to document it.

## Common confusions

- Thinking an expired visa means you must leave: check your I-94 instead
- Thinking a green card holder can vote: permanent residents cannot vote in federal elections
- Thinking a green card never needs renewal: the card expires even though the status does not
- Thinking a U.S.-born child automatically makes parents residents: a child must be 21 before petitioning for a parent


## Frequently asked questions

### Do green card holders need a visa to travel abroad?

They need a visa if the destination country requires one for their nationality. A U.S. green card does not change that, though some countries give green card holders easier entry.

### Can I have a visa and a green card at the same time?

No. Once you become a permanent resident, nonimmigrant visas no longer apply to your U.S. entry. Use your green card and passport.

### Can a visa lead to a green card?

Some visas allow you to pursue a green card while holding them, such as H-1B and L-1. Others, such as tourist and student visas, require nonimmigrant intent, so changing to permanent residence needs a separate qualifying basis.
