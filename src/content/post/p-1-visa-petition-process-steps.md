A P-1 case starts with a petition to USCIS, not a visa application, and the petition can be filed no more than one year before the competition, season or tour begins. Teams and touring groups that wait until a few weeks before the first date usually end up paying for premium processing and still risk a missed opening night. The P-1 visa process has six steps, and the order matters because each one feeds the next. What the athlete or group must prove is set out on our [P-1 visa for internationally recognized athletes and groups](/practice-area/key-services/p-1-visa) page and in the guides to [P-1A requirements for athletes](/p-1a-visa-requirements-athletes) and [P-1B requirements for entertainment groups](/p-1b-entertainment-group-requirements). This article covers the sequence.

## Step 1: Choose the petitioner and define the event

Nobody files a P-1 for themselves. The petitioner must be a U.S. employer, a U.S. sponsoring organization, a U.S. agent, or a foreign employer acting through a U.S. agent. A club signing a player is the employer. A promoter or booking agency can file for a touring group as agent. A foreign club or ensemble visiting the United States files through a U.S. agent.

Then define the event. The P regulations tie the petition to a specific competition, event or performance, which can be one match, a tournament series, a league season or a tour. If the work takes place in more than one location, the petition needs an itinerary with dates and places. A clear event definition drives everything that follows, including how long USCIS approves the petition for.

## Step 2: Request the labor consultation

The petition must include a written consultation from an appropriate labor organization: a players' association for league athletes, the American Federation of Musicians for many musical groups, or the relevant performers' union for other disciplines. The organization may comment on the petition or state that it has no objection. Where no appropriate labor organization exists, USCIS decides the case on the record, and the petition should explain the search. Ask early, attach the contract and itinerary, and keep proof of the request. A late letter delays the whole filing.

## Step 3: Assemble the petition

The filing is Form I-129 with the P classification supplement. A complete package usually contains:

- The contract with the athlete or group, or a summary of the terms of an oral agreement.
- An explanation of the event, with start and end dates, and the itinerary.
- Evidence of international recognition: the criteria for athletes, or the award or criteria for groups.
- For groups, the member list with the dates each member has performed with the group.
- The consultation letter.
- Passport biographic pages and, for anyone in the United States, proof of current status.

Foreign-language documents need certified English translations. The current USCIS filing fee is set by regulation and changes from time to time, so check the fee schedule before filing.

## Step 4: File, and decide on premium processing

File with the USCIS service center the form instructions designate. Regular processing times vary by service center and season, so check USCIS processing times rather than relying on a typical figure. Premium processing is available for P petitions and commits USCIS to act within a short fixed window, either approving, denying or issuing a Request for Evidence. For a season opener or a festival slot, premium processing is often worth the cost. If an RFE arrives, the response deadline is fixed in the notice, so the people who gathered the evidence need to be reachable.

## Step 5: File for support staff and family

Coaches, trainers, technicians and other essential support personnel do not ride on the principal petition. They need their own P-1S petition, with a consultation, a statement describing their essential role, prior work with the principal athlete or group, and their critical skills, plus a contract or summary of terms. P-1S workers must also keep a residence abroad.

The spouse and unmarried children under 21 of a P-1 athlete or entertainer can receive P-4 status. They do not need a separate USCIS petition when the principal applies abroad; each applies for a P-4 visa at the consulate using the principal's approval. P-4 dependents may study but may not work.

## Step 6: The visa interview or change of status

After approval, each person outside the United States applies for the visa at a U.S. consulate: Form DS-160, the interview, and the approval notice, which the consulate verifies electronically. Because P status requires a residence abroad you do not intend to abandon, expect questions about home, family and plans after the event. Canadian citizens generally do not need a visa stamp and can present the approval at the port of entry. A person already in the United States in lawful status can ask for a change of status in the petition instead.

You may be admitted up to ten days before the validity period and remain up to ten days after it, but you may work only during the validity period itself. How long that period lasts, and what happens with extensions and trades, is covered in [how long a P-1 visa lasts and what happens if you are traded](/p-1-visa-validity-athlete-trades). Groups coming through a reciprocal exchange between a U.S. and a foreign organization use the [P-2 reciprocal exchange visa](/practice-area/key-services/p-2-visa) instead, which follows a similar sequence.
