USCIS decides an O-1B arts petition on paper, and the paper most musicians and actors have on hand (posters, a website, a reel, follower counts) is rarely the paper that proves a criterion. This O-1B evidence checklist for musicians and actors maps each regulatory criterion to the documents that satisfy it and flags the proof of reputation that petitions most often leave out. The legal test, distinction in the arts, is explained on our [O-1B visa page for musicians, actors and other artists](/practice-area/key-services/o-1b-visa) and in [what distinction in the arts means for an O-1B visa](/o-1b-distinction-in-the-arts-standard). Here the focus is the exhibits.

## The core petition documents

Before any criterion, the petition needs its structural pieces. Missing one of these produces a Request for Evidence no matter how strong the career is:

- Form I-129 with the O supplement, signed by the U.S. employer or agent.
- Copies of written contracts with you, or a summary of the terms of an oral agreement.
- An explanation of the events or activities, with start and end dates, and an itinerary if you will perform in more than one place.
- The written advisory opinion, typically from the American Federation of Musicians for musicians and from Actors' Equity or SAG-AFTRA for actors, depending on the work.
- Your passport biographic page and, if you are in the United States, proof of your current status.

The consultation deserves its own planning; [who provides the O-1 advisory opinion](/o-1-advisory-opinion-who-provides-it) covers it.

## Lead roles and the reputation behind them

The criterion for a lead or starring role in productions or events with a distinguished reputation needs two sets of documents, and petitions usually supply only the first. You prove your role. Then you prove the production or event mattered.

For musicians, the role evidence is headline or featured billing in festival lineups, concert programs naming you as soloist, album credits as lead artist and contracts showing your billing. For actors, it is playbills, cast lists, screen credits and contracts that state the role. The reputation evidence is separate: reviews of the production or festival in major outlets, the venue's history and capacity, the festival's age and selection process, awards the production won. A lead role at a theater no officer has heard of proves nothing until the exhibit shows why the theater is distinguished. The same two-part proof applies to the criterion for a lead or critical role for distinguished organizations, such as an orchestra, an opera company, a record label or a theater company, where a letter from the organization describing your role should sit next to evidence of the organization's standing.

## Press, reviews and expert testimonials

Published material by or about you in major newspapers, trade journals or magazines should be filed as the complete article, showing the outlet's name, the date and the author. Add circulation, audience or readership data so the officer can see the outlet is major. Interviews count when the outlet is independent; paid placements, press releases and pieces you wrote yourself do not. Foreign-language material needs a full English translation with the translator's certification of competence and accuracy, as USCIS rules require for any foreign-language document.

Testimonials under the significant recognition criterion must come from organizations, critics, government agencies or recognized experts, and the letter has to establish the writer's authority. Attach the writer's biography. A conductor, festival director, casting director or critic with a documented career carries weight; a bandmate or a friend in the industry does not. The general drafting rules are in [what makes a strong recommendation letter for an EB-1 or O-1](/recommendation-letters-extraordinary-ability).

## Commercial success and pay

The criterion for major commercial or critically acclaimed success is where musicians and actors have different raw material:

Musicians can use chart positions, sales or streaming certifications, distributor or label reports with an explanation of what the numbers mean in the genre, sold-out notices and ticket reports from promoters. Raw streaming totals with no context are weak; a short explanation of how they compare in the genre is stronger.

Actors can use box office receipts, ratings, extended or transferred runs, and critics' lists. Again, context is what turns a number into evidence.

For the high remuneration criterion, collect contracts, pay statements and tax records, then add a benchmark: the applicable union scale, published industry surveys, or letters from producers or promoters comparing your fee to others in the field. A good income is not the test; a high income relative to the field is.

## Comparable evidence and the gaps to close before filing

For the arts, and only for the arts, the regulation allows comparable evidence when a criterion does not readily apply to your occupation. A session musician who is never billed, or an actor in a tradition without trade press, can offer substitutes, but the petition must explain why the standard criterion does not fit before presenting the alternative. Comparable evidence is not a way to repackage weak evidence for a criterion that does apply.

Before filing, check for the usual gaps: undated screenshots, web pages printed without the address, translations missing, letters unsigned, and exhibits that never get tied to a criterion in the brief. Every document should be cited for a specific point. If your work involves many venues, the itinerary and contracts follow the rules in [how an agent files an O-1B petition for a touring artist](/o-1b-agent-petition-touring-artists).
