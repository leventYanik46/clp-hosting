Sometimes, but not the way most families expect. A parent who crossed the border without inspection cannot adjust status inside the United States, even though you are a U.S. citizen and your parent is an immediate relative. The green card has to come through a consulate abroad, and leaving the country after years of unlawful presence triggers a ten-year bar on returning. The waiver that solves this for spouses, Form I-601A, often does not work for parents, because a U.S. citizen child is not a qualifying relative. Whether a parent who entered without inspection can get a green card therefore turns on a short list of exceptions, and the first job is to find out which, if any, applies.

## Why a parent who entered without inspection cannot adjust

Adjustment of status requires that the applicant was inspected and admitted or paroled. Immediate relatives are excused from the overstay and unauthorized-work bars, which is why a parent who came on a visitor visa and stayed can adjust, as explained in [adjustment of status for parents](/adjustment-of-status-for-parents). They are not excused from the admission requirement. A parent who was never inspected has no admission, and the I-485 is denied on that ground alone.

That leaves consular processing. The Form I-130 is filed and approved in the normal way, since the petition tests only the relationship. The problem comes at the next step. A person who leaves the United States after more than 180 days of unlawful presence is barred from returning for three years; after a year or more, for ten. Nearly every parent in this situation faces the ten-year bar the moment they depart for the interview. Our [green card for parents](/practice-area/key-services/green-card-for-parents) practice does not file the I-130 in these cases until the family understands what happens after approval.

## The I-601A problem for parents

Form I-601A asks USCIS to waive the unlawful presence bar before the applicant leaves, so the family knows the outcome in advance. The general mechanics are in [how the I-601A provisional waiver works](/i-601a-provisional-waiver-explained). The waiver requires extreme hardship to a U.S. citizen or lawful permanent resident spouse or parent of the applicant. A U.S. citizen son or daughter, however severe the hardship to them, does not qualify.

In practice this means the waiver is available only when your parent has a spouse or a parent with U.S. citizenship or a green card. The most common version: your other parent is a citizen or permanent resident, and hardship to that spouse supports the waiver. Where your parent is widowed, divorced or married to someone without status, and your grandparents have no status, there is no qualifying relative and no I-601A. The petition can still be approved, but the consular interview would mean a decade abroad.

## Section 245(i): the grandfather clause

Some parents can adjust despite the entry. Section 245(i) allows adjustment, on payment of a penalty fee, for a person who was the beneficiary of an immigrant visa petition or labor certification filed on or before April 30, 2001, provided the filing was approvable when filed and, for filings after January 14, 1998, the person was physically present in the United States on December 21, 2000. The old petition does not have to have succeeded; it has to have been properly filed and not frivolous.

Families often do not know that such a filing exists. A petition by a sibling in the 1990s, a labor certification by a former employer, or a petition filed for your parent's spouse that listed your parent as a derivative can all qualify. We ask every parent in this situation about old filings, and we request records from USCIS when memory is uncertain.

## Parole and other lawful entries

An admission or parole at any point can satisfy the adjustment requirement, even if the first entry was without inspection. A parent who later left and returned with a visa, or who was paroled into the United States, may be eligible to adjust on the basis of that later entry, subject to the bars that a prior departure may have triggered. Parole in place, a form of parole granted inside the United States, has long been available to parents, spouses and children of U.S. military members and veterans; if you or a sibling serve or served, ask about it. Other parole programs have come and gone, and we check what exists at the time of consultation rather than rely on what existed last year.

## Problems that close the door entirely

Two facts can take a parent out of every option above. The permanent bar under section 212(a)(9)(C) applies to a person who re-entered or tried to re-enter without inspection after accruing more than a year of unlawful presence, or after a removal order. It cannot be waived with an I-601A; the person must spend ten years outside the United States before even asking for permission to return. A parent with more than one unlawful entry needs this analysis before anything is filed.

A prior removal order, including an old order the parent may not know about, requires its own motion to reopen or a Form I-212 request for permission to reapply. A parent in that position who leaves for a consular interview without addressing the order faces a separate bar. If your parent has ever been apprehended, appeared in immigration court or signed a document at the border, our [removal defense](/practice-area/key-services/removal-defense) team reviews the record first.

## What to do now

Gather every document that touches your parent's immigration history: any receipt notice in anyone's name that listed your parent, any court paper, any border document. Identify whether your parent has a spouse or parent with status. Then decide, with counsel, whether to file the I-130 now and wait for a path to open, or to hold the petition. For a parent who lacks both a 245(i) filing and a qualifying relative, the honest answer is sometimes that the petition should be approved and held while the family's circumstances change, for example when your other parent naturalizes.
