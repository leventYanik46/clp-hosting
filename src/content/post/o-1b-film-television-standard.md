An editor, cinematographer or showrunner applying for an O-1B is not judged by the same standard as a painter or a jazz pianist. For work in the motion picture or television industry, 8 CFR 214.2(o)(3)(ii) requires extraordinary achievement: a very high level of accomplishment, shown by skill and recognition significantly above that ordinarily encountered, so that you are recognized as outstanding, notable or leading in the field. The O-1B for film and television also closes two doors that stay open for other artists. Comparable evidence is not allowed, and the petition needs two advisory opinions instead of one. Choosing the right standard is the first decision in the case, and our [O-1B visa for film, television and the arts](/practice-area/key-services/o-1b-visa) page sets out both standards side by side.

## Who falls under the O-1B for film and television

The test follows the industry of the work, not your job title. Actors, directors, producers, writers, cinematographers, editors, production designers, visual effects supervisors and composers engaged on a production all fall under it when the U.S. work is a film or television project. USCIS generally treats productions made for streaming platforms as part of the motion picture and television industry, so a series made for a streaming service is judged the same way as a network series.

The same person can move between standards. A stage actor coming to perform on Broadway is assessed under the arts standard; the same actor coming to shoot a television series is assessed under extraordinary achievement. Mixed work (commercials, music videos, interactive media, a tour plus a concert film) needs a deliberate choice. Where the classification is arguable, the petition should explain which standard applies and why, and the safer course is to build evidence that would satisfy the higher one.

## How extraordinary achievement differs from distinction

The arts standard asks whether you are prominent, renowned, leading or well known, with skill and recognition substantially above the ordinary. The film and television standard asks whether you are outstanding, notable or leading, with recognition significantly above the ordinary. The words look close, but in practice officers expect industry-level recognition: credits on productions with a documented reputation, coverage in the trade press that follows the industry, and recognition from people whose standing in the industry is itself provable. [What distinction in the arts means for an O-1B visa](/o-1b-distinction-in-the-arts-standard) covers the lower standard in detail, which helps if your work could fit either.

## The criteria, and why comparable evidence is not available

You qualify through receipt of or nomination for a significant national or international award (the regulation names the Academy Award, the Emmy and the Directors Guild Award), or by documenting at least three of these six criteria under 8 CFR 214.2(o)(3)(v):

- A lead or starring role in productions or events with a distinguished reputation, shown by reviews, advertisements, publicity releases, publications, contracts or endorsements.
- National or international recognition shown by critical reviews or other published material by or about you in major newspapers, trade journals or magazines.
- A lead, starring or critical role for organizations and establishments with a distinguished reputation.
- A record of major commercial or critically acclaimed successes, shown by title, rating, standing in the field, box office receipts, ratings and similar achievements reported in trade journals or major newspapers.
- Significant recognition from organizations, critics, government agencies or recognized experts, in testimonials that show the writer's authority and knowledge of your work.
- A high salary or other substantial remuneration compared with others in the field.

For the arts, a petitioner may offer comparable evidence when a criterion does not readily apply. That option does not exist for film and television. If you are a colorist or a sound designer who is never reviewed by name, you cannot substitute something else for the press criterion; you pick three criteria that fit and prove those. For crew, the strongest combination is often a critical role for distinguished production companies or studios, a record of commercially or critically successful titles, and pay well above the applicable union scale, supported by testimonials from recognized figures in the industry. The [O-1B evidence checklist for musicians and actors](/o-1b-evidence-checklist-musicians-actors) shows how to document roles, reputation and pay; most of it carries over to screen work.

## Two advisory opinions: the union and management

Every O-1 petition needs a written consultation, but film and television petitions need two. Under 8 CFR 214.2(o)(5), the petitioner must obtain an opinion from the union representing your occupational peers and another from a management organization in your area of ability. For performers that union is usually SAG-AFTRA; directors, writers and many crafts have their own guilds and locals. Each organization sets its own process and fee, so request both letters early, send each one the production details and your role, and keep proof of every request. A missing management opinion is one of the most avoidable RFE triggers in screen cases. [What is the O-1 advisory opinion and who provides it?](/o-1-advisory-opinion-who-provides-it) explains what the letters must say and what to do with a negative opinion.

## Bringing your crew: the O-2 rule for productions

Support staff integral to your work may qualify for O-2 status, and film and television has its own version of the test. The O-2 worker must have skills and experience with you that are not of a general nature and are critical, either because of a pre-existing and longstanding working relationship or because significant production, including pre-production and post-production, will take place both inside and outside the United States and that person's continued participation is essential to finishing the production. O-2 workers need their own petition and consultation and must keep a residence abroad. Your spouse and unmarried children under 21 may hold O-3 status, which allows study but not work.

## Where screen petitions go wrong

Refusals in film and television cases tend to repeat. The petition is filed under the arts standard for screen work. Festival selections are listed without proof of the festival's reputation. Credits rest on database printouts alone, with no contracts, end-credit captures or call sheets showing the actual role. The petition leans on comparable evidence the regulation does not allow, or omits the management consultation. The production schedule is too vague to identify an event. Each of these can be fixed before filing.
