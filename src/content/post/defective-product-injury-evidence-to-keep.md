The most common way a defective product injury claim dies is that the product disappears. The store takes it back for a refund, the manufacturer asks to "inspect" it and never returns it, the fire department hauls it away, or the injured person throws it out in frustration. Without the product, there is usually no way to prove what was wrong with it. Everything else in this post follows from that one rule, which is the first thing CLP's [dangerous and defective product practice](/practice-area/key-services/dangerous-or-defective-products) tells a new client: keep it, and keep it exactly as it is.

## Get treated and tell the provider what happened

See a doctor the same day, even for an injury that seems minor, and name the product when you describe how it happened. The medical record is the first document that ties the product to the injury, and a chart that says "burn, left hand" is far weaker than one that says "burn from space heater that ignited while in use." Keep every discharge summary, imaging report, prescription, and bill. Photograph the injury as it heals, with dates.

## Keep the product exactly as it is

Do not repair it, clean it, disassemble it, test it, or let anyone else do so. Do not return it to the retailer, even for a refund, and do not ship it to the manufacturer when customer service asks. If a product caught fire, keep the remains, including the charger and the outlet it was plugged into, and get a copy of the fire marshal's report. If an implanted medical device was removed in surgery, ask the hospital in writing, before the surgery, to preserve the explanted device rather than discard it, which is routine when requested and rarely done when not.

Photograph the model number, serial number, lot or batch code, and any date code, because those identify the production run and connect your unit to others. Keep the packaging, the manual, every warning label and insert, the receipt or order confirmation, and the registration card if you sent one in. Store all of it in a dry place and do not let it leave your control without a written chain-of-custody record signed by whoever takes it.

## Document the scene and the way you used it

Photograph and video the place where the injury happened before anything is moved. Write down, while it is fresh, how you were using the product, how long you had owned it, whether you followed the instructions, and whether it had ever behaved oddly before. Collect the names of anyone who saw it happen. Preserve your phone: texts about the product, photos from the day you bought it, and the app data if the product was connected. Stay off social media about the injury, because a post that says "my own fault for leaving it on" will be read back to you at a deposition.

## Report it, but to the right agency

Reporting creates an official record and can surface other incidents involving the same product. The right agency depends on what it was:

- Consumer products such as appliances, furniture, toys, e-bikes, and tools: the Consumer Product Safety Commission, through SaferProducts.gov.
- Vehicles, tires, and child car seats: the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration.
- Drugs, medical devices, and most foods: the Food and Drug Administration, through MedWatch.
- Meat, poultry, and egg products: the Department of Agriculture.

A report is not a claim and does not start any legal process, but the agency file may later show a pattern that supports your case. Whether a recall already exists, or follows your report, changes the case in ways we explain in [does a product recall prove the product was defective](/does-product-recall-prove-defect).

## Preservation letters and the chain of custody

Once a claim is likely, a lawyer sends written preservation demands to the manufacturer, the seller, and anyone else in the chain. These letters put them on notice to retain design files, test data, complaint logs, and the versions of the warnings in use when your unit shipped. Destroying evidence after such notice is spoliation, and courts can respond with an adverse inference instruction or dismissal of a defense.

The same duty runs to you. The defendant will eventually demand to inspect the product, and testing that could alter or destroy it is done jointly, with notice and with both sides' experts present. A plaintiff who tested the product alone, or lost it, faces the same sanctions. Which companies receive the letter is a question of its own, covered in [who is liable for a defective product](/who-is-liable-defective-product), and the answer depends on which defect theory the evidence supports, explained in [the three types of product defects](/three-types-of-product-defects).

## Deadlines for a defective product injury claim

A personal injury claim must be filed within two years in Virginia and three years in DC and New York. A breach of warranty claim, which is the main route in Virginia, has a separate four-year limit that runs from the date the product was delivered, not from the injury, so an older product can have a warranty deadline that has already passed. If the injury happened at work, workers' compensation covers the employer, but a product claim against the manufacturer remains available.
