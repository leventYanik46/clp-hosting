If you're not comfortable speaking English at your USCIS interview, you may be able to bring an interpreter. The USCIS interpreter form, Form G-1256, must be signed by both you and the interpreter at the interview. There are rules about who can serve as an interpreter and when.

## When you can bring an interpreter

You can bring an interpreter to many USCIS interviews, such as:

- Adjustment of status (green card) interviews
- Marriage-based interviews
- Asylum interviews, where you generally must bring your own interpreter

For naturalization interviews, you generally must speak English, unless you qualify for an exception.

## Form G-1256

Form G-1256, Declaration for Interpreted USCIS Interview, is signed at the interview. It confirms:

- The interpreter is fluent in English and your language
- The interpreter will interpret accurately and completely
- You consent to the interpreter

The officer provides the form at the interview. Bring the interpreter's identification.

## Who can serve as an interpreter

The interpreter must:

- Be fluent in English and your language
- Be at least 18 years old
- Bring valid identification

The interpreter shouldn't be:

- Your attorney or accredited representative in the same case
- A witness in your case
- Someone with a conflict of interest, such as a petitioner in some cases

USCIS may disqualify an interpreter if there are concerns about accuracy or conflicts.

## Naturalization interviews

For naturalization, you must show English ability, unless you qualify for an exception:

- **50/20:** age 50 or older with 20 years as a permanent resident
- **55/15:** age 55 or older with 15 years as a permanent resident
- **65/20:** age 65 or older with 20 years as a permanent resident
- **Disability:** qualifying disability documented on Form N-648

If you qualify, you can bring an interpreter for the civics test and the interview.

## Asylum interviews

Asylum applicants generally must bring their own interpreter. USCIS also uses a monitor by phone to check accuracy.

## Tips

1. Choose a professional or experienced interpreter
2. Make sure the interpreter knows the interview format
3. Bring the interpreter's ID
4. Ask the interpreter to interpret everything, not summarize
5. Speak in short sentences

## Choosing between a friend and a professional interpreter

Family members and friends can sometimes serve as interpreters, but they may summarize instead of interpreting word for word, or soften answers they think sound bad. Officers notice. A professional interpreter is usually the safer choice for interviews where details matter, such as asylum and marriage-based interviews.

## If the interpreter isn't working

If you think your interpreter is making mistakes, tell the officer. The officer may stop the interview, reschedule it, or arrange for a different interpreter. It's better to raise the issue during the interview than to try to correct the record later.

## Interpreters at biometrics and other appointments

Interpreters aren't usually needed at biometrics appointments, which involve fingerprints and photos. For interviews in immigration court, the court provides its own interpreters, so you don't bring your own.

## Practical example

A woman applying for a marriage-based green card speaks Spanish and limited English. She brings a professional interpreter to her interview with ID. At the start, the officer has both sign Form G-1256 and confirms the interpreter has no conflict. During the interview, the interpreter translates every question and answer word for word. When the officer asks a complex question, she asks for it to be repeated through the interpreter rather than guessing.
