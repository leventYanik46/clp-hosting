An asylum lawyer helps you explain, document and present why you cannot safely return to your home country. Asylum is protection for people who have suffered persecution or fear it on account of race, religion, nationality, political opinion or membership in a particular social group. The cases turn on details, and small gaps in the story or the evidence can matter a great deal.

## The basics of an asylum case

To qualify, you generally must show that you were persecuted or have a well-founded fear of future persecution, that the harm is connected to one of the five protected grounds, and that your government is unable or unwilling to protect you. You must also meet procedural rules, including a filing deadline.

The application is Form I-589. It is also used to request related protections, such as withholding of removal and protection under the Convention Against Torture, which have different standards and are sometimes available when asylum is not.

## The one-year deadline

Asylum applicants must generally file within one year of arriving in the United States. There are exceptions for changed circumstances or extraordinary circumstances, and they have to be proven. Missing the deadline without a valid exception can bar the claim. This is among the first things a lawyer checks, and it is a reason to seek advice early.

## Affirmative and defensive asylum

- **Affirmative asylum.** You file with USCIS, usually when you are not in removal proceedings, and you attend an interview with an asylum officer. If USCIS does not grant the case and you lack other status, the case can be referred to immigration court.
- **Defensive asylum.** You request asylum before an immigration judge as a defense to removal, after the government has started proceedings against you.

Which track applies to you affects the process, the timeline and the strategy.

## What an asylum lawyer does

- **Evaluates the claim.** A lawyer can tell you whether your facts fit the legal grounds, and what the weak points are.
- **Prepares your declaration.** A clear, consistent account of what happened, in your own words, is the core of the case. Details such as dates, places and names matter.
- **Gathers evidence.** This can include medical or psychological evaluations, police or court records, news reports, country condition reports, photographs and letters from witnesses.
- **Uses country conditions.** Reports that show the situation in your home country support your claim.
- **Prepares you for the interview or hearing.** You will be questioned closely, and preparation helps you testify clearly and truthfully.
- **Represents you in court.** The lawyer speaks for you, cross-examines government witnesses where appropriate and makes legal arguments.
- **Handles related issues,** such as work authorization, derivative family members, appeals and other forms of relief.

## Be careful about who you hire

Beware of people who offer help without being licensed attorneys or accredited representatives, sometimes called notarios. They can harm a case through errors or false promises. Avoid anyone who guarantees an outcome or encourages you to include false statements. A truthful claim is the strongest one, and inaccurate information can lead to a finding of fraud.

## What to bring to a first meeting

Bring any identity documents, immigration papers, notices you have received, and notes about what happened, including dates. If you have evidence like medical records or messages, bring those too. If you are afraid to talk about some parts of your story, say so, since a lawyer can pace the conversation.

If you are considering asylum or have received a notice, speak to counsel promptly.
