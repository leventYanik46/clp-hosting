    <p><strong>The O-1B visa is a temporary work classification for artists and entertainers with distinction in the arts and for people with a record of extraordinary achievement in the motion picture and television industry, and it has no annual cap, no lottery and no degree requirement.</strong> Capitol Law Partners prepares O-1B petitions for artists, performers and film and television professionals and for the companies and agents that sponsor them.</p>

    <h2>What an O-1B attorney does for you</h2>
    <ul>
      <li>Determine whether the arts or the motion picture and television standard applies, and compare the <a href="/practice-area/key-services/p-1-visa">P-1</a> and <a href="/practice-area/key-services/p-3-visa">P-3</a> alternatives.</li>
      <li>Identify the petitioner, prepare the itinerary and contracts for agent petitions, select the criteria you can prove and obtain the advisory opinions.</li>
      <li>Draft the brief and Form I-129, respond to Requests for Evidence, and handle O-2 and O-3 cases.</li>
      <li>Prepare you for the consular interview and plan extensions and the move to an <a href="/practice-area/key-services/eb-1a-green-card">EB-1A green card</a>.</li>
    </ul>

    <h2>Who qualifies: the two O-1B standards</h2>
    <p>The O-1 classification is created by INA 101(a)(15)(O)(i) and governed by 8 CFR 214.2(o). Scientists, executives and athletes use the <a href="/practice-area/key-services/o-1a-visa">O-1A</a>; the O-1B has two standards.</p>
    <h3>Distinction in the arts</h3>
    <p>For the arts, extraordinary ability means distinction: a high level of achievement, with skill and recognition substantially above that ordinarily encountered, so that you are prominent, renowned, leading or well known in the field. The arts include the fine, visual, culinary and performing arts and reach essential persons such as directors and designers.</p>
    <h3>Extraordinary achievement in motion picture and television</h3>
    <p>For the motion picture or television industry, the standard is a very high level of accomplishment, with skill and recognition significantly above that ordinarily encountered, so that you are recognized as outstanding, notable or leading in the industry. USCIS treats streaming productions as part of the industry, and choosing the wrong standard is a common cause of refusal.</p>

    <h2>The O-1B criteria and comparable evidence</h2>
    <p>Under 8 CFR 214.2(o)(3)(iv) for the arts and (o)(3)(v) for motion picture and television, you qualify through nomination for or receipt of a significant national or international award, such as an Academy Award, an Emmy or a Grammy, or by documenting at least three of the following:</p>
    <ol>
      <li>A lead or starring role in productions or events with a distinguished reputation.</li>
      <li>National or international recognition shown by published material by or about you in major newspapers, trade journals or magazines.</li>
      <li>A lead, starring or critical role for organizations and establishments with a distinguished reputation.</li>
      <li>A record of major commercial or critically acclaimed successes, shown by box office receipts, ratings or standing in the field.</li>
      <li>Significant recognition from organizations, critics, government agencies or other recognized experts, in testimonials that establish the author's authority.</li>
      <li>A high salary or other substantial remuneration relative to others in the field, shown by contracts or other reliable evidence.</li>
    </ol>
    <p>Comparable evidence is allowed for the arts where these criteria do not readily apply, but not for motion picture and television cases.</p>

    <h2>Petitioner, agents and the advisory opinion</h2>
    <p>You cannot self-petition; a U.S. employer, a U.S. agent or a foreign employer acting through a U.S. agent must file. An agent may be your actual employer, a representative of you and several employers, or a person authorized by an employer to act in its place; an agent filing for multiple engagements must include an itinerary with dates, locations and employers and the contracts or summaries of terms.</p>
    <p>Under 8 CFR 214.2(o)(5), every O-1B petition needs a written advisory opinion: for the arts, from a peer group or a labor organization such as Actors' Equity, SAG-AFTRA or the American Federation of Musicians; for motion picture and television, from both the appropriate union and a management organization. If no appropriate peer group exists, USCIS decides on the record, and a waiver is available for an artist returning within two years of a previous consultation.</p>

    <h2>Validity, support staff and family</h2>
    <p>An initial O-1B petition may be approved for the time needed to complete the event or activity, up to three years, with one-year extensions to continue the same work and no fixed maximum stay. No residence abroad is required.</p>
    <p>O-2 status covers support personnel who are integral to your performance and have critical skills not of a general nature that a U.S. worker cannot readily perform. O-2 workers need their own petition and consultation and a residence abroad. Your spouse and unmarried children under 21 may hold O-3 status, which allows study but not work.</p>

    <h2>Process and timeline</h2>
    <ol>
      <li><strong>Structure, advisory opinions and filing.</strong> We decide which standard applies, identify the petitioner, prepare the agent agreement and itinerary, request the consultations and prepare the brief, Form I-129 and the exhibits. The petition may be filed up to one year ahead, and premium processing commits USCIS to act within 15 business days.</li>
      <li><strong>Visa or change of status.</strong> After approval you apply for the visa at a U.S. consulate, or, if you are in the United States in lawful status, the petition can request a change of status.</li>
      <li><strong>Extensions and amendments.</strong> We calendar expirations, file extensions and amend the petition when engagements change.</li>
    </ol>

    <h2>Evidence and documents</h2>
    <ul>
      <li>Reviews and features in major newspapers, trade journals and magazines.</li>
      <li>Playbills, credits, contracts and publicity showing lead roles and the reputation of each production.</li>
      <li>Award nominations and box office, chart, ratings and sales data.</li>
      <li>Contracts and pay records, the agent agreement, the itinerary and the advisory opinions.</li>
    </ul>

    <h2>Common reasons O-1B petitions are refused</h2>
    <p>Refusals usually trace to a few issues: applying the arts standard to film or television work; promotional or self-generated press; lead roles in productions whose reputation is never documented; testimonials that do not establish the writer's expertise; an agent petition with no itinerary or contracts; and engagements too vague for USCIS to identify an event. Consular refusals follow when the applicant cannot explain the engagements. We take over RFEs and denials on petitions prepared elsewhere.</p>

    <h2>Immigrant intent and the path to a green card</h2>
    <p>Under 8 CFR 214.2(o)(13), an immigrant petition or approved labor certification is not a basis for denying an O-1 petition, extension or admission. Many artists build their record on an O-1B and then move to an <a href="/practice-area/key-services/eb-1a-green-card">EB-1A green card</a>, which covers the arts, or to an <a href="/practice-area/key-services/eb-2-niw-green-card">EB-2 National Interest Waiver</a>, and we plan that transition from the first filing.</p>

  - question: What is the difference between the O-1A and the O-1B visa?
    answer: >-
      Both are O-1 classifications for people with extraordinary ability. The
      O-1A covers the sciences, education, business and athletics and requires
      sustained national or international acclaim. The O-1B covers the arts,
      where the standard is distinction, and the motion picture and television
      industry, where the standard is extraordinary achievement. The criteria,
      the advisory opinion requirements and the kinds of evidence differ between
      the two.
  - question: Can I petition for an O-1B visa myself?
    answer: >-
      No. The petition must be filed by a U.S. employer, a U.S. agent, or a
      foreign employer acting through a U.S. agent. Artists who work for many
      venues or clients usually use a U.S. agent who files one petition with an
      itinerary and the contracts for each engagement. We help structure the
      agent relationship and, where appropriate, set up an entity that can act
      as petitioner.
  - question: What is the advisory opinion and who provides it for artists?
    answer: >-
      The advisory opinion, or consultation, is a written statement from an
      appropriate peer group or labor organization about your achievements and
      the proposed work. For the arts it may come from a union such as Actors'
      Equity, SAG-AFTRA or the American Federation of Musicians, or from a peer
      group with expertise in your field. For motion picture and television
      work, USCIS requires opinions from both the relevant union and a
      management organization.
  - question: How long is an O-1B visa valid and can it be extended?
    answer: >-
      An initial O-1B petition can be approved for the time needed to complete
      the event or activity, up to three years. Extensions are granted in
      increments of up to one year to continue or complete the same work, and
      there is no fixed maximum stay. A new petition is required for new
      engagements or a new petitioner. You may enter up to ten days before the
      validity period and remain ten days after it.
  - question: Do streaming and web productions count as motion picture and television?
    answer: >-
      Yes. USCIS treats productions made for streaming platforms as part of the
      motion picture and television industry, so actors, directors, editors and
      crew working on such content are judged under the extraordinary
      achievement standard rather than the distinction standard for the arts.
      Comparable evidence is not available in motion picture and television
      cases, so the evidence must fit the listed criteria.
  - question: Can my band members, crew or assistants come with me?
    answer: >-
      Essential support personnel may qualify for O-2 status if they are an
      integral part of your performance and have critical skills and experience
      that are not of a general nature and cannot readily be performed by a U.S.
      worker. O-2 workers need their own petition and consultation and must
      maintain a residence abroad. Your spouse and unmarried children under 21
      may receive O-3 status, which allows study but not employment.
  - question: How quickly can an O-1B petition be approved?
    answer: >-
      With premium processing, USCIS commits to act on the Form I-129 within 15
      business days by approving, denying or issuing a Request for Evidence.
      Regular processing times vary and are published by USCIS. The petition may
      be filed up to one year before the start date, and if you are abroad you
      should allow additional time for the consular interview and visa issuance.
  - question: Can I apply for a green card while on an O-1B visa?
    answer: >-
      Yes. Under 8 CFR 214.2(o)(13), filing an immigrant petition or obtaining
      an approved labor certification is not a basis for denying an O-1
      petition, extension or admission. Many artists and entertainers move from
      the O-1B to an EB-1A green card, which covers the arts, or to an EB-2
      National Interest Waiver. We plan that transition from the start so the
      evidence serves both cases.
