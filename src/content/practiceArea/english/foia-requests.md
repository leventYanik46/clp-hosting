    <p><strong>Before you file a new application, defend a removal case or apply for citizenship, you need to know exactly what the government already has on you, and a Freedom of Information Act request is how you find out.</strong> Capitol Law Partners prepares and tracks FOIA requests to USCIS, EOIR, CBP, ICE, the Department of State and the FBI, reads the results with a lawyer's eye, and sues in federal court when an agency does not respond.</p>

    <h2>Why the FOIA request often comes first</h2>
    <p>Clients frequently do not know their own immigration history. A hearing notice may have gone to an old address and an in absentia removal order entered without the person's knowledge. A border encounter years ago may have produced a sworn statement or an expedited removal order. USCIS may hold a prior application with answers that contradict what you plan to say now. Filing without that knowledge invites a denial, a fraud finding or a referral to immigration court.</p>

    <h2>Which agency holds which records</h2>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>USCIS</strong> holds the Alien File, or A-file: every application and petition filed with legacy INS and USCIS, the decisions, interview notes, sworn statements and records from other DHS components. Requests are filed online through the USCIS FOIA system or on Form G-639.</li>
      <li><strong>EOIR</strong>, the Executive Office for Immigration Review, holds the Record of Proceedings for every immigration court case: the Notice to Appear, exhibits, hearing recordings, the judge's decision and any Board of Immigration Appeals filings. Case status and hearing dates are also available through EOIR's automated case information system.</li>
      <li><strong>CBP</strong>, Customs and Border Protection, holds entry and exit records, I-94 history, inspection records including secondary inspection notes, and expedited removal and withdrawal documents. Recent I-94 history is available online; older records require a FOIA request.</li>
      <li><strong>ICE</strong>, Immigration and Customs Enforcement, holds detention records, the Record of Deportable or Inadmissible Alien on Form I-213, warrants, detainers and enforcement history.</li>
      <li><strong>The Department of State</strong> holds visa records, which are confidential under INA 222(f). It generally releases only the documents you submitted yourself and withholds consular officers' notes and the reasons for a refusal.</li>
      <li><strong>The FBI</strong> provides the Identity History Summary, a fingerprint-based record of arrests and dispositions reported to the FBI, requested directly from the FBI with fingerprints.</li>
    </ul>

    <h2>Processing tracks and expedited treatment</h2>
    <p>USCIS sorts requests into three tracks: simple requests, complex requests such as a full A-file, and an accelerated track for people with a hearing scheduled before an immigration judge, which requires proof of the pending proceedings such as the Notice to Appear or a hearing notice. DHS regulations also permit expedited processing where delay would threaten life or safety or cause the loss of substantial due process rights. The statute gives agencies 20 working days to respond, extendable by 10 in unusual circumstances, but complex requests routinely take months.</p>

    <h2>The Privacy Act, consent and third-party records</h2>
    <p>You are entitled to your own records, but an agency will not release records about another person without that person's written consent, signed under penalty of perjury or notarized, or proof that the person is deceased. This matters when a case depends on a parent's or spouse's file. We prepare the consent and the Form G-28 so that records are released to the firm directly.</p>

    <h2>When the agency does not respond: FOIA litigation</h2>
    <p>If an agency misses the statutory deadline, you are deemed to have exhausted your administrative remedies and may sue in U.S. district court under 5 U.S.C. 552(a)(4)(B). The court can order production, review withheld material and award attorney fees when the requester substantially prevails. FOIA litigation and <a href="/practice-area/key-services/immigration-mandamus">mandamus</a> are related tools: FOIA compels the records, mandamus compels a decision on a delayed application, and the records obtained through FOIA often show whether a mandamus suit is wise. Where a client is detained, the file is also the foundation of a <a href="/practice-area/key-services/habeas-corpus">habeas corpus</a> petition.</p>

    <h2>Reading and using the results</h2>
    <p>The release is a large electronic file, often out of order and heavily redacted. We index it, build a timeline of entries, filings and decisions, and compare it against your account. The results then go to work:</p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Naturalization.</strong> Confirming the dates in the green card case, prior statements under oath and any good moral character issue before an N-400 is filed. See <a href="/practice-area/key-services/citizenship-and-naturalization">citizenship and naturalization</a>.</li>
      <li><strong>Waivers.</strong> Identifying the exact ground of inadmissibility, prior removal orders, and fraud or misrepresentation findings that a waiver must address.</li>
      <li><strong>Asylum.</strong> Locating border sworn statements and credible fear interview notes so that the declaration is consistent with them.</li>
      <li><strong>Removal defense.</strong> Testing the allegations in the Notice to Appear, proving how hearing notices were served, and finding the prior orders that a <a href="/practice-area/key-services/bia-appeals-and-motions">motion to reopen</a> must address. Our <a href="/practice-area/key-services/removal-defense">removal defense</a> team relies on the file in every case.</li>
    </ul>

    <h2>Redactions and administrative appeals</h2>
    <p>Agencies withhold material under the FOIA exemptions, most often officer notes and internal deliberations, third parties' personal information and law enforcement techniques. Some redactions are improper, and a no records response is sometimes wrong, often because the A-number was mistyped or the request went to the wrong component. You may file an administrative appeal, generally within 90 days of the response, and the agency's letter states the exact deadline.</p>

faq:
  - question: What is an immigration FOIA request?
    answer: >-
      A request under the Freedom of Information Act for the records a federal
      agency holds about you. In immigration cases that usually means your
      USCIS A-file, the immigration court record from EOIR, entry and exit
      records from CBP, detention and enforcement records from ICE, and visa
      records from the Department of State. Several agencies accept requests
      online, and the results are delivered electronically.
  - question: How long does a USCIS FOIA request take?
    answer: >-
      The statute requires a response within 20 working days, extendable by 10,
      but USCIS complex requests for a full A-file commonly take months.
      Requests from people with a scheduled immigration court hearing are
      placed in an accelerated track and move faster, provided you include
      proof of the hearing. If the agency ignores the deadline, you are
      considered to have exhausted administrative remedies and can sue in
      federal court.
  - question: Can I request another person's immigration records?
    answer: >-
      Only with that person's written consent, signed under penalty of perjury
      or notarized, or with proof that the person is deceased. This comes up
      often when a child's citizenship claim depends on a parent's
      naturalization file, or when a waiver depends on a spouse's record. We
      prepare the consent documents and request the records so they are sent
      directly to the firm.
  - question: Will a FOIA request alert immigration authorities to my presence?
    answer: >-
      A FOIA request is a routine records request handled by a separate FOIA
      office, and it is not an application for a benefit or an admission about
      your status. Every request does include identifying information, so we
      discuss your situation before filing, including whether a prior order of
      removal may exist. In most cases, knowing what is in the file is far
      safer than guessing.
  - question: What does an A-file contain?
    answer: >-
      The Alien File is the master file USCIS keeps on a person. It typically
      includes every application and petition filed with legacy INS or USCIS,
      the notices and decisions, interview notes, sworn statements, biometrics
      records, copies of passports and visas, Notices to Appear, and documents
      received from CBP, ICE and consulates. It is often the single most useful
      set of documents in an immigration case.
  - question: Can I get the notes a consular officer made when my visa was refused?
    answer: >-
      Usually not. Visa records are confidential under INA 222(f), and the
      Department of State generally releases only the documents you submitted,
      such as your DS-160 or DS-260, while withholding the officer's notes and
      internal reasons. A FOIA request can still confirm what was submitted and
      when, which helps in a waiver or a later application.
  - question: What can I do about a no records response or heavy redactions?
    answer: >-
      File an administrative appeal within the deadline stated in the agency's
      letter, generally 90 days. A no records response is often the result of a
      mistyped A-number, a different name spelling, or a request sent to the
      wrong agency, and we correct and resubmit. If redactions appear improper,
      the appeal challenges the exemption claimed, and the dispute can be taken
      to federal district court if necessary.
  - question: Is FOIA litigation the same as a mandamus lawsuit?
    answer: >-
      No. A FOIA lawsuit compels an agency to release records it has unlawfully
      withheld or failed to produce. A mandamus action compels an agency to
      decide a delayed application. They often work together: we obtain the
      file through FOIA to assess the strength of the underlying case, then
      file mandamus if the delay is unreasonable and the case is sound.
