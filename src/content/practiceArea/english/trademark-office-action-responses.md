    <p><strong>An office action is not a denial. It is the USPTO examining attorney's written statement of what stands between your application and registration, and most office actions can be answered.</strong> Capitol Law Partners responds to office actions for applicants who filed with us, on their own or through a filing service, and for foreign-domiciled applicants who need U.S. counsel.</p>

    <h2>What a trademark office action attorney does for you</h2>
    <ul>
      <li>Explain what is refused, what is merely required and when the response is due.</li>
      <li>Draft the response and assemble the evidence: third-party registrations, marketplace evidence, declarations.</li>
      <li>Satisfy procedural requirements such as disclaimers and identification amendments in the same response.</li>
      <li>Handle reconsideration, appeals and petitions to revive.</li>
    </ul>

    <h2>Nonfinal and final office actions, and the deadline</h2>
    <p>The first office action is usually nonfinal: it raises every issue the examiner has found and invites a response. If an issue remains, the examiner may issue a final office action, after which the choices narrow to reconsideration, appeal or abandonment. For most applications a response is due within three months of the issue date, and one three-month extension can be requested for a fee before that deadline passes, a rule in place since late 2022. Madrid Protocol applications keep a six-month deadline with no extension. We calendar every deadline and confirm the current rules for each response.</p>

    <h2>Substantive refusals and how we answer them</h2>
    <h3>Section 2(d): likelihood of confusion</h3>
    <p>The examiner cites an earlier mark and concludes that confusion is likely. The response works through the <em>du Pont</em> factors with evidence:</p>
    <ul>
      <li>Differences in the marks' appearance, sound, meaning and commercial impression.</li>
      <li>Differences in the goods and services, trade channels and purchasers, shown with marketplace evidence.</li>
      <li>Weakness of the cited mark, shown through third-party registrations and uses of similar terms.</li>
      <li>Narrowing the identification to remove the overlap.</li>
      <li>A consent agreement with the cited owner explaining why confusion is unlikely.</li>
      <li>Where the cited registration appears unused, suspension while a cancellation, expungement or reexamination proceeding runs.</li>
    </ul>
    <h3>Section 2(e)(1): merely descriptive</h3>
    <p>The examiner concludes that the mark immediately describes a feature, quality, function or purpose of the goods. Responses include arguing that the mark is suggestive because reaching the meaning takes imagination; claiming acquired distinctiveness under Section 2(f), through a verified statement of five years of substantially exclusive and continuous use, which may not suffice for a highly descriptive term, or through evidence of sales, advertising and consumer recognition; or amending to the Supplemental Register, which requires that the mark be in use.</p>
    <h3>Surname and geographic refusals</h3>
    <p>A surname refusal can be met by showing that the surname is rare, has another meaning or does not look like a surname, or through Section 2(f) or the Supplemental Register. A primarily geographically descriptive refusal can be met by showing that the place is obscure or not associated with the goods, or through acquired distinctiveness. A geographically deceptively misdescriptive refusal cannot be cured by acquired distinctiveness.</p>
    <h3>Specimen refusals</h3>
    <p>The specimen does not show the mark in use for the listed goods or services, or looks like a mock-up. The response substitutes a specimen that was actually in use by the relevant date, with a supporting declaration: labels, packaging, product photographs or a webpage with ordering information for goods; advertising or a website for services. Webpage specimens must show the URL and capture date.</p>
    <h3>Ornamental refusals</h3>
    <p>A slogan printed across the front of a shirt is often treated as decoration rather than a source identifier. Answers include a specimen showing the mark on a tag or label, proof that the mark already serves as a trademark for other goods, Section 2(f) or the Supplemental Register.</p>

    <h2>Procedural requirements</h2>
    <p>Office actions also list requirements, and a response that ignores them is incomplete: a disclaimer of descriptive or generic wording; amendments to clarify an indefinite identification or correct the classification, since an identification can be narrowed but never broadened; clarification of the applicant's entity type and state or country of organization; a domicile address; a translation of non-English words, common for Turkish-language marks; and consent from any living individual named in the mark.</p>

    <h2>After a final action: reconsideration and appeal</h2>
    <p>A final office action can be answered with a request for reconsideration, presenting new evidence or amendments to the same examiner, and with a notice of appeal to the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB). The two can be filed together so the appeal preserves the deadline. In an ex parte appeal, briefs are exchanged and a panel of administrative trademark judges decides. The Board generally will not consider evidence that was not in the file before the appeal. A TTAB decision can be reviewed by the Federal Circuit or a district court. See <a href="/practice-area/key-services/trademark-enforcement-and-ttab-proceedings">trademark enforcement and TTAB proceedings</a>.</p>

    <h2>Abandonment and petitions to revive</h2>
    <p>If no response is filed, the USPTO issues a notice of abandonment. A petition to revive may be filed within two months of that notice if the delay was unintentional, together with the missing response. If the notice was never received, a petition may still be possible within two months of learning of the abandonment, subject to an outer limit measured from the recorded abandonment date. Otherwise the only path is a new application.</p>

    <h2>Taking over an application filed elsewhere</h2>
    <p>Many clients come to us after filing on their own and receiving an unexpected refusal. We file an appointment of attorney, review the complete file history, assess whether the application is worth saving or a fresh filing is wiser, and respond. Applicants domiciled outside the United States, including in Türkiye, must be represented by a U.S.-licensed attorney, and we step in for them.</p>

    <h2>Evidence we need from you</h2>
    <ul>
      <li>The office action and serial number, and how the mark is used: photographs, packaging and web pages.</li>
      <li>When use began and how continuous it has been, plus marketing, revenue and press where distinctiveness is at issue.</li>
      <li>What you know about the owner of a cited mark and how its goods are sold.</li>
    </ul>

    <h2>Common mistakes we help you avoid</h2>
    <ul>
      <li>Arguing a Section 2(d) refusal with opinion instead of evidence.</li>
      <li>Submitting a substitute specimen created after the fact, or broadening the identification by accident.</li>
      <li>Answering the refusal but not the requirements, or missing the deadline because the notice went to an old email address.</li>
    </ul>

faq:
  - question: Does an office action mean my trademark application was denied?
    answer: >-
      No. An office action is the examining attorney's explanation of the
      refusals and requirements that currently prevent registration, together
      with an invitation to respond. Many office actions raise only procedural
      points such as a disclaimer or an identification amendment. Even
      substantive refusals, including likelihood of confusion and
      descriptiveness, are often overcome with argument, evidence or amendment.
      The application is denied only if you fail to respond or the refusal is
      made final and upheld on appeal.
  - question: How long do I have to respond to a USPTO office action?
    answer: >-
      For most applications, three months from the issue date, with one
      three-month extension available for a fee if requested before the first
      deadline passes. This has been the rule since late 2022. Applications
      extended to the United States through the Madrid Protocol have six months
      and no extension. Because the USPTO has changed these rules in recent
      years, we confirm the deadline on each office action and calendar it the
      day we receive the file.
  - question: What is a likelihood of confusion refusal and can it be overcome?
    answer: >-
      The examining attorney has found an earlier registration or application
      that is similar enough, for related enough goods or services, that
      consumers might be confused. Responses argue the du Pont factors with
      evidence: differences in the marks, differences in the goods and trade
      channels, the weakness of the cited mark in a crowded field, or a consent
      agreement with the cited owner. Narrowing your identification can also
      resolve the overlap. Many of these refusals are withdrawn after a
      well-supported response.
  - question: What does merely descriptive mean?
    answer: >-
      A mark is merely descriptive under Section 2(e)(1) when it immediately
      conveys a quality, feature, function or purpose of the goods or services
      without any imagination required. Responses include arguing that the mark
      is suggestive instead, claiming acquired distinctiveness under Section 2(f)
      based on long use or evidence of consumer recognition, or amending the
      application to the Supplemental Register if the mark is in use. The best
      path depends on how descriptive the term is and how long you have used it.
  - question: What is a final office action?
    answer: >-
      A final office action issues when the examining attorney maintains a
      refusal or requirement after reviewing your first response. It closes
      ordinary prosecution. Within the response period you may file a request
      for reconsideration with new evidence or amendments, a notice of appeal to
      the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board, or both. If nothing is filed, the
      application is abandoned. The record should be complete before appeal,
      because the Board generally does not accept new evidence.
  - question: My application was abandoned. Can it be revived?
    answer: >-
      Often, if you act quickly. A petition to revive can be filed within two
      months of the notice of abandonment if the failure to respond was
      unintentional, and the petition must include the missing response. If you
      never received the notice, a petition may still be possible within two
      months of learning of the abandonment, subject to an outer time limit.
      After those windows close, the only option is a new application with a
      later filing date.
  - question: I filed through an online service. Can you take over the response?
    answer: >-
      Yes. We file an appointment of attorney with the USPTO, obtain the full
      file history and review the application as if it were our own, including
      the owner named, the identification and the specimen. Then we tell you
      whether the office action can be answered or whether a new application
      would serve you better. Applicants located outside the United States must
      have a U.S.-licensed attorney to continue, and we regularly step into that
      role for Turkish companies.
  - question: What is the Supplemental Register and when does it make sense?
    answer: >-
      The Supplemental Register is for marks that are capable of distinguishing
      goods but are not yet distinctive, such as descriptive terms and surnames.
      A Supplemental registration allows the ® symbol and is cited against later
      confusingly similar applications, but it does not carry the presumption of
      validity that Principal Register registrations enjoy. It makes sense when
      a descriptive refusal cannot be overcome now and you want protection on
      the record while distinctiveness builds through use.
