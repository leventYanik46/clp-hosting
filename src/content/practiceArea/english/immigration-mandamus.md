    <p><strong>When USCIS, the State Department or a consulate has held your case for far longer than it should, a writ of mandamus asks a federal judge to order a decision.</strong> Capitol Law Partners files immigration mandamus lawsuits for applicants stuck in green card, work permit, naturalization, asylum and visa backlogs. We evaluate whether your delay is unreasonable, send a demand to the agency, and litigate in U.S. district court when the agency does not act.</p>

    <h2>What an immigration mandamus lawsuit is</h2>
    <p>A mandamus action is a civil lawsuit against the government agency responsible for your case. It relies on two federal statutes: the Mandamus Act, 28 U.S.C. § 1361, which lets a court compel a federal officer to perform a duty owed to you, and the Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. §§ 555(b) and 706(1), which requires agencies to decide matters within a reasonable time and lets a court compel action that has been unreasonably delayed.</p>
    <p>The lawsuit does not ask the court to approve your application. It asks the court to order the agency to make a decision. In practice, most mandamus cases end with the agency adjudicating the case on its own after the complaint is served, without the judge ever ruling.</p>

    <h2>Cases where mandamus can help</h2>
    <ul>
      <li>Form I-485 adjustment of status pending beyond posted processing times, including cases stuck in background or security checks.</li>
      <li>Form I-130 family petitions and Form I-140 employment petitions, including <a href="/practice-area/key-services/eb-2-niw-green-card">EB-2 NIW</a> and EB-1 cases.</li>
      <li>Form N-400 naturalization applications, including cases with no decision within 120 days after the interview, where a separate remedy under 8 U.S.C. § 1447(b) may also apply.</li>
      <li>Form I-589 <a href="/practice-area/key-services/affirmative-asylum-applications">asylum applications</a> waiting years for an interview or a decision.</li>
      <li>Form I-765 work permits and Form I-131 travel documents.</li>
      <li>Immigrant and nonimmigrant visa applications held in "administrative processing" under INA § 221(g) at a U.S. consulate.</li>
      <li>Form I-730 refugee and asylee relative petitions, Form I-601 and I-601A waivers, and U and T visa cases.</li>
    </ul>

    <h2>How long is too long?</h2>
    <p>There is no fixed number. Courts apply the six factors from <em>Telecommunications Research &amp; Action Center v. FCC</em>, known as the TRAC factors: whether there is a rule of reason for the agency's timing, whether Congress set a timetable, whether human health and welfare are at stake, the effect of expediting on other agency priorities, the nature of the interests harmed by delay, and whether the agency acted improperly. As a rule of thumb, a case that has waited well beyond the processing time USCIS or the State Department publishes for that form and office is a candidate. Delays of a year or more are common in the cases we file, but shorter delays can qualify where the harm is serious, such as a lapsed work permit, a family separation or a medical need.</p>

    <h2>The mandamus process, step by step</h2>
    <ol>
      <li><strong>Case review.</strong> We confirm the filing dates, receipts, inquiries already made, and any reason the agency has given. We also assess the strength of the underlying application, because a mandamus will force a decision in either direction.</li>
      <li><strong>Pre-suit demand.</strong> Where appropriate, we send a written demand to the agency and, for USCIS cases, use the CIS Ombudsman and congressional inquiry channels. Some cases resolve here.</li>
      <li><strong>Complaint.</strong> We draft and file the complaint in the U.S. District Court with proper venue, naming the responsible officials, such as the USCIS Director, the Secretary of Homeland Security, or the Secretary of State and the consular post.</li>
      <li><strong>Service and the government's response.</strong> The U.S. Attorney's Office has 60 days to respond. In many cases the agency adjudicates the application during this window and the case is dismissed as moot.</li>
      <li><strong>Litigation, if needed.</strong> If the government moves to dismiss or argues the delay is reasonable, we brief the TRAC factors and ask the court for an order setting a deadline for a decision.</li>
      <li><strong>Attorney fees.</strong> Where the government's position was not substantially justified, we seek fees under the Equal Access to Justice Act.</li>
    </ol>

    <h2>Risks and realistic expectations</h2>
    <p>Mandamus compels a decision, not an approval. If the application has a real problem, forcing a decision can produce a denial faster than waiting would have. That is why we review the merits before filing and tell you plainly if a lawsuit is the wrong tool. Consular cases carry an additional issue: courts generally will not review a consular officer's decision, but many courts will order a decision where the consulate has simply not acted. We explain how the courts in your district have treated these cases before you commit.</p>

faq:
  - question: What is a writ of mandamus in immigration?
    answer: >-
      It is a federal court order compelling a government agency to perform a
      duty it owes you, in this context to decide an immigration application
      that has been pending for an unreasonable time. The lawsuit is filed in
      U.S. district court under the Mandamus Act and the Administrative Procedure
      Act.
  - question: How long must my case be delayed before I can file?
    answer: >-
      There is no statutory minimum. Courts weigh the TRAC factors, including the
      agency's own published processing times and the harm the delay causes you.
      Cases well past the posted processing time, often a year or more, are the
      strongest candidates, but shorter delays can qualify when the stakes are
      high.
  - question: Will a mandamus lawsuit get my case approved?
    answer: >-
      No. The court can order the agency to decide, not to approve. If your
      underlying application is strong, a decision is what you want. If it has
      problems, we address them before filing, or we advise you not to file.
  - question: How long does a mandamus case take?
    answer: >-
      The government has 60 days to respond to the complaint, and many cases are
      decided by the agency within that window. Cases that go to briefing can
      take several months. Either way, it is usually far faster than continuing
      to wait.
  - question: Can I file mandamus for a visa stuck in administrative processing at a consulate?
    answer: >-
      Often, yes. Courts generally cannot review a consular officer's decision,
      but a visa left in administrative processing under INA 221(g) for many
      months is a failure to decide, and many courts will order the State
      Department to act. Outcomes vary by district, which we review with you
      before filing.
  - question: Will suing USCIS make them deny my case out of retaliation?
    answer: >-
      Agencies are not permitted to retaliate against applicants for exercising
      their right to sue, and in our experience the filing simply moves the case
      to the front of the line. The real risk is that a weak application gets a
      quick denial, which is why we review the merits first.
  - question: How much does a mandamus lawsuit cost?
    answer: >-
      We quote a flat attorney fee after reviewing your file, in addition to the
      court's filing fee. If you prevail and the government's position was not
      substantially justified, the court may award attorney fees under the Equal
      Access to Justice Act.
  - question: Do I need to live near one of your offices?
    answer: >-
      No. Mandamus cases are filed in federal court, and we represent applicants
      across the United States and abroad. Consultations are available by phone
      and video.
