    <h2>A guide to the stages</h2>
    <h3>Keeping status while in the United States</h3>
    <p>Your authorized stay is set by the admit-until date on Form I-94, not by your visa. File any extension or change of status before that date; a timely filing keeps you in a period of authorized stay while USCIS decides. Workers in H-1B, L-1, O-1, E and TN status may keep working for the same employer for up to 240 days after the I-94 expires while a timely extension is pending, and may remain up to 60 days after a job ends. F-1 students have a 60-day grace period after their program or OPT; J-1 and M-1 students have 30 days.</p>
    <h3>Protecting a green card while abroad</h3>
    <p>A green card is not a valid entry document after an absence of a year or more, and an absence over 180 days lets CBP question whether you abandoned your residence. For a trip of a year or longer, obtain a re-entry permit before leaving; you must file and complete biometrics in the United States. A permit guards against abandonment but not continuous residence for naturalization; residents sent abroad by the U.S. government or qualifying American employers can file Form N-470 before departure to preserve it.</p>
    <h3>Becoming a citizen</h3>
    <p>Most permanent residents may file Form N-400 after five years, or three years if married to and living with a U.S. citizen throughout, and up to 90 days before that anniversary. You must show continuous residence, physical presence for at least half the period, good moral character, and English and civics ability, subject to age and disability exceptions. Children often need no application: under the Child Citizenship Act, a child with a green card living in the United States in the legal and physical custody of a citizen parent before turning 18 becomes a citizen automatically, documented on Form N-600 or a passport.</p>
    <h3>Students and exchange visitors</h3>
    <p>M-1 students follow a fixed vocational program and cannot change to F-1, while F-1 students may transfer or begin a new program. J-1 exchange visitors whose program was government funded, whose field is on the skills list for their country, or who came for graduate medical training must return home for two years before they can change status in the United States or obtain an H, L or immigrant visa, unless they obtain a waiver such as a no-objection statement or an interested government agency request.</p>

    <h2>Common mistakes</h2>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Staying after a denial.</strong> If an extension or change is denied after the I-94 has expired, unlawful presence begins at the denial; more than 180 days triggers a three-year bar on departure and one year a ten-year bar.</li>
      <li><strong>Traveling without advance parole.</strong> Leaving with a pending I-485 and no advance parole abandons the application unless you hold valid H or L status.</li>
      <li><strong>Long absences before naturalization.</strong> A trip of more than six months is presumed to break continuous residence; a year or more breaks it and restarts the clock.</li>
      <li><strong>Studying on a B-2.</strong> A visitor may not enroll in a course of study until a change to F-1 is approved.</li>
    </ul>

faq:
  - question: When can I apply for U.S. citizenship?
    answer: >-
      Generally after five years as a permanent resident, or three years if you
      have been married to and living with a U.S. citizen for that period and
      your spouse was a citizen throughout. You may file Form N-400 up to 90
      days before the anniversary. You must also meet the continuous residence,
      physical presence, good moral character, and English and civics
      requirements, with exceptions based on age and disability.
  - question: Does a re-entry permit protect my naturalization timeline?
    answer: >-
      No. A re-entry permit protects against a finding that you abandoned your
      permanent residence during a long trip, but it does not preserve
      continuous residence for naturalization. An absence of a year or more
      still breaks continuous residence unless you obtained approval of Form
      N-470 before leaving, which is available only for certain employment
      abroad. Absences between six months and a year create a presumption you
      can rebut.
  - question: Can I travel while my green card application is pending?
    answer: >-
      Only with an approved advance parole document or, in limited cases, valid
      H or L status. Leaving while Form I-485 is pending without advance parole
      abandons the application, and leaving while the I-131 itself is pending
      abandons that request. Even with advance parole, anyone with prior
      unlawful presence, a removal order or a criminal record should have the
      trip reviewed before booking.
  - question: What is the J-1 two-year home residency requirement?
    answer: >-
      Under INA 212(e), certain exchange visitors must return to their home
      country for two years after the program before they can change status in
      the United States or receive an H, L or immigrant visa. It applies when
      the program was funded by the U.S. or home government, when the field is
      on the skills list for the home country, or when the visitor came for
      graduate medical training. Waivers are available on several grounds.
  - question: Can I study on a B-2 visa or while my change to F-1 is pending?
    answer: >-
      No. A B-2 visitor may not enroll in a course of study, and attending
      classes before USCIS approves a change to F-1 violates status and leads to
      denial. You must obtain a Form I-20, pay the SEVIS fee and file Form
      I-539 before your authorized stay ends. We request premium processing
      where available and work with the school to defer the start date if
      needed.
  - question: What happens if my extension is denied after my I-94 expired?
    answer: >-
      You are out of status on the date of the denial and unlawful presence
      starts to accrue from that day. More than 180 days of unlawful presence
      triggers a three-year bar when you depart, and one year or more triggers a
      ten-year bar. USCIS may also issue a Notice to Appear. Options include a
      motion to reopen or reconsider, prompt departure and a consular
      application, or other relief.
  - question: Is my child already a U.S. citizen?
    answer: >-
      Possibly. A child born abroad to a U.S. citizen parent may have acquired
      citizenship at birth if the parent met the physical presence rules in
      force at the time. A child who received a green card and lived in the
      United States in the legal and physical custody of a citizen parent before
      turning 18 generally became a citizen automatically under the Child
      Citizenship Act. We document the claim on Form N-600 or through a
      passport.
  - question: What is the difference between the F-1 and M-1 visas?
    answer: >-
      The F-1 is for academic study at a college, university, high school or
      language program and allows transfers, program changes and practical
      training through CPT and OPT. The M-1 is for vocational and technical
      programs, admits you for the length of the program, allows only limited
      practical training after completion, and does not permit a change to F-1
      status. We help you choose the right category before the school issues
      the I-20.
