    <p><strong>Leaving the United States with the wrong document, or without one, can abandon a pending green card application, trigger a multi-year bar on returning, or cost a permanent resident the status it took years to obtain.</strong> Capitol Law Partners prepares Form I-131 applications for all three travel documents, advises permanent residents on long absences, and plans travel around pending naturalization and removal cases.</p>

    <h2>What a travel document attorney does</h2>
    <ul>
      <li>Confirm which document your status requires and whether travel is advisable at all.</li>
      <li>Prepare Form I-131 with the evidence USCIS expects and time the filing around biometrics and your departure.</li>
      <li>Review unlawful presence, criminal history and prior violations before you leave, because a departure can make an inadmissibility problem permanent.</li>
    </ul>

    <h2>Form I-131 and the three travel documents</h2>
    <p>Form I-131 covers three documents, each tied to a different status; USCIS revised the form in 2025, so we confirm the current edition before filing.</p>
    <h3>Advance parole</h3>
    <p>Advance parole lets a person without a valid visa return after travel. It is used mainly by applicants with a pending Form I-485 and, subject to ongoing litigation, by DACA recipients. TPS beneficiaries request travel authorization on the same form, now issued as Form I-512T. Leaving while an I-485 is pending without advance parole abandons the application unless you hold valid H or L status, and departing while the I-131 itself is pending abandons that request. Under the BIA's decision in <em>Matter of Arrabally and Yerrabelly</em>, a departure under advance parole granted to an adjustment applicant does not trigger the three-year and ten-year unlawful presence bars of INA 212(a)(9)(B). Anyone with past unlawful presence, a removal order or a criminal record should not travel without a legal review. In a true emergency, such as a relative's grave illness or death abroad, USCIS can issue advance parole at a field office; otherwise we file a documented expedite request.</p>
    <h3>Refugee travel document</h3>
    <p>Asylees, refugees and permanent residents who obtained status through asylum travel on a refugee travel document, generally valid for one year and requested before departure, rather than a passport from the country they fled. Returning to the country of persecution, or using that country's passport, can be treated as evidence that your fear was not well-founded and can support termination of asylum. Clients from our <a href="/practice-area/key-services/green-card-through-asylum">green card through asylum</a> practice should keep using it until they naturalize.</p>
    <h3>Re-entry permit</h3>
    <p>A permanent resident who will be abroad for a year or more should obtain a re-entry permit before leaving. You must file while physically present in the United States and complete biometrics before you depart; the permit itself can be delivered abroad. It is generally valid for two years, cannot be extended, and is limited to one year for residents who have spent more than four of the last five years abroad. A permit stops CBP from treating the length of your absence alone as abandonment, but it does not preserve continuous residence for naturalization or protect someone who has clearly moved abroad.</p>

    <h2>Abandonment of permanent residence</h2>
    <p>A green card is not a valid entry document after an absence of one year, and an absence of more than 180 days allows CBP to treat you as an applicant for admission and question your residence. CBP looks at intent: the purpose and length of the trip, whether you kept a home, job and family in the United States, and whether you filed U.S. tax returns as a resident. Officers sometimes ask returning residents to sign Form I-407 giving up their status; you are not required to sign, and only an immigration judge can find abandonment. If your absence has exceeded a year or your permit has expired, the <strong>SB-1 returning resident visa</strong> at a U.S. consulate is the remedy when the stay was for reasons beyond your control and you always intended to return.</p>

    <h2>Preserving residence for naturalization</h2>
    <p>Naturalization requires continuous residence, usually five years, or three years for spouses of U.S. citizens who hold a <a href="/practice-area/key-services/marriage-based-green-card">marriage-based green card</a>, plus physical presence for at least half of that period. An absence of more than six months but less than one year is presumed to break continuous residence unless you prove otherwise. An absence of one year or more breaks it, and the clock restarts. Residents who work abroad for the U.S. government, a U.S. research institution, an American company engaged in foreign trade or certain international or religious organizations can file <strong>Form N-470</strong> before leaving to preserve continuous residence, provided they first spent one uninterrupted year here as a resident. The N-470 does not count time abroad as physical presence except for certain religious workers. Our <a href="/practice-area/key-services/citizenship-and-naturalization">citizenship and naturalization</a> team plans travel for clients approaching eligibility.</p>

    <h2>Pending N-400 and removal proceedings</h2>
    <p>You may travel while Form N-400 is pending, but every trip must be disclosed, long or frequent absences can defeat continuous residence, and you must be in the United States for the interview and oath. If you are in <a href="/practice-area/key-services/removal-defense">removal proceedings</a>, travel is almost always inadvisable: departure executes an outstanding removal order, is treated as withdrawal of a pending appeal, and can leave you unable to return. Relatives abroad awaiting <a href="/practice-area/key-services/family-reunification">family reunification</a> should process through the consulate rather than attempt entry on an expired document.</p>

    <h2>Evidence and documents</h2>
    <ul>
      <li>Proof of the status behind the request, such as your green card, I-485 receipt or asylum grant, with photographs.</li>
      <li>For advance parole, a statement of purpose with supporting documents such as a medical or employer letter.</li>
      <li>For re-entry permits and Form N-470, evidence of U.S. ties and the reason for the stay abroad.</li>
    </ul>

    <h2>Common mistakes</h2>
    <ul>
      <li>Leaving while an I-485 or I-131 is pending because a prior trip went smoothly.</li>
      <li>Traveling to the home country on a refugee travel document.</li>
      <li>Signing Form I-407 at the airport under pressure, or letting a permit expire abroad.</li>
    </ul>

faq:
  - question: Can I travel while my I-485 adjustment of status application is pending?
    answer: >-
      Only with an approved advance parole document or, in limited cases, a valid
      H or L visa. Leaving without it abandons the I-485 and you may not be able
      to return. Many applicants receive a combined work and travel card. Even
      with advance parole, travel can be unsafe for people with prior unlawful
      presence, a removal order or a criminal record, so we review your history
      before you book.
  - question: Does advance parole protect me from the three-year and ten-year bars?
    answer: >-
      For adjustment applicants, generally yes. In Matter of Arrabally and
      Yerrabelly, the Board of Immigration Appeals held that leaving under advance
      parole granted to an adjustment applicant is not a departure that triggers
      the unlawful presence bars of INA 212(a)(9)(B). The decision does not erase
      other inadmissibility grounds, and its application to other travel
      authorizations is fact-specific. We analyze your record before you travel.
  - question: How long can a permanent resident stay outside the United States?
    answer: >-
      A green card is valid as an entry document for absences of less than one
      year, but an absence of more than 180 days lets CBP treat you as an
      applicant for admission and question whether you abandoned your residence.
      Absences of a year or more require a re-entry permit obtained before
      departure or an SB-1 returning resident visa afterward. What matters most is
      evidence that your permanent home remained in the United States.
  - question: Can I apply for a re-entry permit from abroad?
    answer: >-
      No. You must be physically present in the United States when you file Form
      I-131 and you must complete biometrics here before you leave. The permit can
      be mailed to a U.S. address or to a U.S. embassy or consulate for pickup.
      Plan on filing several weeks before departure. A permit cannot be extended;
      when it nears expiration you must return and file a new application.
  - question: Does a re-entry permit protect my eligibility for naturalization?
    answer: >-
      No. A re-entry permit addresses abandonment of permanent residence, not
      continuous residence for naturalization. An absence of one year or more
      still breaks continuous residence unless you obtained approval of Form N-470
      before leaving, which is available only for certain employment abroad.
      Absences between six months and a year create a presumption of a break that
      you can rebut with evidence of ties to the United States.
  - question: What is the SB-1 returning resident visa?
    answer: >-
      The SB-1 is an immigrant visa for permanent residents who remained abroad
      beyond one year, or beyond the validity of a re-entry permit, for reasons
      beyond their control. You apply at a U.S. consulate, show that you left
      intending to return and never abandoned residence, and complete a medical
      exam and interview. If the consulate refuses, the alternative is a new
      immigrant visa petition through a qualifying relative or employer.
  - question: Can an asylee visit their home country?
    answer: >-
      We strongly advise against it. Returning to the country where you claimed
      persecution, or renewing and using that country's passport, can be treated
      as evidence that your fear was not genuine and can lead USCIS to terminate
      asylum or deny your green card or naturalization. Asylees and refugees
      should travel on a refugee travel document, obtained on Form I-131 before
      departure, and should discuss any travel plans with an attorney first.
  - question: Can I travel while my N-400 naturalization application is pending?
    answer: >-
      Yes, as long as you remain a permanent resident in good standing and keep
      your trips short. You must disclose every trip at the interview, and long or
      frequent absences can break the continuous residence you need. You must be
      in the United States for your biometrics, interview and oath ceremony, so
      schedule travel around USCIS notices and keep your address current.
