    <p><strong>A U.S. citizen who is at least 21 can sponsor each parent for a green card as an immediate relative, with no annual cap and no Visa Bulletin wait.</strong> Capitol Law Partners prepares the I-130 for each parent, the adjustment or consular package, the affidavit of support and any waiver, and represents you at the interview and on any Request for Evidence. Our <a href="/practice-area/key-services/family-based-immigration">family-based immigration</a> page covers the wider system.</p>

    <h2>Who can petition for a parent</h2>
    <h3>The U.S. citizen son or daughter</h3>
    <p>Only a U.S. citizen who is at least 21 may file for a parent. Permanent residents cannot sponsor parents, so a green card holder must naturalize first; if you are close to eligibility, we time the N-400 so the I-130 is filed as soon as you take the oath.</p>
    <h3>Proving the relationship</h3>
    <p>For a mother, your birth certificate naming her is usually enough. For a father, add your parents' marriage certificate. Where they were not married, we show either that you were legitimated before you turned 18 or that a bona fide father-child relationship existed before you turned 21, through evidence of support and contact.</p>
    <h3>Stepparents and adoptive parents</h3>
    <p>A stepparent qualifies if the marriage that created the step relationship took place before you turned 18. An adoptive parent qualifies if the adoption was finalized before you turned 16 and the parent had two years of legal custody and two years of residence with you.</p>

    <h2>One petition per parent, and no derivatives</h2>
    <p>Each parent is a separate beneficiary with a separate Form I-130. Immediate relative petitions carry no derivatives, so one parent cannot be added to the other's case and your parents' other children cannot be included. Your siblings and your own children have their own categories; see <a href="/practice-area/key-services/child-green-card">green cards for children</a> and <a href="/practice-area/key-services/sibling-green-card">sibling green cards</a>.</p>

    <h2>Parents outside the United States: consular processing</h2>
    <p>After USCIS approves the I-130, the National Visa Center collects the fees, the DS-260, your parent's civil documents and police certificates and your Form I-864, then schedules the interview at the U.S. embassy or consulate in your parent's country. Your parent completes a medical examination with a panel physician shortly before the interview and, if the visa is issued, pays the USCIS immigrant fee and enters the United States as a permanent resident. Our <a href="/locations/istanbul">Istanbul office</a> prepares parents applying from Türkiye.</p>

    <h2>Parents already in the United States: adjustment of status</h2>
    <h3>After a lawful entry</h3>
    <p>A parent who was inspected and admitted can usually file Form I-485 together with the I-130 and remain in the United States while the case is pending. Because parents of adult citizens are immediate relatives, an expired status, a long overstay or unauthorized work does not bar adjustment. We file Form I-765 for a work permit and Form I-131 for advance parole at the same time; a parent who travels without advance parole abandons the I-485.</p>
    <h3>Entry without inspection</h3>
    <p>A parent who crossed the border without inspection generally cannot adjust status unless a petition or labor certification filed on or before April 30, 2001 protects them under section 245(i). Otherwise the case finishes at a consulate abroad, and leaving after more than 180 days of unlawful presence triggers a bar to returning. The Form I-601A provisional waiver can forgive that unlawful presence before departure, but it requires extreme hardship to a U.S. citizen or permanent resident spouse or parent of the applicant. A U.S. citizen son or daughter does not count, so many parents have no waiver available.</p>
    <h3>The visitor visa caution</h3>
    <p>A parent who enters on a visitor visa with a preformed plan to stay and adjust risks a finding of misrepresentation, which is difficult to waive, and may be refused entry while a petition is pending. Adjustment fits when the decision to stay developed after a lawful arrival. If your parent is abroad and intends to immigrate, consular processing is usually the safer path.</p>

    <h2>The affidavit of support and public charge</h2>
    <p>You sign Form I-864 and must show household income at or above 125 percent of the Federal Poverty Guidelines for a household that counts you, your dependents, anyone else you have sponsored and every parent you are sponsoring now. Where income falls short, certain assets, a household member's income on Form I-864A or a joint sponsor on a separate I-864 can close the gap; the affidavit is an enforceable contract. The officer also weighs the public charge ground, considering your parent's age, health, family status, resources, education and skills.</p>

    <h2>Medical examination and the interview</h2>
    <p>A parent adjusting in the United States completes Form I-693 with a USCIS-designated civil surgeon, including the required vaccinations, and we file it with the I-485. USCIS may interview you and your parent or decide the case on the record; consular cases always include an interview.</p>

    <h2>Process and timeline</h2>
    <ol>
      <li><strong>Eligibility review.</strong> Your citizenship and age, the relationship, each parent's entry history and any inadmissibility issue.</li>
      <li><strong>I-130 filing.</strong> One petition per parent. A parent here after a lawful admission files the I-485, I-765, I-131 and I-693 at the same time.</li>
      <li><strong>USCIS processing or the NVC stage.</strong> Biometrics and, if needed, an interview for adjustment cases; the DS-260, civil documents and I-864 for consular cases.</li>
      <li><strong>Interview and decision.</strong> We attend USCIS interviews, prepare your parent for the consular interview and answer any Request for Evidence.</li>
      <li><strong>Permanent residence.</strong> No priority date wait applies; the timeline depends on current USCIS, NVC and consular processing times.</li>
    </ol>

    <h2>Documents we will need</h2>
    <ul>
      <li>Proof of your U.S. citizenship and, for a stepparent or adoptive parent, the marriage certificate or adoption decree and custody records.</li>
      <li>Your birth certificate and your parent's birth certificate, passport and photographs, with certified English translations.</li>
      <li>Every passport, visa, Form I-94 and prior filing for a parent in the United States, and tax returns, W-2s and proof of current income for you and any joint sponsor.</li>
    </ul>

    <h2>Common problems we help you avoid</h2>
    <ul>
      <li>A father not named on the birth certificate, with no plan for proving the relationship.</li>
      <li>An entry without inspection, a prior removal order or a misrepresentation discovered after the I-485 is filed.</li>
      <li>A case that sits past normal processing times. If USCIS or the consulate stops moving, an <a href="/practice-area/key-services/immigration-mandamus">immigration mandamus</a> lawsuit can compel a decision.</li>
    </ul>

  - question: How long does a green card for a parent take?
    answer: >-

      delays. We give you a realistic estimate at intake based on the published
      times.
